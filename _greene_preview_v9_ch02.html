The Sustainability Diet Part One  ·  II
Definition
Equus and the deep equid record

The genus Equus — the lineage that includes every living horse, donkey, and zebra — is the crown of a much longer equid story. Before Equus, the continent already had a succession of horse-family genera: Dinohippus, its likely direct ancestor; before that, the three-toed Pliohippus; before that, the radiation of Merychippus.

The equid family has been running on this continent for close to twenty million years. Equus is the last four million of that twenty.

When paleontologists say horses evolved in North America, they are not saying one species showed up here, stayed a while, and left. They are saying the entire deep history of the family played out on this continent, and the Spanish ships of the sixteenth century were returning the crown of a tree whose roots had been American the whole time.

Chapter Two

The Columbian Curtain

◆ ◆ ◆

The story most Americans carry about the horse is this: horses came from Spain. The Spanish brought them in the early 1500s, and before that there were no horses on the continent. This is, as far as it goes, true. It is also among the most misleading true things in the country’s ecological imagination, because the sentence after it almost always gets dropped.

The sentence after it is that horses evolved here.

The genus Equus originated in North America roughly four million years ago and diversified on this continent for most of its evolutionary history.1 Horses walked out across the Bering land bridge, populated Eurasia and Africa, and continued to evolve there, eventually producing the animals that would be domesticated on the Eurasian steppe some six thousand years ago. Meanwhile, the horses that had stayed in their continent of origin went on being American horses until the end of the Pleistocene, when all of them disappeared in the same wave of extinctions that took the camels and the mammoths.2

Case
The American horses by name

The late-Pleistocene equid fauna of North America went by several specific names, and the specifics matter. Equus scotti was large, stout-bodied, and widely distributed across the southern and central portions of the continent.

Equus conversidens was smaller and more gracile, centered on what is now Mexico and the American Southwest. Equus lambei, the Yukon horse, persisted in the Alaskan and Yukon refugia; the Thistle Creek genome that confirmed the deep relationship between late-Pleistocene American horses and modern Equus caballus came from this population.

The point of the list is not taxonomic tidiness. American horses were a diversified fauna occupying a range of habitats and body sizes, and the wave of terminal-Pleistocene extinctions took the whole set.

Genetic work in the last two decades has tightened the story. In 2013 a team led by Ludovic Orlando reported a complete nuclear genome recovered from a horse bone frozen in permafrost at Thistle Creek in the Canadian Yukon. The bone is dated to the middle Pleistocene, around seven hundred thousand years old. The genome clustered within the diversity of modern Equus, confirming that the domestic horse and the late-Pleistocene American horse are close members of a single long-running lineage — the same animal, in a deep sense, with its genetic lights never quite going out.3

Earlier mitochondrial work by Jaco Weinstock and colleagues had reached a similar conclusion from Alaskan and Yukon Pleistocene material: North American late-Pleistocene horses fell within, not outside, the modern Equus caballus / Equus ferus group.4 The phylogeny does not particularly care which ships crossed which ocean. A horse on the plains in 1600 is the same kind of animal as a horse on the plains in 15,000 BP. The gap between the two dates is the question.

The gap is also not, by any reasonable ecological measure, a long one. Twelve and a half thousand years sounds like a great deal in the scale of a human life. It is not very much in the scale over which grassland plant communities, ungulate digestive physiology, or soil microbial assemblages evolve. The grasses of the central plains spent tens of millions of years evolving in the presence of large-bodied grazers of the horse’s general architecture. Their defenses, their growth habits, their response to compaction and disturbance are calibrated to those animals. Thirteen thousand years of relative quiet on that front is not enough time for those calibrations to disappear. When the Spanish horse arrives on the American plains, it does not enter an ecosystem naive to its body plan. It enters one that has been waiting.

That gap is what the standard story hides. Told as “the Spanish brought horses,” the sentence puts the entire evolutionary record of the genus outside the frame. Told as “horses returned to their continent of origin with the Spanish, after an absence of roughly thirteen thousand years,” the sentence includes the record, and the ecological meaning of the animals’ arrival changes. They are not an introduction from elsewhere. They are a homecoming that took the scenic route.

This is the conceptual move the chapter is about. Nothing in the fossil record changes whether we call the horse native or non-native. What changes is our choice of when to start counting.

The Baseline Is a Choice

The dominant framework of American conservation treats the year 1492 as the effective ecological baseline for the continent. Animals present here when Columbus made landfall are categorized as native. Animals not present are categorized as non-native, invasive, exotic, or feral, depending on the disposition of the speaker. The vocabulary varies; the baseline does not.

Paul S. Martin, who spent most of his career as a paleoecologist at the University of Arizona, named this framing the Columbian Curtain. The prologue has introduced it. I want here to take the name more seriously than the prologue had room to. A curtain is the right image. It is not a hard wall. It is a piece of fabric hung at a particular point in time, with everything behind it obscured from view.

Martin’s argument was not that the curtain is wrong in the sense that 1492 is a fake date. European contact is a real event, and it does mark one of the largest biotic exchanges in the planet’s recent history. His argument was that the curtain is arbitrary. There is nothing about the ecological state of North America in 1492 that makes it the correct reference point against which to measure restoration. The continent Columbus encountered had been losing large-bodied fauna for twelve and a half thousand years by the time he arrived.5 More than forty species of large mammals were already absent from North America before a single Spanish sail appeared on the horizon.

Calling that assemblage “the native condition” is a bit like calling a library “complete” on the strength of its remaining shelves, after most of its collection has been carted off.

Granted, every historical science has to choose a reference point somewhere. Paleontology works in sliding windows and does not pretend they have natural edges. The choice of 1492 as the baseline for “native” was not a deliberate scientific decision so much as a cultural inheritance. The framing came downstream from the history we were telling ourselves about the continent. The arrival of Europeans was the episode after which record-keeping, in the form that came to count, began. The animals present at that moment became the reference animals, and the ones that had been here earlier dropped out of the frame along with everything else the record did not carry forward. The baseline is a cultural artifact dressed as an ecological one.

Notice what the baseline does once it is fixed. A horse that shows up in the sixteenth century is, under this frame, an introduction. A horse that was here in the fourteenth millennium BP is not an ancestor to which the arriving animal is returning; it is an unrelated animal in an unrelated world. A burro roaming the Colorado Plateau is not occupying an ecological niche left behind by extinct American equids; it is a non-native species degrading a native landscape. The entire vocabulary of invasion biology, as applied to these animals, assumes the 1492 baseline. Change the baseline and you do not change whether the animal is doing what it is doing. You change what you call it.
