# Authorial Revisions Log

**Purpose:** This file tracks where Pendergrass's thinking has changed between the 2012 manuscript and the current book. It exists to prevent the new book from quoting the author against herself, especially when source material is being mined from the 2012 manuscript or earlier essays.

**How to use:** Before drafting any chapter that touches a topic listed here, check this file. If the 2012 manuscript or an earlier essay states a position contrary to the current position, the new book follows the current position. The historical position may be acknowledged where the revision itself is intellectually relevant ("I once argued the opposite, and here is what changed my mind"), but the historical position is never presented as the author's current view.

**Maintained by:** Pendergrass (additions and corrections); Claude (compliance check during drafting).

## Major Revisions

### 1. The Cause of the Late Pleistocene Extinctions

**Previous position (2012, 2015, 2016):** Humans crossing the Bering Strait into the Americas were the primary or sole cause of the megafaunal extinctions. The 2016 essay states this directly: "humans crossed the Bering Strait into the Americas and populated it. This event coincided with a decrease of total animal land mass from extinction and ecosystemic implosion." The 2015 essay frames the extinctions as "ecosystem disruption" caused by human arrival. This was the standard Martin/Diamond consensus position the author had inherited.

**Current position (2026):** The Late Pleistocene extinctions were likely driven by a combination of rapid climate oscillation during the Younger Dryas period (~12,800 years ago), possible cosmic impact event, and other factors. Human overhunting may have been a contributing factor but is not now considered the primary or sole driver. The peer-reviewed literature on the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis (Firestone, Kennett, Wittke, Comet Research Group, and related work) is the engagement source.

**What changed the author's mind:** Exposure to the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis literature.

**Implication for the book:** The rewilding argument is *strengthened* by this revision, not weakened. The case for restoration no longer rests on collective human guilt for past extinctions. It rests on the simple ecological observation that an ecosystem missing its keystone species functions poorly, regardless of why those species are missing.

**Where this matters in drafting:** Chapter 3 (Near Time), Chapter 9 (Proxies and Critics), and any chapter that discusses the cause of the extinctions. Source material from the 2015 and 2016 essays must not be reproduced verbatim where it asserts human causation as primary.

**Do not cite:** Graham Hancock or Hancock-adjacent popularizers, even though they have publicized aspects of this material. Engage the peer-reviewed literature directly.

### 2. The Universality of the Paleo Diet

**Previous position (2012):** The Paleo diet is broadly appropriate for human beings as a species. The species-appropriate-diet argument applied to humans as well as to the animals humans eat. Departures from a Paleo pattern were framed as causes of disease.

**Current position (2026):** There is no single human dietary pattern that is appropriate for all people. Microbiome individuality, metabolic variation, ancestral adaptation, and personal health context all mean that what works well for one person may not work well for another. The Paleo pattern works for the people for whom it works; it is not a universal prescription. The author's current professional work in microbiome and heavy metals over the past fourteen years has informed this revision.

**What changed the author's mind:** Sustained clinical and research engagement with microbiome science and individual variation in human nutrition.

**Implication for the book:** The species-appropriate-diet argument *still works for the animals in food systems*. A chicken did not evolve to subsist on soy meal; this is a fact about ecological function regardless of what any individual human should eat. The argument does not extend, as it did in 2012, to a universal human dietary prescription.

**Where this matters in drafting:** Any chapter that discusses dietary guidance for humans. The book makes no health claims and no universal dietary prescriptions. It addresses sustainability — what eating from particular food systems means for the systems themselves, and what frameworks exist for choosing among them.

**Tonal implication:** The 2012 manuscript's confident posture about "what humans should eat" must not appear in the new book in any form. The book's authorial posture toward dietary choice is one of respect for autonomy and informed decision-making.

### 3. Monocultures as a Categorical Evil

**Previous position (2012):** Monocultures are categorically destructive and unsustainable. The 2012 manuscript treats this as essentially a moral fact, with no room for improvement or qualification.

**Current position (2026):** Monocultures have serious sustainability problems, particularly under industrial-scale chemical-input regimes. They also have potential for significant improvement through better practices (cover cropping, reduced tillage, integration with perennial systems, precision agriculture, microbial soil management). The world is changing rapidly. Backward is not the only direction. There is room for monoculture improvement, and the book acknowledges it.

**What changed the author's mind:** Reading Matt Ridley's *The Rational Optimist*, and a broader recognition that the author's own pattern of "if I cannot argue the opposing position, I do not understand it" required taking the case for technological agricultural improvement seriously rather than dismissing it.

**Implication for the book:** The book does not present monocultures as categorical evil. It presents them as a particular system with measurable strengths and weaknesses across the four-axis framework (economic, social, environmental, survivability). Industrial commodity grain monocultures score poorly on survivability for specific, structural reasons; this is a different argument than "all monocultures are bad."

**Where this matters in drafting:** Chapters on industrial grain agriculture. The argument is structural (these specific systems have these specific vulnerabilities) rather than categorical (monocultures are evil).

### 4. Hostility Toward Vegetarians and Vegans

**Previous position (2012):** Extensive, sustained hostility. The 2012 manuscript devotes entire chapters to attacking vegetarians as people, mocking their reasoning, and characterizing their dietary choices as ethically and ecologically destructive. PETA is treated as an enemy organization.

**Current position (2026):** Vegetarians and vegans are autonomous adults making informed choices. The author respects their choice and their autonomy. The author's hope is that all readers — whatever dietary pattern they follow — make better-informed decisions, with full visibility into the systems their food comes from. Disagreement with specific vegetarian arguments about agricultural sustainability remains, but is engaged as intellectual disagreement rather than personal hostility.

**What changed the author's mind:** Time, maturation, and the recognition that the 2012 hostility was disproportionate, unkind, and counterproductive.

**Implication for the book:** No anti-vegetarian content of any kind survives into the book. Where vegetarian arguments about agricultural sustainability are engaged (notably the argument that reducing meat consumption is the path to lower agricultural impact), they are engaged respectfully and with the strongest version of the argument represented first. The book's engagement with figures like Lierre Keith remains possible but is conducted in the same fair register.

**See also:** The exclusion list and the anti-voice reference for specific patterns to suppress.

### 5. Personal Hostility Toward Named Figures

**Previous position (2012):** Open hostility toward Joel Salatin, Allan Savory (less so), Tom Vilsack, Michael Taylor, Colin Campbell, Sylvester Graham, John Harvey Kellogg, and others. Insulting nicknames, character attacks, and personal mockery were routine.

**Current position (2026):** No ill will toward Salatin, Savory, Pollan, or other figures in the food-systems conversation. Where their positions are critiqued, the critique is intellectual, fair, and presents their strongest argument first.

**What changed the author's mind:** Time, and the recognition that personal hostility toward people doing serious work in the field — even where the author disagrees with them — undermines the author's own arguments.

**Implication for the book:** All engagement with named figures is conducted under the steelman-first standard. The Salatin chapter (Chapter 6 in the current outline) is not a takedown; it is an honest assessment of the structural limits of even the best version of regenerative agriculture, with full credit to what Polyface achieves.

**See also:** The exclusion list (cleared-for-inclusion section) and the anti-voice reference (Section III, Adversarial-Posture Patterns).

### 6. The Pendergrass Scoring Method as a Personal Brand

**Previous position (2016):** The framework introduced in the 2016 essay was named "the Pendergrass Scoring Method of Sustainability."

**Current position (2026):** The framework remains intellectually intact and is the analytical spine of the book. The personal eponym is dropped. The framework is referred to descriptively (as "the survivability framework," "the four-axis assessment," or similar) rather than under any author's name.

**What changed the author's mind:** The book is a tribute to Paul Martin's work, not a vehicle for personal branding. A self-named scoring method works against the book's stated intent.

**Implication for the book:** No reference to "the Pendergrass Score" or any variant. The framework is described and used; it is not personally branded.

### 7. Paleo Approved as the Solution

**Previous position (2012):** The Paleo Approved certification is the answer to the inadequacies of existing food labels. The 2012 manuscript actively promotes the certification as the path forward.

**Current position (2026):** The certification effort is part of the author's history. It is not the book's solution, not the book's recommendation, and not promoted in the book. The book's argument is conceptual and structural; it does not point to a specific certification body as the answer.

**Implication for the book:** No promotional framing of Paleo Approved. If the certification's existence becomes briefly relevant to the narrative for historical reasons, it is acknowledged factually and then the book moves on.

**See also:** The exclusion list (Topics — Do Not Advocate).

## Minor or Tonal Revisions

### Use of profanity as analytical category

**Previous (2012):** "Bullshit" used dozens of times as the substantive verdict on opposing positions.
**Current (2026):** Profanity acceptable in moderation as occasional flavor; never as substitute for analysis.

### Confidence register

**Previous (2012):** Performed certainty, all-caps emphasis, repeated assertion as substitute for argument.
**Current (2026):** Calibrated claims, acknowledged uncertainty where it exists, evidence presented and allowed to do its work.

### Posture toward the reader

**Previous (2012):** Adversarial; reader frequently insulted, commanded, or characterized as belonging to a despised group.
**Current (2026):** Reader is a thinking adult addressed warmly and directly. No insults, no commands, no group-attacks.

### Use of pop-culture references

**Previous (2012):** Heavy reliance on Walking Dead, Hunger Games, Lion King, Princess Bride, Ghostbusters. Some chapters structured around TV shows.
**Current (2026):** Sparing use, only where genuinely illustrative, only where the reference will land for readers in 2026 and 2036.

### Self-reference

**Previous (2012):** "Original Paleo Honey Badger," "anthropocentric bitch," and other mascot-style self-naming.
**Current (2026):** No mascots. The author addresses the reader as a person, not a brand.

## Positions That Have Not Changed

For completeness, the following positions are continuous between the 2012 manuscript and the current book:

- The conviction that Paul S. Martin's rewilding work is foundational and underused
- The interest in Pleistocene baselines as a frame for restoration
- The structural critique of CAFO operations (specific facts updated; framework intact)
- The structural observation about Salatin's outsourced feed (math intact, framing transformed)
- The interest in species-appropriate diets *for animals in food systems*
- Respect and admiration for small-scale family farmers
- The conviction that current food systems have serious sustainability problems
- The interest in survivability as a meaningful axis of sustainability assessment

These are the threads of intellectual continuity. Where the new book draws on the 2012 manuscript, it draws on these continuities — never on positions listed in the major revisions section above.

## Process

This file is consulted before drafting any chapter that touches a topic listed here. Updates may be made by Pendergrass at any time. If a draft inadvertently includes a position the author has revised, it is treated as a blocking issue (same standard as the exclusion list).

If a position not yet listed here turns out to need revision during drafting, Pendergrass adds it to this file and any affected drafts are revised.
