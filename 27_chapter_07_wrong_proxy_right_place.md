# Chapter 7: The Wrong Proxy in the Right Place

The first time I saw Allan Savory speak in person was at his very first conference. There were maybe twenty-five people there, total. Maybe. It was a small classroom: no podium, no stage, no microphone. A handful of ranchers, a few people who had read the book and wanted to meet the man behind it, and me, in my twenties, with a marked-up copy of Paul Martin's *Twilight of the Mammoths* in my bag. I had read Savory's book twice by that point. I was not there to be convinced. I was there because I had a question.

The room was small enough that you could see everyone's face. Savory spoke with the calm authority of someone who had been making the same argument for decades and had outlasted most of his critics, and even at his age, there was something about him that felt younger than I had expected: a quickness, an energy, a sense that the ideas were still alive in him rather than calcified into a script. The talk he gave was genuinely good, substantive, grounded in decades of observation. He showed photographs of degraded rangeland in Zimbabwe and the same rangeland after years of planned grazing. He showed soil profiles. He showed vegetation transects. The evidence was real, and he presented it without overselling, which I respected. A lot of people in the regenerative space oversell. Savory did not need to. Only a small number of people can say they were in that room to witness such a great man on the way to becoming who he is today. I photobombed him afterward.

I asked him whether holistic management worked everywhere, or whether it worked better in some landscapes than others.

He paused. Then he said it was not working in the arid Southwest.

The room was small enough that you could hear the shift — the way twenty people adjusted in their chairs when the answer was not the one they expected. Savory did not hedge. He did not qualify it into nothing. He said the results had not tracked in the Southwest the way they had tracked in southern Africa or the Great Plains. He said it with the particular kind of candor that serious people use when they are being honest about something they have not yet resolved — not defensive, not dismissive, just honest.

I spent the rest of the session thinking about it. The talks continued. I took notes on a few of them, but my attention kept circling back to what Savory had said. The arid Southwest. The results had not tracked. I thought I knew why.

---

The food truck was parked at the edge of the conference grounds, one of those converted Airstream trailers with a sliding window and a short menu of things that could plausibly be called lunch. I got a plate and a bottle of water. Savory had also gotten a plate, and instead of eating outside we both took our food back into the classroom, and that is where the conversation happened. A few others wandered in and listened. I monopolized him during the entire lunch period. The food was good. Neither of us ate it until much later.

I should say what I had with me, because it matters. I had my conference bag, my phone, and a copy of Paul Martin's *Twilight of the Mammoths*. It was not a new copy. I had read it three times by that point, and it looked like it. The spine was cracked, the pages were dog-eared in places I had found worth returning to, and I had marked passages in the margins with the kind of shorthand that means nothing to anyone else: page numbers cross-referencing other pages, small arrows connecting ideas across chapters, question marks next to claims I had wanted to verify. It was a working copy of a book I had been thinking with for several years. I did not bring it to impress him. I brought it because I thought I might need to show him specific passages, and I did.

The conversation started where the Q&A had ended. I told him I thought I understood why the Southwest had been difficult, and that the answer was not in his framework but in the fossil record beneath the landscape his framework was being applied to. He looked at the book on the table between us. I opened it.

I introduced myself. I told him I ran a certification organization and that I had been working on food-system evaluation for years, which was true and also not the point. The point was the book. I told him I had been reading Paul Martin, the paleoecologist at the University of Arizona who had spent his career on the late Pleistocene extinctions and their ecological consequences. I thought Martin's work explained something about the Southwest that Savory's framework, on its own, could not.

He looked at the book on the table between us. He said he actually did know who Paul Martin was. And something in me shifted — I remember holding my hand out toward the book, a little wide-eyed, thinking: *Why has this work not been integrated with your work yet?* The fossil record and the management framework were sitting in the same intellectual space, pointed at the same landscapes, and no one had connected them. I told him the version I had been turning over in my head for months: that the Pleistocene fossil record preserves, in extraordinary detail, the specific animals that co-evolved with specific landscapes across the American continent, and that the record for the Southwest tells a story that does not involve cattle.

What I walked him through, and what I want to walk the reader through now because the argument is the hinge of everything that follows in this book, is a specific claim about the relationship between animal species and landscape. The claim is this: the fossil record tells us which animals co-evolved with which landscapes, and the American Southwest's fossil record does not say cattle.

Martin had spent decades building the evidentiary case. His chapter on the Grand Canyon, titled "Grand Canyon Suite," is the most concentrated version of it, and it is the chapter I had marked most heavily. The cave deposits along the Colorado River and its tributaries preserve one of the most detailed records of late Pleistocene fauna in the American West. Rampart Cave, a limestone overhang above the river in western Grand Canyon, contained stratified deposits of Shasta ground sloth dung, five feet of it in places, layered over thousands of years, preserved so well that the plant material in it could be identified to genus and sometimes species.[^1] Stanton's Cave, downstream in Marble Canyon, held the bones and dung pellets of Harrington's mountain goat, an extinct species whose remains turned up alongside those of bighorn sheep, small equids, and a scattering of bison. Condor remains appeared at multiple cave sites. The assemblage that emerged from the deposits was specific: ground sloths, mountain goats, equids, camelids, condors. Not one of those animals is a bovid.

I explained this to Savory as plainly as I could. The ground sloths at Rampart Cave had been eating globe mallow, Mormon tea, saltbush, catclaw acacia, yucca, and cactus. Dick Hansen's composition analysis of the dung showed that desert globe mallow leaves and stem fragments made up roughly half the plant parts in the deposit, with Mormon tea pollen accounting for eighteen percent and the remainder distributed among saltbush, acacia, common reed, and various succulents.[^2] What Hansen found almost none of was grass. The Shasta ground sloth, a thousand-pound browser that had occupied the Southwest for tens of thousands of years, was not a grazer. Neither were the camelids whose bones turn up across the basin-and-range country. The equids grazed, and so did the mammoths, but the overall assemblage was weighted heavily toward browsing, toward animals whose digestive systems and foraging behavior were calibrated to the woody, thorny, armed vegetation that still dominates the arid Southwest today.

Martin made the connection explicit in his later chapters. The thorny, oil-rich, tannin-laden shrubs of the Southwest — creosote, mesquite, catclaw acacia, the entire armored understory of the Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts — carry the structural signatures of vegetation that evolved under heavy browsing pressure from large-bodied animals.[^3] The thorns are not decorative. They are anti-herbivore defenses, and the herbivores they evolved against are the ones the fossil record preserves: ground sloths, camelids, and to a lesser extent the equids and proboscideans that ranged through the region. Martin put the principle as directly as anyone has: "the grasses, forbs, shrubs, and trees of the Americas coevolved with a much greater variety of large herbivores than exists today."[^4] The vegetation is still shaped by the animals. The animals are no longer there.

Savory listened. He did not interrupt, which I noticed. People who are performing attention interrupt. People who are actually paying attention do not. He asked a question about the camelids: where specifically the fossil evidence placed them, whether the Arizona deposits had been dated reliably. I turned to the pages I had marked and showed him the relevant passages. He read them. He asked about the equids, and I walked him through the Grand Canyon deposits and the broader Western record that Chapter 3 of this book has already covered. He asked about the dating methods, and I explained that the radiocarbon dates on the Rampart Cave deposits were among the most robust in the North American Pleistocene record: tens of thousands of years of stratified organic material, independently dated by multiple laboratories, cross-referenced against the geological stratigraphy of the cave itself.

He sat back in his chair. His plate was mostly untouched. He knew Martin's work. He said so. But he had not read it from this angle, had not connected the proxy question to the management question. He said something I will paraphrase rather than quote, because I did not record the conversation and I do not trust my memory to preserve his exact words across the years since: he said that in southern Africa, where the framework had been developed, the large herbivores were still present. The wildebeest, the zebra, the buffalo, the elephant — the animals the African grasslands had evolved under were still there, and the management methodology had been designed around their behavior. He had always known that the American situation was different in some respects. He had not thought about it from the direction I was pointing.

This was the moment the conversation pivoted, and it is the moment I want the reader to sit with, because the pivot matters. Savory was not admitting a flaw in his framework. He was recognizing a variable his framework had not needed to specify in the context where it was developed. In southern Africa, the proxy question does not arise, because the original animals are still there. You do not need to ask "which herbivore?" when the wildebeest and the zebra and the buffalo are standing in front of you. The question only becomes visible when the original animals are gone and you are choosing a substitute. And in the Americas, the original animals have been gone for twelve thousand years, and every herbivore currently on the landscape is a substitute, some closer to what the fossil record prescribes and some further away.

What I was suggesting was simple enough to say at a folding table over lunch, and difficult enough that almost no one had said it yet. The arid Southwest was calling for equid species: donkeys, horses, the animals whose lineage actually belonged to that landscape. It made sense to take a look at the fossils to get a better idea of the match with the flora of the area. Not just the animal, but the relationship between the animal and the vegetation: the specific co-evolutionary partnership that the fossil record preserves and the current management regime ignores.

I want to state the broader version of this argument with the same care I stated the lunch version at that table.

---

Allan Savory's holistic management framework is a genuine intellectual achievement. I do not say this as a courtesy before a critique, because what follows is not a critique. It is a refinement, one that I believe makes the framework significantly stronger.

The core of holistic management, as Savory and Jody Butterfield laid it out in *Holistic Management: A New Framework for Decision Making*, is the proposition that the relationship between large herbivores and grassland is not incidental but constitutive.[^5] The grassland needs the animal. Not the animal standing in place, overgrazing a single patch until the roots give out, but the animal moving — concentrated by predation pressure or management pressure into dense herds that graze intensively, trample standing dead material into contact with the soil surface, deposit urine and dung, and then move on, leaving the grazed area to recover for an extended period before the herd returns. The cycle of impact and recovery is what the grassland evolved under, and in its absence the grassland degrades: the standing dead material oxidizes in place rather than being incorporated into the soil, the root systems weaken without the stimulus of defoliation, and the bare ground between grass plants widens until the system tips into desertification.

The framework is powerful, and I do not use that word casually. It explains a great deal about the dynamics of grassland degradation that rest-based conservation management has struggled to address, and the empirical results from southern Africa, from the Great Plains, and from managed operations around the world are real. The ranchers and land managers who have adopted holistic management and seen their landscapes recover are not imagining it. The grass is coming back. The soil biology is rebuilding. The water-infiltration rates are improving. The framework works.

What the framework does not do — and this is the refinement, not the critique — is specify which animal. Holistic management tells you that the landscape needs a large herbivore, managed in a way that mimics the evolutionary pressure the landscape evolved under. It tells you how to manage the animal: the timing, the density, the recovery period, the monitoring. What it does not tell you is which animal belongs in which landscape. It treats the herbivore slot as generic: fill it with whatever large ruminant is available, manage it correctly, and the system responds.

For the Great Plains, this works. The shortgrass prairie evolved under bison, and cattle, which are bovids like bison, occupy a similar enough ecological niche that the substitution holds. The grass species, the root architectures, the soil microbiology, the grazing-and-recovery dynamics are all calibrated to a large-bodied bovid grazer, and cattle are a large-bodied bovid grazer. The proxy is close enough that the framework's management methodology can do the rest of the work. Holistic management on the Great Plains is, in a meaningful sense, restoring a relationship that the landscape recognizes.

The Southwest is different. And the reason it is different is the reason Savory's results had not tracked there, and the reason I had come to the conference with Martin's book.

The Southwest did not evolve under bovids. The fossil record is unambiguous on this point. The dominant large herbivores of the late Pleistocene Southwest were ground sloths, camelids, and equids: browsers and mixed feeders whose digestive systems and foraging behaviors were adapted to the woody, thorny, browse-heavy vegetation of the arid basins and canyons. Bison were present in the Southwest in the late Pleistocene, but they were a minor component of the fauna, not the dominant one. The bones in Stanton's Cave included some bison, but the assemblage was overwhelmingly Harrington's mountain goat and equids.[^6] The Rampart Cave deposits were ground sloth from top to bottom. The landscape's primary large-herbivore relationships were with animals that no longer exist, and those relationships were not with grazers.

When you put cattle on that landscape, even well-managed cattle under the best holistic management protocol available, you are asking a bovid grazer to fill an ecological role that was held by browsers and mixed feeders. The grass species of the arid Southwest, to the extent that they exist, did not evolve under the kind of intensive grazing pressure that the Great Plains grasses did. The shortgrass species of the Plains — the blue grama, the buffalo grass, the sideoats grama — co-evolved with bison over millions of years; their growth points sit at or below the soil surface, their root systems respond to defoliation with renewed vigor, their entire architecture is an adaptation to heavy, periodic grazing. The browse species of the Southwest — the acacias, the mesquite, the saltbush, the globe mallow, the yucca and prickly pear — evolved under a fundamentally different kind of pressure. They evolved to be eaten by animals with the body mass and the gut capacity to process woody, thorny, chemically defended plant tissue. Cattle do not browse acacia. Cattle do not eat globe mallow. Cattle do not strip mesquite pods. Cattle graze, and when the grass in the arid Southwest gives out under grazing pressure, the system does not recover the way a Great Plains grassland recovers, because the system was never organized around that kind of pressure in the first place. The browse thickens. The mesquite encroaches. The bare ground between the surviving grasses widens. And the conventional diagnosis — overgrazing, poor management, insufficient rest — misses the deeper cause. The animal is wrong for the landscape.

This is what I told Savory in the classroom over plates neither of us was eating. The holistic management framework provides the management methodology — the how. Martin's fossil record provides the species guide — the which. Together, they are stronger than either one alone. Separately, the framework has a blind spot in exactly the landscapes where the proxy mismatch is largest.

He did not reject it. He sat with it. He asked me whether anyone had tried running camelids in the Southwest under managed grazing protocols, and I told him the honest answer, which was that almost no one had, because the conversation about proxy selection had barely begun. Josh Donlan and his colleagues had published their provocative essay in *Nature* in 2005 proposing Pleistocene rewilding as a conservation strategy, arguing that the ecological histories of the Americas make the case for restoring large vertebrates, including proxies for extinct species, to landscapes that had co-evolved with them.[^7] Donlan's longer treatment in *The American Naturalist* the following year had laid out the agenda more carefully, with specific proposals and specific risk assessments.[^8] But the rewilding conversation had remained largely in the conservation-biology lane. Almost no one had connected it to the food-systems conversation. Almost no one had asked the question that seemed, to me, obvious: if the fossil record tells us which animals belong in which landscapes, and if those animals can be managed for food production, then the rewilding agenda and the food-systems agenda are the same agenda.

Savory, to his credit, saw the connection immediately. The man had spent his career arguing that the landscape needs the animal. He did not need to be persuaded of the principle. What the fossil record offered was a specification of the principle that his framework had left unspecified. The landscape needs *this* animal, in *this* place, because *this* is the animal the landscape's vegetation and soil biology evolved under. The proxy is not interchangeable. The proxy is chosen by the fossil record.

---

The principle extends far beyond the Southwest, and it extends far beyond the conversation I had with Savory in that classroom. Let me widen it.

If the fossil record provides the species guide, and I believe the evidence from multiple continents and multiple research programs shows that it does, then the question of which animals belong in which landscapes is answerable on a continental scale. The answer is not uniform. It varies by region, by biome, by the specific evolutionary history of the specific landscape in question. And the variation is exactly what makes the principle useful, because the variation tells you something the current food system does not know it needs to know.

The Great Plains, as I have said, want bovids. The shortgrass prairie is a bovid landscape, and the best available proxy for the extinct *Bison antiquus* is its living descendant, *Bison bison*. Bison restoration on the Plains, the work American Prairie is doing in Montana and tribal nations are doing across the northern Plains, is proxy selection that the fossil record endorses. The management methodology that Savory provides is appropriate here, because the proxy is appropriate here. The framework and the fossil record agree.

The Southwest wants something different. The arid basins and canyons of Arizona, New Mexico, and the northern Sonoran and Chihuahuan deserts evolved under camelids, equids, and ground sloths. The ground sloths have no living proxy; no extant species closely replicates a thousand-pound herbivorous xenarthran. But the camelids and equids do. Camels are already in the Americas: feral in parts of the West, stabled on hobby ranches, used in tourism and sport. Dromedaries thrive in arid heat, browse thorny shrubs that cattle will not touch, and produce milk with higher fat and protein content than cow's milk, a fact that matters if the question is not just which animal belongs here but what the animal provides. Horses and donkeys are already in the Americas: feral in vast herds across the basin and range, managed on public and private land, embedded in the cultural and economic fabric of the West. Donkeys in particular are superbly adapted to the arid Southwest: hardy in heat, efficient on sparse forage, and browsers as readily as grazers. The small equids whose bones turned up in Stanton's Cave were closer in size to a donkey than to a modern ranch horse. The species are here. What is missing is the ecological framing that would put them in the role the fossil record identifies as theirs. A camel on Southwest rangeland is not an exotic introduction. It is a proxy for *Camelops*, returning to a landscape its lineage shaped for millions of years before a twelve-thousand-year interruption. A donkey or a horse on that same rangeland is not a feral pest. It is the closest living relative of the Pleistocene equids whose bones lie in the canyon deposits, returning to an ecological role that the vegetation still remembers. The Southwest does not need more cattle. It needs the animals it lost.

The eastern hardwood forests present yet another case, and one that connects directly to the ecological-anachronism argument the Prologue introduced. The Pleistocene fauna of the eastern woodlands included mastodons, the dominant large-bodied browser of the deciduous forests, whose range extended from the Great Lakes to the Gulf Coast, along with woodland-adapted ground sloths, elk, deer, and a suite of smaller herbivores. The osage orange, the pawpaw, the honey locust, the Kentucky coffee tree. These megafaunal-dispersed fruit trees, which Janzen and Martin identified as ecological anachronisms, are eastern species, and the mouths they evolved for were mastodon and ground-sloth mouths.[^9] There is no living proxy for an American mastodon. But the principle still operates: the eastern hardwood food system should be oriented around the animals that the eastern hardwood fossil record identifies, which are browsers and mixed feeders, not open-grassland grazers. Silvopastoral systems in the eastern woodlands — integrating ruminant browse, mast, and tree crops on the same acreage — are closer to the fossil record's prescription than any open-pasture cattle operation, even a well-managed one.

The Gulf Coast and the subtropical Southeast present their own puzzle. The Pleistocene fauna of Florida and the coastal plain included tapirs, capybara, llamas, ground sloths, glyptodonts, and a suite of animals whose nearest living relatives now occupy Central and South America. The fossil presence of vampire bats and tapirs in Arizona and California, animals whose modern ranges are exclusively tropical, tells us that the ecological communities of the southern United States were connected to Neotropical faunas in ways that the current landscape does not reflect. Martin noted that tapirs ranged from the Sonoran lowlands to over six thousand feet on the Colorado Plateau, and that their late-Quaternary disappearance is more reasonably modeled as a retreat to tropical refugia under predation pressure than as a response to climate change.[^10] The proxy implications for the subtropical Southeast are different from those for the Southwest or the Plains: the animal assemblage the landscape evolved under was more diverse, more Neotropical in character, and farther from anything the current food system provides.

The boreal and subarctic tell a different story again. The Pleistocene fauna of the far north included woolly mammoths, steppe bison, horses, muskoxen, and caribou. Caribou and muskoxen survive. The mammoth and the steppe bison do not. The proxy question here is whether elephants, African or Asian, could serve as mammoth proxies in the boreal, a question Donlan and his colleagues raised explicitly and that the Pleistocene Park experiment in Siberia has begun to test with horses, bison, muskoxen, and yak.[^11] The point is not that elephants are about to be introduced to Alaska. The point is that the principle, the fossil record tells you which animal, applies to every landscape on the continent, and the answers it gives are not the same from one landscape to the next.

The map that emerges from this exercise is nothing like the map the food system currently operates from. The current map is simple: cattle everywhere, with regional adjustments for breed and forage type. The fossil-record map is complex: bovids on the Plains, camelids and equids in the arid West, browsers and mixed feeders in the eastern hardwoods, Neotropical assemblages in the subtropical South, Arctic-adapted species in the far north. The current map treats the continent as a single production surface. The fossil-record map treats it as what it actually is — a mosaic of distinct evolutionary histories, each with its own set of animal relationships, most of which have been unreciprocated for a hundred and twenty centuries.

This is what the current food system misses entirely. The industrial system treats animals as interchangeable production units and landscapes as interchangeable production surfaces. The regenerative-agriculture movement, for all its real achievements, has treated the proxy question as settled — cattle for grasslands, chickens and pigs for everything else — without consulting the record that would tell it whether those proxies are correct. Savory's framework gets the management right and leaves the species unspecified. Martin's fossil record specifies the species and does not provide the management. The synthesis, holistic management guided by paleontological proxy selection, is what neither framework achieves alone.

I am not proposing that the synthesis is easy to implement. I am proposing that it is the correct framework, and that the difficulty of implementation is not an argument against its correctness. The permits for running camels on Southwest rangeland do not currently exist in most jurisdictions. The infrastructure for processing camel or horse meat for the US market does not currently exist at any meaningful scale. The cultural resistance to eating horses in the United States is real and historically deep, and I am not dismissing it. That resistance has its own history, its own logic, and its own emotional weight, and a framework that requires people to change what they eat had better take seriously the reasons they eat what they eat now. The ecological risk assessments for introducing proxy species to landscapes they have not occupied in twelve thousand years are genuinely complex, and the critics of Pleistocene rewilding, Rubenstein and colleagues' careful objections in particular, deserve the engagement they will receive in Chapter 9 of this book.[^12] None of these obstacles are trivial. All of them are navigable.

What I am saying is that the question "which animal belongs here" has an answer, and the answer is in the ground beneath our feet. The fossil record is not a metaphor. It is a dataset. It contains the bones and the dung and the pollen and the diet profiles of the animals that co-evolved with every landscape on this continent, and those profiles tell us — specifically, empirically, verifiably — which species the landscape's vegetation, soil biology, and ecological dynamics evolved under. When we ignore that record and put cattle on a landscape that evolved under camelids, we are making a proxy-selection error. When we compound that error by building an entire food system around the wrong proxy, we are making the error structural.

The chapter before this one ended with a question: what would actually escape the substrate? What would an operation look like whose animals are selected for the landscape rather than for the commodity market?

The answer begins with the fossil record and a worn copy of Martin's book on a folding table between two people who care about the land. Savory spent his career arguing that the landscape needs the animal. Martin spent his career identifying which animal the landscape needs. The food-truck conversation was the moment I understood that the two frameworks are halves of the same argument, and that the argument, stated whole, reorganizes everything the food system thinks it knows about which species belong where.

I think about that conversation often. I think about Savory sitting with the information, not arguing, not deflecting, just absorbing it and asking the right questions. I think about Martin's book on the table between us — marked up, worn out, full of someone else's lifetime of work distilled into a set of observations so straightforward that the only mystery is why they have not yet reorganized the conversation they belong to. I think about the folding table, the food truck, the conference going on inside without us, and two people outside eating lunch and talking about which animals belong in which places on a continent that has been making do without most of its animals for longer than there has been agriculture.

The synthesis is not mine. The management framework belongs to Savory. The species framework belongs to Martin. What I am doing, what this book is doing, is putting the two together and asking what follows. What follows is the subject of the next chapter: from the principle to the plate. What does it actually look like to eat from landscapes managed under proxy assemblages the fossil record endorses? The question is not abstract. The animals are real, some of them are already here, and the landscapes are waiting.

The stratified dung deposits span approximately 40,000 to 11,000 radiocarbon years before present.

[^2]: Hansen, R. M. (1978). "Shasta ground sloth food habits, Rampart Cave, Arizona." *Paleobiology,* 4(3), 302–319. [^3]: Martin (2005), chapter 10 ("Restoration"), pp. 185–186. Martin's argument that the thorny, armed vegetation of the arid Southwest evolved under heavy browsing pressure from now-extinct large herbivores, and that the anti-herbivore defenses of these plants are calibrated to animals that have been absent for approximately 13,000 years.

[^4]: Martin (2005), p. 185. The full passage reads: "From fossils we know that the grasses, forbs, shrubs, and trees of the Americas coevolved with a much greater variety of large herbivores than exists today."

[^5]: Savory, A., & Butterfield, J. (1999). *Holistic Management: A New Framework for Decision Making.* Island Press. The foundational text of holistic management theory, laying out the relationship between large-herbivore impact, grassland health, and desertification reversal.

[^6]: Martin (2005), chapter 5. The Stanton's Cave faunal assemblage, discussed alongside the Rampart Cave deposits, includes predominantly Harrington's mountain goat (*Oreamnos harringtoni*) bones, with smaller numbers of bighorn sheep, two small species of *Equus*, and some bison. The initial public statement of the Pleistocene rewilding proposal, arguing that ecological history justifies the managed introduction of large vertebrates — including Old World proxies for extinct New World species — to North American landscapes.

[^8]: Donlan, C. J., Berger, J., Bock, C. E., Bock, J. H., Burney, D. A., Estes, J. A., Foreman, D., Martin, P. S., Roemer, G. W., Smith, F. A., Soulé, M. E., & Greene, H. W. (2006). The extended treatment of the rewilding proposal, with detailed discussion of proxy-species selection, ecological risk assessment, and phased implementation strategies for multiple North American biomes.

[^9]: Janzen, D. H., & Martin, P. S. (1982). "Neotropical anachronisms: the fruits the gomphotheres ate." *Science,* 215(4528), 19–27. The foundational paper establishing the concept of megafaunal fruit syndrome and identifying specific New World fruit species — including osage orange, honey locust, and pawpaw — as ecological anachronisms whose dispersal biology is calibrated to large-bodied herbivores that went extinct in the late Pleistocene.

[^10]: Martin (2005), chapters 9 and 10. [^11]: Zimov, S. A. (2005). "Pleistocene Park: return of the mammoth's ecosystem." *Science,* 308(5723), 796–798. Sergey Zimov's initial description of the Pleistocene Park experiment in northeastern Siberia, which reintroduced horses, bison, muskoxen, and other large herbivores to test the hypothesis that megafaunal grazing can restore the mammoth-steppe grassland ecosystem and reduce permafrost thaw.

[^12]: Rubenstein, D. R., Rubenstein, D. I., Sherman, P. W., & Gavin, T. A. (2006). "Pleistocene Park: does re-wilding North America represent sound conservation for the 21st century?" *Biological Conservation,* 132(2), 232–238. The most careful of the early critiques of the Donlan et al. rewilding proposal, raising concerns about ecological risk, invasive-species dynamics, and the assumption that proxy species will fill the ecological roles of their extinct counterparts.
