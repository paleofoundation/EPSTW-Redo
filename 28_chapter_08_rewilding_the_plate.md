# Chapter 8: Rewilding the Plate

The previous chapter ended with a principle: the fossil record tells you which animal belongs in which landscape, and the management framework tells you how to manage the animal once it is there. The principle is clean. What I want to do now is show what the principle looks like when it touches actual ground: real landscapes, real animals, real food being produced and consumed by real people, today, not in some aspirational future that requires a revolution before breakfast.

The vision this chapter describes is not hypothetical. Parts of it are already operating. Other parts are within reach of deliberate effort. The honest assessment of scale, what rewilded food systems can and cannot do at present, comes later in the chapter, because the honest assessment matters and the book's credibility depends on not overselling. But the starting point is what exists, because what exists is more substantial than most people in the food-systems conversation realize.

## What Already Exists

Start with the feral hog.

Texas is home to an estimated 2.6 million feral hogs (*Sus scrofa*), descendants of domestic pigs escaped or released over the centuries since Spanish colonization, now established across every one of the state's 254 counties.[^1] The animals are ecological demolition crews. They root through native grassland, destroy ground-nesting bird habitat, contaminate waterways, damage agricultural crops to the tune of an estimated $52 million per year in direct agricultural losses in Texas alone, and compete with native wildlife for mast and forage.[^2] They are, by any ecological measure, an invasive species causing measurable harm. They are also, by any nutritional measure, a substantial source of animal protein living on land that did not ask for them.

The dual value is underappreciated in the food-systems conversation. Texas Parks and Wildlife Department reports that hunters harvest approximately 750,000 feral hogs per year in the state, a figure that does not keep pace with the animals' reproductive rate. Feral sows can produce two litters per year, averaging five to six piglets per litter, and populations can double in four months under favorable conditions.[^3] The harvest, even at three-quarters of a million animals annually, is not reducing the population. It is converting a fraction of the ecological damage into usable food. The meat is lean, free-range in the most literal sense of the term, and produced without any of the inputs that industrial pork requires: no commodity feed, no confinement infrastructure, no antibiotics, no manure lagoons, no federal subsidies for corn. The animal feeds itself on the landscape, damages the landscape in the process, and the harvest simultaneously produces food and reduces the damage.

I am not romanticizing feral hogs. They are genuinely destructive, and their management is a serious and expensive problem. What I am pointing out is that the food-system value of their harvest is almost never counted in the food-systems literature, because the animals do not fit any of the categories the literature recognizes. They are not livestock. They are not game in the managed sense. They are not rewilded proxy species. They are an accident of colonial history that happens to produce millions of pounds of protein per year as a byproduct of pest management. The protein is real. The ecological benefit of the harvest is real. The fact that neither appears in any sustainability assessment I have encountered tells us something about the narrowness of what the assessments are measuring.

Bison are a different case: not an accident but a deliberate restoration, and one already well under way.

The American bison, *Bison bison*, is the only surviving North American megaherbivore from the Pleistocene assemblage this book has described. It survived the terminal extinction pulse, persisted through the Holocene in herds that European observers described in the millions, was reduced by the commercial hide hunt of the 1870s and 1880s to fewer than a thousand individuals, and has since been rebuilt to a continental population of roughly 500,000, most of them in commercial herds managed as livestock, roughly 20,000 in conservation herds managed primarily for genetic diversity and ecological function.[^4]

The conservation herds are the ones that matter for this chapter's argument. American Prairie, a private land conservation initiative in northeastern Montana, is assembling the largest contiguous tract of rewilded grassland in the temperate United States: currently over 450,000 acres of deeded and leased land along the Missouri River breaks, with a long-term acquisition target that would create a continuous habitat block adjoining the Charles M. Russell National Wildlife Refuge.[^5] Bison are central to the project. American Prairie's herd, managed for genetic diversity and free-ranging behavior rather than for commercial production metrics, is intended to restore the grazing dynamics the northern Great Plains evolved under: concentrated impact, long recovery, nutrient redistribution across a landscape large enough that the animals' movement patterns can express themselves without fencing constraints.

The food-system dimension is real and deliberate. American Prairie's bison produce meat, marketed under the Wild Sky Beef brand, that is sold commercially as a revenue stream supporting the conservation mission. The animals are not supplemented with grain. They eat what the prairie grows. They are harvested by skilled marksmen in the field rather than transported to a processing facility, which eliminates both the transport stress and the infrastructure dependency. The product that reaches the consumer is, in a meaningful sense, food from a rewilded landscape: an animal that belongs on that landscape by any baseline the fossil record supports, managed under conditions that approximate the ecological dynamics the landscape evolved under, producing food as a function of its ecological role rather than in spite of it.

Tribal nations across the northern Plains are doing related work on a different institutional and cultural foundation. The InterTribal Buffalo Council, a cooperative of more than seventy-six tribal nations, has facilitated the restoration of bison to tribal lands across nineteen states, with a collective herd now numbering in the tens of thousands.[^6] The restoration is simultaneously cultural and ecological: bison are central to the spiritual and subsistence traditions of many Plains nations, and their return to tribal land is an act of cultural restoration as much as an ecological one. The food produced from these herds feeds tribal communities directly, in some cases through programs that distribute bison meat as a healthier alternative to the commodity foods that federal nutrition programs have historically provided to reservation communities. The survivability profile of these operations is notable: the animals require no purchased feed, no confinement infrastructure, and no industrial inputs beyond the fencing and water infrastructure that any large-herbivore operation requires. The dependency profile is thin. The system functions because the animal and the landscape have a relationship that predates agriculture itself.

Wild-caught fish from intact fisheries represent another existing component that is already producing food from systems the industrial model has not organized. The Alaska salmon fisheries are the clearest case. Alaska's constitution mandates sustainable management of its fisheries, and the state's management regime, built on the stock-assessment methodology that Ray Hilborn and Carl Walters systematized in their foundational *Quantitative Fisheries Stock Assessment*, is among the most rigorous in the world.[^7] The system works by monitoring escapement: the number of spawning adults that pass upstream past counting stations to reproduce. Harvest is permitted only when escapement targets are met. In years when returns are low, fisheries are restricted or closed entirely. The result is a fishery that has sustained commercial-scale harvest for decades without the stock collapses that have characterized poorly managed fisheries elsewhere.

Hilborn and colleagues, in a 2020 analysis in *PNAS*, demonstrated that this kind of science-based management works at a global scale: fisheries under intensive management have, on the whole, been improving in stock status, while unmanaged or poorly managed fisheries continue to decline.[^8] The finding matters for the food-systems argument because it establishes that wild-harvest food production from intact ecosystems is not inherently extractive or unsustainable. It is extractive when it is unmanaged. It is sustainable when the management matches the biology. Alaska's salmon fisheries produce hundreds of millions of pounds of protein per year from a system that requires no feed inputs, no freshwater diversion, no antibiotic administration, and no arable land. The system's primary requirement is that the ecosystem remain intact and that the harvest be governed by the stock's reproductive capacity. The dependency profile is thin in the same way the bison operation's is thin: what the system requires is the ecosystem itself, and management disciplined enough to not take more than the ecosystem can give.

Shellfish restoration on both coasts extends the marine case. Oyster reef restoration in the Chesapeake Bay, driven by federal and state partnership programs, has rebuilt reef acreage that was functionally destroyed by a century of overharvest and disease pressure. A 2011 synthesis by Michael Beck and colleagues in *BioScience* documented that approximately eighty-five percent of the world's oyster reef habitat had been lost, and called for restoration as both an ecological and an economic priority: oyster reefs provide water filtration, shoreline stabilization, and habitat for commercially important fish and invertebrate species, in addition to producing a harvestable food product.[^9] The restored reefs in the Chesapeake, on the Gulf Coast, and in Puget Sound are early-stage examples of what marine rewilding looks like when it produces food: a system in which the harvested organism is simultaneously performing ecological work — filtering water, building reef structure, providing habitat — and producing a food product for human consumption. The dependency profile is, again, thin. The oyster needs the water column. It does not need feed, antibiotics, or industrial infrastructure.

Managed elk and deer populations across much of the American West round out the existing picture. State fish and wildlife agencies manage ungulate populations through regulated hunting seasons calibrated to population surveys, habitat carrying capacity, and predator-prey dynamics. The system is imperfect (chronic wasting disease is a serious and growing problem in cervid populations, and the politics of predator management complicate the ecological picture), but the food produced is substantial. The US Fish and Wildlife Service estimates that hunters harvest approximately six million white-tailed deer per year nationally, producing roughly 600 million pounds of venison.[^10] Elk harvests in the western states add to the total. This is food from landscapes that have been managed, at least in part, for the ecological relationships the animals participate in. It is not food from a rewilded system in the full sense this book describes — most state wildlife management operates under the 1492 baseline the book has critiqued — but it is food from animals on landscapes they belong to, managed for population health rather than for maximum commodity throughput, and it represents an existing infrastructure of harvest, processing, and distribution that a more deliberately rewilded food system could build upon.

## What Could Exist

The distance between what already exists and what could exist with deliberate effort is shorter than most people assume. It is a distance of policy, of infrastructure, and of imagination, not of technology or of ecological feasibility.

Knepp Estate in West Sussex, England, is the most thoroughly documented case of what happens when a conventional farm is released into a rewilding process, and Isabella Tree's 2018 account of the project in *Wilding* is the single best available narrative of rewilding as a food-producing enterprise.[^11]

The Knepp story is worth telling in some detail, because the details are what make it instructive. The estate had been farmed conventionally for generations: arable crops, dairy cattle, the full infrastructure of a productive English lowland farm. By the late 1990s, the operation was losing money. The heavy Weald clay soils were marginal for arable production, and the costs of maintaining the fertility and drainage that intensive cropping required on those soils exceeded what the crops returned. Charlie Burrell and Isabella Tree made a decision that was, at the time, seen by neighboring farmers as somewhere between eccentric and irresponsible: they stopped farming the land conventionally and allowed it to begin reverting.

What happened over the subsequent two decades is one of the most closely observed ecological transitions in the European literature. The key decision — and the decision that makes Knepp relevant to this book rather than merely to the conservation literature — was to stock the land with proxy herbivores chosen for their ecological role rather than for their production characteristics. Old English longhorn cattle, Exmoor ponies, Tamworth pigs, and fallow deer were introduced, not managed in paddocks or rotations, but allowed to range freely across the estate, creating their own grazing and browsing patterns in response to the vegetation as it developed. The ponies browsed scrub and maintained open rides. The cattle grazed the grassland areas and created the dung-based invertebrate habitat that supports insectivorous birds. The pigs rooted through the woodland floor, creating the soil disturbance that germination of many native plant species requires. The deer browsed the woodland edge. Each species was performing a different ecological function, and the combination of functions produced a landscape that neither species could have produced alone. The results, documented by ecological surveys conducted by Imperial College London and other research institutions, were striking. Species that had been absent from the region for decades reappeared: turtle doves, nightingales, peregrine falcons, purple emperor butterflies, and all five British species of owl now breed on the estate.[^12] Soil biological activity increased. Water retention improved. The landscape shifted from a simplified agricultural surface into a structurally complex mosaic of scrub, wood pasture, grassland, and wetland, a mosaic that ecologists recognized as closer to the natural vegetation structure of lowland Britain than either the arable farmland it had replaced or the dense closed-canopy woodland that "natural" often implies.

The food produced from Knepp is not incidental. The estate sells pasture-fed organic meat from its free-roaming herds — beef, venison, pork — and the revenue from the meat operation, combined with income from ecotourism and conservation stewardship payments, has made the estate financially viable in a way the conventional farming operation had not been for years. The animals are not supplemented with purchased feed. They eat what the rewilding landscape produces. The meat is marketed as a premium product, and the market absorbs it. The estate demonstrates, at a scale large enough to be taken seriously (roughly 3,500 acres), that a rewilded landscape can produce food, support ecological recovery, and generate revenue simultaneously.

Tree's documentation of the Knepp process is careful about what the project does and does not prove. It proves that a specific piece of marginal English farmland, released from conventional management and stocked with proxy herbivores, produced measurable ecological recovery and a financially viable food product over a twenty-year period. It does not prove that every piece of farmland would respond the same way, or that rewilding is universally more profitable than conventional agriculture, or that the Knepp model scales to feed a nation. What it proves is that the model works: that rewilding and food production are not in opposition, that the landscape can do both, and that the animals doing the ecological work are also the animals producing the food.

American Prairie, which I described above in its current form, represents the most ambitious version of this model currently under development in the United States. The long-term vision — a continuous habitat block of several million acres along the Missouri River — would create a landscape large enough for bison herds to express genuinely free-ranging behavior, for predator-prey dynamics to operate without intensive human management, and for the prairie ecosystem to function at something approaching its pre-industrial ecological capacity. The food-system implications are direct: bison managed at that scale, on that landscape, under those conditions, would produce food as a function of their ecological role. The harvest would be governed by population dynamics rather than by production targets. The product would be genuinely wild-harvested meat from a genuinely rewilded landscape.

Smaller-scale rewilding efforts on private and public land across the United States represent a distributed version of the same trajectory. Ranchers in the southern Plains who have replaced cattle with bison on shortgrass prairie are running operations that the fossil record endorses and that produce food with dependency profiles substantially thinner than conventional beef. Landowners in the Southwest who are experimenting with managed burro and horse populations on degraded rangeland are testing, in practice, the proxy-selection hypothesis Chapter 7 described. Coastal restoration programs that combine oyster reef rebuilding with regulated harvest are producing food from ecosystems they are simultaneously restoring.

The distributed model deserves attention on its own terms, because the distributed model is how most food-system transitions actually proceed. The shift from draft horses to tractors did not happen by decree. It happened farm by farm, across decades, as individual operators made individual decisions based on what worked for their land and their economics. A shift toward rewilded food production would likely follow the same pattern: individual ranchers choosing bison over cattle on land that responds better to bison, individual landowners leasing acreage for conservation grazing rather than for commodity production, individual coastal communities investing in reef restoration that produces both ecological benefit and harvestable shellfish. Each individual decision is small. The aggregate is a trajectory, and trajectories compound. A hundred small operations producing food from rewilded landscapes, spread across a dozen bioregions, constitute a proof-of-concept network that no single demonstration site can provide. They test the model across soils, climates, regulatory environments, and market conditions. They generate the data that policy needs to scale what works. And they produce food while they do it: food that people eat, food that enters local and regional markets, food that gradually makes the idea of eating from rewilded systems less exotic and more ordinary.

None of these operations is large enough, individually, to register in the national food-supply statistics. Collectively, they represent a direction the food system could move in, with deliberate support, at a pace that is honest about what is currently possible and ambitious about what could become possible.

## The Honest Scaling Assessment

The trajectory matters, and stating it honestly matters more.

Rewilded food systems cannot, today, feed every person currently eating from industrial systems. The numbers do not support that claim and I am not making it. The roughly 500,000 bison in the United States, even if all of them were managed as conservation herds on rewilded grassland, would produce a small fraction of the roughly 27 billion pounds of beef the US market consumes annually.[^13] The Alaska salmon fisheries, impressive as they are, produce roughly 700 million to 1 billion pounds of fish per year, a substantial total, but a fraction of the roughly 6.3 billion pounds of seafood Americans consume annually.[^14] Feral hog harvest, managed elk and deer harvest, restored shellfish, and the scattered rewilding operations on private land add real volume without changing the fundamental arithmetic. Rewilded food systems today occupy a margin. The margin is real and productive, but it is a margin.

The argument this book is making is not that the margin should replace the center overnight. The argument is about trajectory: about which direction the food system should be moving, about which investments in land, infrastructure, and policy would produce the most durable food systems over the next century, and about what the framework developed in this book reveals when it is applied honestly to the options available.

Three things are true simultaneously, and the honest position requires holding all three.

First, rewilded food systems score well across all four axes of the framework this book has developed. They score well on the economic axis where they have been given the chance to operate: Knepp is financially viable, American Prairie's bison operation generates revenue, Alaska's salmon fisheries support a multi-billion-dollar industry. They score well on the social axis: tribal bison restoration has direct community-health and cultural-restoration dimensions, managed hunting supports rural economies and wildlife management infrastructure, coastal shellfish restoration employs restoration workers and supports fishing communities. They score well on the environmental axis: by definition, a system that produces food as a function of ecological restoration is not trading ecological health against food production but producing both together. And they score well on the survivability axis, which is the axis that matters most for the argument this book has been building.

The survivability score is where the distinction sharpens. Recall the axis as Chapter 4 defined it: survivability asks what a system requires to keep functioning, and what happens when those requirements are not met. An industrial feedlot requires continuous commodity grain, continuous diesel, continuous antibiotics, continuous federal subsidies, and continuous stable climate. A regenerative pasture operation requires most of what the industrial system requires for its monogastric enterprises, as Chapter 6 documented. A bison herd on rewilded prairie requires grass, water, and management. The dependency profile is not zero — the management requires human skill, the fencing requires materials, the harvest requires processing infrastructure — but the profile is structurally thinner than anything the industrial or regenerative alternatives can offer. A system whose primary input is an intact ecosystem is a system that does not collapse when the commodity supply chain is disrupted, because it was never on the commodity supply chain. It does not require diesel for feed delivery, because the feed is growing under the animals' feet. It does not require antibiotics for disease management, because the stocking densities and movement patterns that produce disease pressure in confined systems do not exist. It does not require federal subsidies to make the economics work, because the economics are not built on the gap between input costs and commodity prices that the subsidies exist to bridge.

This is not a theoretical advantage. It is the practical consequence of a food system organized around ecological relationships rather than around industrial inputs. Consider what this means concretely. When the 2022 disruption of Ukrainian grain exports drove US feed costs to levels that Chapter 4 described as existential for some pastured-poultry operations, American Prairie's bison herd was unaffected. The animals did not eat commodity grain. The price of corn was irrelevant to their diet, to their management, and to the economics of their harvest. When the Texas winter storm of 2021 knocked out grid power across the state and killed livestock in operations dependent on electrically pumped water and heated shelters, free-ranging elk and deer in the same state continued to drink from streams and shelter in draws, as they had always done. The resilience was not engineered. It was inherent in the relationship between the animal and the landscape. That relationship predates, and does not depend on, any of the industrial infrastructure that the conventional system treats as a permanent condition. The poultry operation in the Piedmont that opened Chapter 4, the one that scored well on three axes and collapsed when the corn supply was disrupted, could not have collapsed on the fourth axis if it had never been on the commodity corn supply chain in the first place. Rewilded food systems are not on that supply chain. Their vulnerability surfaces are different: they are vulnerable to ecosystem degradation, to climate shifts that alter the vegetation the animals depend on, to disease events in wild or semi-wild populations, to political decisions about land use and access. Those vulnerabilities are real and should not be minimized. They are also vulnerabilities of a fundamentally different kind from the ones the industrial system carries, because they are vulnerabilities of the ecosystem itself rather than vulnerabilities of the industrial substrate layered on top of the ecosystem.

Second, the margin is expandable. The amount of land currently managed for rewilded food production is a small fraction of the land that could be managed that way. American Prairie's eventual footprint, if the acquisition and leasing program reaches its long-term target, would be measured in millions of acres, a single project on a single section of the northern Great Plains. Equivalent projects on equivalent landscapes across the continent — the shortgrass prairies of the central Plains, the basin-and-range country of the Southwest, the Gulf Coast marshlands, the eastern hardwood silvopastoral opportunities — would multiply the acreage by an order of magnitude. The constraint is not ecological feasibility. It is capital, policy, and the cultural willingness to manage landscapes for ecological function and food production simultaneously rather than treating the two as competing uses.

Third, the question is one of trajectory, not of overnight replacement. No serious person proposes dismantling the industrial food system on Monday and replacing it with bison and salmon on Tuesday. The industrial system feeds people. It feeds people badly, in the sense that the system's externalities are substantial and its survivability is poor, but it feeds them, and any transition away from it has to account for what people eat during the transition. The trajectory this book proposes is the deliberate expansion of rewilded food systems alongside — not instead of — the existing infrastructure, with the understanding that every acre moved from industrial production to rewilded production is an acre that scores better on all four axes and is more likely to still be producing food in fifty years under conditions that may look nothing like today's.

The trajectory framing is not a concession. It is the honest version of the argument. A food system that scores well on all four axes, that produces food as a function of ecological restoration rather than in spite of ecological degradation, and that does not depend on the persistence of an industrial substrate whose vulnerabilities the last decade has made starkly visible — that system deserves expansion. It deserves investment. It deserves the policy support and the cultural attention that have historically been directed at the systems it would gradually, partially, honestly replace. The argument is not that rewilded systems are the only answer. The argument is that they are the most underexplored answer, and that the framework this book has developed reveals them as the direction most consistent with the conditions the next century is likely to impose.

## From the Framework to the Fork

The systems described in this chapter are not abstractions. They produce food that people eat. The bison from American Prairie and from tribal herds becomes steaks and roasts and ground meat that appears in kitchens and restaurants. The salmon from Alaska's managed fisheries fills freezers and feeds families. The venison from managed elk and deer harvests is processed and consumed by millions of American hunters and their communities every year. The pork from feral hog harvest in Texas, still underutilized relative to the volume available, feeds the hunters who take the animals and, increasingly, supplies processors who sell it into regional markets. The beef and pork and venison from Knepp feeds people in West Sussex and beyond.

What eating from rewilded systems looks like, in practice, is not exotic. It looks like a bison burger. It looks like grilled salmon. It looks like venison stew. It looks like oysters from a restored reef. It looks like food: recognizable, preparable, delicious in the ways that animal protein from well-managed systems is always delicious, because the animals ate what they were supposed to eat and lived on land that supported them. The plate is not strange. The system behind the plate is different, and the difference is what the framework measures.

There is a deeper point here about the relationship between the food and the landscape it comes from, and the point is worth stating because it connects to the book's larger argument about what sustainability means. When a bison grazes the northern Plains, it is not merely producing meat. It is performing the ecological work the prairie requires — the trampling, the grazing, the nutrient deposition, the seed-coat scarification — and the meat is a consequence of that work. When an oyster filters water in a restored Chesapeake reef, it is not merely producing a shellfish product. It is cleaning the estuary, building reef structure, creating habitat for juvenile fish and crustaceans, and the harvestable oyster is a consequence of that function. When a managed deer herd is held at carrying capacity by regulated hunting, the hunting is performing the predator function that wolves and cougars once performed at broader scale, and the venison is a consequence of that ecological substitution. In each case, the food is not the purpose of the system: the ecological function is the purpose, and the food is what the function produces. This inverts the relationship that industrial agriculture assumes. Industrial agriculture organizes landscapes around food production and treats ecological consequences as externalities to be managed. Rewilded food systems organize landscapes around ecological function and treat food production as an output of that function. The inversion is not merely philosophical. It produces systems that score differently on the framework this book has developed, because the framework asks what a system requires to keep functioning, and a system whose purpose is ecological function requires the ecosystem — not the industrial substrate — to keep functioning.

The previous chapter established the principle that the fossil record provides the species guide for which animals belong in which landscapes. This chapter has shown that the principle is not merely a principle. It is an operational reality in the places where it has been given the chance to operate. Bison on the northern Plains are the fossil record's recommendation in practice. Salmon in Alaska's rivers are an intact fishery managed for persistence rather than for maximum extraction. Elk across the western mountains are a managed population of animals that belong where they stand. Even the feral hog — the accidental case, the invasive species converted into food — demonstrates that the landscape produces animal protein whether we plan for it or not, and that the question is whether we harvest it intelligently.

I want to pause on that inversion, because it is the thing this chapter has been building toward. The food systems described in Chapters 5 and 6 (industrial production and regenerative agriculture, respectively) both organize the landscape around the production of food. They differ enormously in how they do it, and the regenerative version is substantially better by every measure this book has applied. But both start from the same premise: the landscape is a production surface, and the question is how to manage the production surface responsibly. Rewilded food systems start from a different premise: the landscape is an ecosystem, the ecosystem has a set of relationships it evolved to maintain, and the food is what those relationships produce when they are allowed to operate. The distinction is not semantic. It produces food systems with fundamentally different dependency profiles, fundamentally different vulnerability surfaces, and fundamentally different relationships to the conditions the next century is likely to impose.

The next chapter takes the serious critics of Pleistocene rewilding head-on. The vision this chapter has described is not uncontested. Biologists have raised legitimate concerns about proxy-species equivalence, about ecological risk, about the cultural and political feasibility of the proposals the rewilding literature has advanced. Those concerns deserve the engagement Chapter 9 will give them. What this chapter has tried to establish, before the critics are heard, is that the vision is not speculative. Parts of it are already here. The animals are real. The landscapes are real. The food is real. The question is not whether rewilded food systems can work: they already do, at the scales where they have been tried. The question is whether the scales can grow, and what it would take to grow them honestly. The framework says the direction is right. The next chapter asks whether the objections are answerable.
