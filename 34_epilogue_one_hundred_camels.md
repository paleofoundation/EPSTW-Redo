# Epilogue: Returning to the Orchard

The orchard does not exist yet. I want to return to it anyway.

I opened this book with a camel: *Camelops hesternus*, yesterday's camel, native to North America for most of the history of its genus, gone for thirteen thousand years, remembered mostly in bones and the occasional museum mount. I opened it with an osage orange tree, producing its softball-sized fruit for a mouth that is no longer there. I opened it with a wish: to see one hundred camels in an osage orange orchard, just to see if it could work. The wish was not a program. It was the thing the program would be for.

The book between the prologue and this page has been the program, or at least the argument for one. Thirteen chapters of framework, evidence, application, and honest reckoning with what the framework cannot do. The reader who has come this far knows what the four-axis assessment measures and what it reveals about the dominant food system. The reader knows what the Columbian Curtain hides and what the near-time window makes visible. The reader has walked through the Polyface math, the Savory food-truck conversation, the fossil record of the Grand Canyon, the critics' strongest arguments, the marine restorations, the Indigenous management traditions that operate under a parallel frame. The reader has sat with Chapter 13's description of the structural changes that would need to happen for any of this to move from argument to practice, and the reader has noticed — I hope — that the chapter was honest about the distance between the argument and the enactment.

I do not want to summarize any of that. The reader has just read it. What I want to do, here at the end, is return to the image that started it, because the image has changed, not in its content but in what it carries.

When I described the orchard in the prologue, it carried one thing: a longing. A specific want, plainly stated, for a specific arrangement of animals and trees on a specific kind of ground. The longing was personal. It was mine. I did not try to dress it as policy or as science, because it was not those things. It was the reason I had spent years thinking about the questions the book asks, and it seemed honest to say so at the beginning, before the analytical apparatus had been set up and before the reader had any reason to take the analysis seriously.

The longing has not gone away. I still want to see those camels. I still want to know what the fruit looks like when it has a mouth. But the image is no longer sitting in the space it sat in at the prologue's close — a private wish, carried by a person the reader had just met, before the argument had been made. It is sitting, now, on the other side of the argument. The orchard has accumulated meaning it did not have sixty thousand words ago.

The camels in the orchard are not just camels anymore. They are the proxy-selection principle from Chapter 7 — the fossil record consulted, the co-evolutionary relationship identified, the right animal placed in the right landscape for reasons the paleontology supports. They are the survivability argument from Chapter 4 — animals that feed themselves on what the landscape produces, with a dependency profile thinner than anything the industrial system can offer. They are the proof-of-concept experiment from Chapter 9 — bounded, monitored, designed to generate data rather than to prove a point. They are the structural change from Chapter 13 — the permitting pathway that does not yet exist, the regulatory category that has not been created, the political constituency that has not yet found itself. The hundred camels carry all of that now. They carried none of it before.

The osage orange carries something different than it did at the beginning, too. In the prologue, it was an anachronism: a tree stranded in a present that had forgotten what it was for, producing fruit for a disperser that had been gone for a hundred and twenty centuries. That much has not changed. What has changed is the frame around it. The tree is no longer an isolated curiosity, a bit of natural-history trivia about the deep past. It is an instance of a pattern the book has traced across the entire continent: the grasslands still calibrated to grazers that are gone, the thorny shrubs still armed against browsers that are absent, the pronghorn still running from nothing, the kelp forests and oyster reefs stripped to fractions of their historical extent, the Indigenous fire regimes suppressed, the soil communities waiting for trampling patterns that have not been produced in twelve thousand years. The osage orange, in the prologue, was one tree with one absent partner. In the epilogue, it is a representative of a continent whose ecological vocabulary has been spoken at half-volume for longer than there has been agriculture, and whose restoration (if it is attempted, if it is permitted, if the experiments are funded and the baselines are expanded and the political will materializes) would be the most ambitious act of ecological repair in the history of the species attempting it.

That is a large thing to rest on one tree and a hundred hypothetical camels. I am aware of the disproportion. The book has been aware of it from the beginning. The analytical argument is not proportional to the longing. The longing is outsized, personal, and not fully defensible on the evidence the book has assembled. The evidence supports a framework. The framework supports a set of experiments. The experiments might support a direction. The direction might, over decades, produce a food system organized around ecological function rather than industrial inputs. None of that is the same as a hundred camels in an orchard. The orchard is where the argument started, and it is further from realization than any of the intermediate steps the book has described.

I think this disproportion is honest rather than embarrassing. The book began with a wish because the wish is what generated the work. The work is what the book contains. The work does not fulfill the wish, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What the work does is take the wish seriously enough to ask what it would actually require (what framework, what evidence, what engagement with critics, what policy changes, what institutional capacity, what cultural shift) and to lay that out as clearly as I can, knowing that I do not control most of the conditions the answer depends on.

The book has not solved the problem it describes. It has not demonstrated that Pleistocene rewilding works at scale, because the experiments have not been conducted. It has not demonstrated that the four-axis framework produces better policy outcomes than the three-axis model, because the framework has not been adopted by any policymaking body. It has not demonstrated that Americans will eat camel, or that the permitting pathway will be created, or that the farm bill will be restructured, or that the cultural imagination will expand to include a continent whose memory is older than its paperwork. The book has made an argument. The argument is partial, contested, and dependent on conditions I do not control. I have tried to make it carefully and to be honest about where the evidence is strong, where it is provisional, and where I am asking for something the evidence does not yet support.

What I am asking for, in the end, is not certainty. It is not even, really, that the experiment be mine. I read Stephen Hawking when I was young, the way you do, with a flashlight, understanding half of it, and ever since I have wanted to see a black hole and throw popcorn at its event horizon. The popcorn was mine. The want was mine. The physics were going to go on doing what they do without my participation, and the participation was never the point. The point was to watch the popcorn go in and know, finally, what happens at the edge. Or a refrigerator. Just to watch. I will most likely die before I get to throw popcorn at an event horizon, or a refrigerator. What will not die is that I really, truly, just want to see. The wanting does not go away. It hasn't now for thirty years.

What I am describing at the end of this book is the same kind of wanting. I want to know whether a parcel of desertified ground in the basin and range country of California or Nevada — real ground, not hypothetical, ground that is sick and losing what little it has left to lose — can be flipped. Not flipped the way a house gets flipped, exactly, though that is the register I mean. Bought distressed. Returned to function by putting the right animals back on it and the right trees in it, running as a closed-loop system for a decade with honest monitoring. Sold, or kept, or left to the commons, whichever way the experiment wants to go once the experiment has been run. What I want to know is whether the ground can be healed. I see desertification as a disease. I do not see it as a settled condition. I do not see it as something the ground has chosen, or earned, or is stuck with. I see it as something nobody has yet been permitted to treat.

I have said as much at Allan Savory conferences and been eye-rolled by people in that audience whom I love and respect and whose work I admire. I do not care. I am not trying to win the argument at the conference. I am trying to find out whether the answer exists. Those are not the same project, and the second one is the one I am interested in.

The experiment is the point.

The experiment does not need to be mine. It needs to be somebody's. The ground is there. The camels are there: feral in parts of the American West, stabled on hobby ranches, eating mesquite because that is what camels eat when you put them on ground that grows it. The osage orange is there, falling and rotting and asking for a mouth nobody has yet put in front of it. The regulatory pathway does not exist, but that is what Chapter 13 was for. The patron, the university, the protocol, the acreage: none of these exist yet in the configuration the experiment requires, and I do not control whether they come together. What I can do is name what I am asking for, and hope the person who can do it reads this far.

I do not know what the data would show. I think the hypothesis is worth testing, or I would not have written the book. I think the ecological logic is sound: that a continent missing its keystone herbivores for twelve thousand years is functioning below its capacity, that the fossil record provides a guide to restoration that the current conservation baseline does not see, and that food systems organized around ecological function are more survivable than food systems organized around industrial inputs. I think these things because the evidence the book has assembled supports them. I do not think them with the certainty I once brought to simpler versions of this argument, because the revisions the book has documented have taught me something about the relationship between confidence and evidence that the earlier version of me had not yet learned.

The orchard is still hypothetical. The camels are not standing in it. The osage oranges are still falling, still rotting in place, still producing for no one. Somewhere in the arid basin country of the Southwest, a dromedary on a hobby ranch is eating mesquite, because mesquite is what camels eat when you put them on ground that grows it, and neither the camel nor the mesquite requires an argument to know what they are for.

I would still like to see those camels. I would still like to know what happens when the fruit has a mouth. I would like, more than either of those things, to know whether the ground can be flipped — whether a parcel that has been desertifying for a century can, under the right animals and the right trees and enough time, come back. I will probably not be the person who finds out. I want to be alive when somebody does.
