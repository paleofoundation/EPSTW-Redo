# Chapter 13: What Must Change

This book has been, for the most part, an argument directed at readers, at people who buy food, think about food, and have some capacity to choose what food they buy. The framework it has proposed, the four-axis assessment of food systems, is a tool for individual evaluation: a way for a person standing in a grocery store or a farmers' market or a restaurant to think more clearly about what the food in front of them depends on, what it costs in ways the price does not capture, and whether the system that produced it could survive conditions different from the ones it was designed for. The practical guidance the previous chapter offered was guidance for individuals making decisions within the food system as it currently exists. That guidance is real, and I stand behind it.

It is also not sufficient.

The limits of consumer choice as an engine of systemic change are well documented in the food-systems literature and are worth stating directly, because the temptation to end a book like this with an appeal to individual action is strong and the appeal is, on its own, inadequate to the scale of the problem the book has described. A 2012 analysis by Daniel Welch in the *Journal of Consumer Culture* examined the structural assumptions behind the "vote with your fork" framing that has dominated the sustainable-food conversation since Michael Pollan's *The Omnivore's Dilemma* helped popularize it, and concluded that consumer choice operates within a system whose structure is determined by forces the consumer does not control: commodity policy, land-use regulation, subsidy allocation, infrastructure investment, and the path dependencies of an industrial food system built over seventy years of policy and capital accumulation.[^1] The consumer can choose among the options the system offers. The consumer cannot, by choosing, change what options the system offers.

This is not a criticism of the readers who have followed this book's argument. It is a structural observation about the relationship between individual action and systemic architecture. A reader who adopts the four-axis framework, shifts purchasing toward rewilded and sustainably managed food sources, supports local producers operating outside the industrial commodity system, and advocates for the experiments the book has called for is doing everything the individual can do. If a hundred thousand readers did the same, the effect on the food system would be measurable but bounded. If a million readers did the same, the effect would be larger but still bounded by the same structural constraints. The industrial commodity system, the subsidy architecture, the land-use regulatory framework, the conservation baselines, and the institutional infrastructure that governs what kind of food production is permitted, incentivized, and scaled in the United States are not determined by consumer purchasing decisions. They are determined by policy, by regulation, by the allocation of public resources, and by the institutional inertia of a system that has been building in its current direction since the middle of the twentieth century.

The book's argument, if it is correct, has implications that extend past the individual reader. If the four-axis framework reveals structural vulnerabilities in the dominant food system that three-axis assessment cannot see, then the response to those vulnerabilities cannot be purely individual. If the fossil record provides a guide to which animals belong in which landscapes, and if the proxy-species experiments the book has called for are worth conducting, then the institutional and regulatory conditions under which those experiments could occur need to exist. If the rewilding vision the book has described, in which food is produced from landscapes managed under ecologically appropriate species assemblages, represents a direction the food system should move toward, then the policy environment needs to make that direction possible rather than impossible.

This chapter walks through what would have to change at the structural level for the book's vision to move from argument to practice. I want to be direct about the difficulty. None of what follows is easy. None of it is politically assured. I do not have a theory of how these changes get enacted, because I do not think such a theory exists in honest form. What I have is a description of the structural conditions that would need to be different, offered in the hope that naming them clearly is a precondition for working toward them, even when the path from naming to enactment is not visible.

## Subsidy Reform

The federal subsidy structure is the floor on which the current food system stands. The commodity support system, administered through successive iterations of the farm bill since the Nixon-era restructuring of agricultural policy in the early 1970s, has shaped what the United States grows, how it grows it, where the economic incentives in agriculture point, and what kinds of food production are financially viable at scale. The structural effects are not subtle. Between 1995 and 2021, the federal government disbursed approximately $478 billion in agricultural subsidies, with the majority flowing to commodity crop producers — corn, soybeans, wheat, cotton, and rice — and to crop insurance premium subsidies that further underwrite commodity production risk.[^2] The distribution is concentrated: Environmental Working Group analyses of USDA subsidy data have consistently shown that the top ten percent of subsidy recipients receive the majority of payments, that the payments flow disproportionately to the largest operations, and that the crops subsidized are overwhelmingly the commodity inputs to the industrial food system the previous chapters have scored.[^3]

The consequence of this structure for the food system the book has described is direct. When commodity corn is subsidized below the cost of production, the feedlot system that depends on cheap corn is effectively subsidized as well. The Tufts analysis documented roughly $35 billion in implicit savings to the industrial livestock sector from below-cost feed between 1997 and 2005, and the structural dynamic has not fundamentally changed since.[^4] When crop insurance subsidies reduce the financial risk of planting commodity crops on marginal land, the incentive to convert that land to other uses (perennial pasture, agroforestry, rewilding) is suppressed. When conservation payments through the Conservation Reserve Program and its successors pay landowners to take land out of production but do not create pathways for that land to be managed under the kinds of ecological restoration the book has described, the policy creates a binary — produce commodities or produce nothing — that forecloses the middle option of producing food from ecologically managed landscapes.

Restructuring the subsidy system toward ecological outcomes would change the economics of food production in the United States in ways that the current system's beneficiaries have understandable reasons to resist. The restructuring would mean, concretely, that federal agricultural spending would be redirected, partially or substantially, from commodity price supports and crop insurance premium subsidies toward payments for ecosystem services: soil carbon accumulation, biodiversity outcomes, water quality improvements, and the maintenance of ecological function on working landscapes. The concept is not novel. The Conservation Stewardship Program already makes payments for conservation practices on working land, and the 2018 farm bill expanded some of these provisions. What does not yet exist is a subsidy architecture in which the ecological outcomes the book's framework measures, including survivability and the restoration of species-landscape relationships, are weighted as heavily in the allocation of federal agricultural spending as commodity production volumes currently are.

Julie Witcover and colleagues, in a 2019 analysis for the UC Davis Policy Institute for Energy, Environment, and the Economy, examined the potential effects of redirecting a fraction of federal commodity subsidies toward ecosystem-service payments and found that even modest redirections could produce measurable shifts in land-use patterns, particularly on marginal cropland where the return to commodity production is lowest and the ecological return to alternative management is highest.[^5] The finding is intuitive: if the subsidy system pays you to grow corn on land that is marginal for corn, you grow corn; if the subsidy system pays you to manage that land for ecological outcomes, you manage for ecological outcomes. The incentive structure determines the land use. Changing the incentive structure changes the land use. The policy question is not whether this mechanism works. It is whether the political conditions exist to redirect the incentive.

I am not confident they do. Farm bill politics in the United States are among the most entrenched in the federal legislative process. The commodity support programs have institutional constituencies in the agricultural committees of Congress, in the commodity organizations, in the agribusiness firms that supply inputs to commodity producers, and in the rural communities whose economies have been organized around commodity production for generations. Those constituencies have successfully defended the basic architecture of the subsidy system through more than a dozen farm bill reauthorizations since 1973. Structural reform of the subsidy system is not impossible. It has happened before: the 1996 farm bill's elimination of deficiency payments and the subsequent reinstatement of support mechanisms under different names in the 2002 and subsequent bills illustrate both that the system can be restructured and that restructured systems tend to reconverge on the patterns they replaced.[^6] I do not think the political difficulty is a reason to stop talking about the structural need. I do think it is a reason to be honest about the distance between the need and the enactment.

## Conservation Baselines

Chapter 2 of this book argued that the Columbian Curtain, the conservation paradigm that treats 1492 as the ecological baseline, shapes what counts as native, what counts as damage, and what counts as restoration. The argument was analytical. The policy implications are concrete.

Conservation policy in the United States operates, in the main, under a post-Columbian baseline. The Endangered Species Act protects species that are threatened or endangered relative to their post-Columbian range and abundance. The National Park Service manages park ecosystems toward reference conditions derived from the early historical record. The Fish and Wildlife Service's management of wildlife refuges is oriented toward the maintenance of habitat for species present in the post-Columbian fauna. The regulatory architecture, in short, is built on the baseline the book has argued should be reexamined.

Shifting the conservation baseline to include near-time frames would not require abandoning the work that has been done under the post-Columbian paradigm. The bald eagle recovery, the alligator recovery, the wolf reintroduction in the Northern Rockies: all of these are genuine conservation achievements accomplished under existing baselines, and none of them would be undermined by an expanded baseline. What the shift would do is create regulatory space for a category of restoration that the current baseline forecloses: the reintroduction of species, or proxies for species, that were present in the near-time fauna but absent from the post-Columbian baseline. Under the current regulatory architecture, a proposal to introduce camels to the American Southwest as proxies for *Camelops* is not merely unusual. It is categorically outside the frame of what conservation policy is designed to evaluate. The camel is not listed under the Endangered Species Act, because it is not a native species under the Act's baseline. It is not a candidate for reintroduction under any existing wildlife management program, because no existing program operates under a baseline that recognizes its lineage's presence on the continent. The regulatory system does not say "no" to the proposal because the proposal has been evaluated and found wanting. The regulatory system does not have a category in which the proposal can be evaluated at all.

Creating that category, a regulatory pathway for the evaluation of near-time proxy reintroductions, would be a structural change of considerable significance. It would not require the approval of any specific reintroduction. It would require only that the regulatory system acknowledge the existence of the question and create a process for answering it: a framework for evaluating proposals, assessing ecological risks, setting monitoring requirements, and establishing the conditions under which a proof-of-concept experiment could proceed. The framework would need to be rigorous. The ecological risk assessments would need to meet or exceed the standards applied to endangered-species reintroductions. The monitoring requirements would need to be sustained and independently evaluated. The bar should be high, because the stakes of getting it wrong (introducing an animal that causes unforeseen ecological damage) are real, as Caro and Rubenstein have argued and as this book has acknowledged.

The institutional model closest to what would be needed is the experimental-population designation under Section 10(j) of the Endangered Species Act, which allows the Fish and Wildlife Service to designate reintroduced populations of listed species as "experimental" and manage them under more flexible regulatory provisions than fully protected populations receive.[^7] The gray wolf reintroductions in Yellowstone and central Idaho were conducted under 10(j) designations, which allowed the managing agencies to address the legitimate concerns of livestock operators and adjacent landowners through management flexibility that full ESA protection would not have permitted. The mechanism is not directly applicable to proxy-species introductions, since a camel is not a listed species, but the conceptual architecture is relevant: a regulatory pathway that acknowledges the experimental nature of the introduction, provides for rigorous monitoring, establishes conditions for expansion or termination based on empirical results, and creates a legal framework within which the experiment can proceed without being foreclosed by categorical exclusion.

Jens-Christian Svenning and colleagues, in their 2016 synthesis on trophic rewilding in *PNAS*, argued that the scientific case for expanding conservation baselines to include Pleistocene-era ecological assemblages has strengthened substantially over the last decade, and that the institutional frameworks governing conservation practice have not kept pace with the science.[^8] The gap between the scientific literature and the regulatory architecture is not unique to this issue. Regulatory frameworks routinely lag behind the science they are meant to implement. But the gap is particularly consequential here, because it forecloses an entire category of restoration work that the science has identified as worth pursuing.

## Further Structural Changes

Three additional areas of structural change bear noting, each briefer than the subsidy and baseline questions but each a real barrier to the vision the book has described.

The first is land-use regulation. Current agricultural zoning and conservation-easement structures assume a binary: commodity production or conservation set-aside. Ecologically managed food production — the Knepp model, the bison operations, the proxy-browsing experiments — is a third thing, and the regulatory apparatus does not have a well-developed framework for it. The Conservation Reserve Program pays landowners to take land out of production and maintain vegetative cover, but CRP contracts generally restrict the commercial use of enrolled acreage.[^9] The result is a policy binary that forecloses the option of producing food from an ecologically managed landscape. What would need to change is the creation of a third land-use classification with its own ecological-outcome standards, its own monitoring requirements, and its own eligibility for public support. The classification would need to be rigorous enough to prevent greenwashing and flexible enough to accommodate the diversity of landscapes the book has described. State-level habitat exchange programs for greater sage-grouse offer institutional models that could be adapted.[^10]

The second is regulatory space for proxy-species experiments. The proof-of-concept work the book has called for faces a permitting landscape that was not built with it in mind. A dromedary camel on Arizona rangeland under a university research protocol might need permits from the state department of agriculture, the state game and fish department, and the county — each operating under a different statutory framework, none designed for ecologically motivated species introductions. The friction is not deliberate. It is a side effect of regulatory architecture built for different purposes. What is needed is a streamlined pathway that integrates federal, state, and local authority into a single review process with clear criteria and defined timelines, modeled on the ESA's Section 10(j) experimental-population designation, which enabled the Yellowstone wolf reintroductions and the California condor recovery.[^7] The institutional capacity to manage large-bodied animal introductions on working landscapes exists. It needs to be adapted to a new category of question.

The third is fisheries management. Here the institutional infrastructure is, in some respects, further along than the terrestrial equivalent. The Magnuson-Stevens Act provides the statutory framework, the regional fishery management councils provide governance, and Hilborn's stock-assessment methodology provides the scientific tools. The Alaska salmon fisheries are a working example of sustainable wild-harvest food production at commercial scale.[^11] What would need to change is the extension of this model to marine restoration — kelp forests, oyster reefs, coastal wetlands — under regulatory frameworks that treat restored marine ecosystems as productive systems rather than merely as conservation amenities, and that treat the food-production value of restoration as part of the justification for public investment.[^12]

## What Would Have to Be True

I have described five areas of structural change: subsidy reform, conservation-baseline expansion, land-use regulatory modernization, regulatory pathways for proxy-species experiments, and the extension and strengthening of science-based fisheries management. Each is individually difficult. Together they constitute a reorientation of the policy framework governing food production and land management in the United States, a reorientation that is, by any honest assessment, politically formidable.

I want to name what would have to be true for these changes to become possible, because naming the preconditions honestly is more useful than pretending the changes are around the corner.

There would have to be a political constituency for ecological food-system reform that is large enough and organized enough to influence farm bill deliberations. Farm bill politics are currently dominated by the commodity agriculture constituency, the nutrition-assistance constituency (SNAP and related programs account for the majority of farm bill spending), and the conservation constituency, which has achieved meaningful but incremental gains over successive reauthorizations. A constituency for the kind of structural reform this chapter describes — one that includes producers operating outside the commodity system, conservation organizations working on near-time baselines, Indigenous nations managing land under traditional and restored practices, and consumers who have adopted some version of the framework this book proposes — does not currently exist as a coordinated political force. It exists in fragments. The fragments have not yet found each other.

There would have to be a sustained research program generating the empirical evidence that policy change requires. The proof-of-concept experiments the book has called for are not just interesting ecological questions. They are the evidentiary foundation without which no policy advocate can credibly argue for regulatory change. A policymaker being asked to create a permitting pathway for proxy-species experiments will ask what the evidence shows. If the evidence does not yet exist because the experiments have not been conducted, the argument stalls. The research comes first. The policy follows the research.

There would have to be an honest reckoning with the distributional consequences of subsidy reform. Redirecting federal agricultural spending from commodity support to ecosystem-service payments would produce winners and losers, and the losers would include producers and communities whose livelihoods are organized around the current commodity system. A reform that does not account for the transition costs — that does not provide pathways for commodity producers to shift toward ecological management, that does not invest in the rural infrastructure and retraining that the transition would require — is a reform that imposes its costs on the people least able to absorb them. The people most affected by subsidy reform are not the agribusiness corporations that capture the largest share of the payments. They are the family-scale commodity producers and the rural communities whose economies have been organized around commodity production for generations. A just transition would need to address their interests, and addressing their interests would need to be part of the reform's design rather than an afterthought.

There would have to be, finally, a cultural shift in how Americans think about the land they live on: a shift from treating the post-Columbian landscape as the default ecological condition to recognizing it as one state in a longer ecological history. This is the shift the Columbian Curtain argument was designed to support. It is not a shift that a book or a policy proposal can produce on its own. It is a shift that happens, if it happens, through the accumulation of evidence, education, and experience: through people visiting rewilded landscapes and seeing what they look like, through students learning near-time ecology alongside contemporary ecology, through the slow accretion of a different ecological imagination. I do not know how long this takes. I know it has not happened yet.

> **FIELD NOTE: The Minimum Viable Flip**
>
> The structural changes this chapter has named are individually difficult and collectively formidable. None of them is a precondition for running the smallest version of the experiment. The MVP, to borrow a frame from outside ecology, is what Eric Reis, writing about startups rather than ecosystems, called a minimum viable product: not the cheapest version of the project, but the smallest version that can be falsified.
>
> A hundred camels in an osage orange orchard is the long-tail outcome, not the MVP. The MVP is something more like: forty acres of distressed basin-and-range ground, six to ten camels (or feral burros, or whatever combination of available proxies for what the fossil record says belongs there can actually be permitted), a small planting of a co-evolved tree species, and a monitoring protocol that captures soil carbon, water infiltration, plant diversity, and animal body condition at fixed intervals over three to five years. Bounded. Instrumented. Designed to fail informatively if it fails. The point is not to prove the orchard hypothesis — the MVP cannot do that. The point is to generate the first real signal where signal currently does not exist, at a cost low enough that the permitting pathway and the funding architecture can be tested against a concrete proposal rather than a theoretical one.
>
> The case for running the MVP rests on a separate argument this book has been making in different vocabulary throughout. The current food system is fragile in the sense Chapter Four named: optimized for stable conditions and brittle when those conditions change. The dominant agricultural modality is engineered toward an equilibrium of stable rainfall, stable energy prices, stable trade lanes, stable phosphate, stable nitrogen, stable insect ecology, stable microbial ecology. The engineering succeeds only as long as the equilibrium holds. The Dust Bowl was a fragility cascade. The Irish Potato Famine was a fragility cascade. Panama disease in bananas, late blight, foot-and-mouth, BSE, the broiler flock culls of the last decade — all fragility cascades. Each one was solved by reaching for more of the input the system already depended on, which deepened the specialization, which set up the next cascade.
>
> A keystone-driven, ecologically diverse system would not be immune to disturbance. It would be the opposite of fragile to it. Fire, drought, herd movement, mass mortality — these are not anomalies in a Pleistocene-functional landscape. They are the conditions the landscape is calibrated to. Take the disturbance away and the system degrades. Put it back and the system reorganizes.
>
> Intellectual honesty asks the policy framework to plan for both worlds. The one in which the dominant agricultural modality keeps refining itself, throws ever-better solutions at its dependencies, and reaches the better version of what it already is. That world is worth planning for. So is the other one. If the input chains tighten, if the climate signal shifts past what the cultivars can tolerate, if a microbial event takes out a single-genome crop the way late blight took out the lumper potato, the contingency is not "find a cleverer monoculture." The contingency is "have the alternative system already running, somewhere, at any scale, with enough data that it can be expanded."
>
> That is the case for funding the MVP even if the dominant system is fine. Diversity of crops is what survivability looks like at the field level. Diversity of approaches is what survivability looks like at the system level. We did not learn nothing from the Dust Bowl. We learned, at minimum, that running every farm by the same playbook is what makes a Dust Bowl possible. The same logic applies one level up. The MVP is a way of buying optionality before the option needs to be exercised.

## Closing

This book has made its argument. It has proposed a framework, applied it, followed it into territory the food-systems conversation has mostly not visited, and arrived at a set of conclusions I believe the evidence supports. The framework identifies structural vulnerabilities in the dominant food system. The fossil record identifies a direction for restoration that most of the sustainability literature has not considered. The existing proof-of-concept work — the bison operations, the feral hog harvests, the intact fisheries, the Knepp restoration — demonstrates that rewilded food production is not an abstraction. The serious critics have been engaged, and the responses to their critiques are, I think, defensible.

What the book cannot do is enact what it proposes. The distance between an argument and its realization is not a distance the argument itself can cross. The structural changes this chapter has described are preconditions for the book's vision to scale beyond the readers who adopt the framework individually. Those changes are politically difficult, institutionally complex, and dependent on conditions — a political constituency, an evidentiary base, a cultural shift — that do not yet fully exist.

I am not ending with certainty about outcomes, because certainty about political outcomes is not something the evidence supports, and the book's commitment to stating what it can defend requires applying that standard here as well. I am ending with a description of the door. The door is the set of structural conditions under which the experiments could be tried, the baselines could be expanded, the subsidy system could be restructured, and the food system could begin to move in the direction the framework points. The door exists. It is not open. Whether it opens depends on work that this book can contribute to but cannot complete.

The epilogue returns to the orchard. What remains after the argument, after the framework, after the policy, is the question the prologue already asked: is the experiment worth trying?

The book has laid out what it would take. The answer to whether or not the experiment is worth trying is not the book's to give.

It is the reader's.
