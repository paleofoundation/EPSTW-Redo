# Chapter 9: Proxies and Critics

In 2005, Josh Donlan and twelve co-authors published a one-page essay in *Nature* titled "Re-wilding North America."[^1] The essay proposed, in compressed form, what the previous three chapters of this book have been working toward at length: that the ecological histories of the Americas make the case for restoring large vertebrates, including Old World proxies for extinct Pleistocene species, to landscapes that had co-evolved with them. Donlan and his co-signatories included Paul Martin, Michael Soulé, Dave Foreman, and several prominent conservation biologists. The essay was short, ambitious, and deliberately provocative. It ended with a call for an "optimistic agenda for twenty-first century conservation" centered on the phased introduction of large mammals to carefully selected North American sites.

The response was substantial and, in large part, skeptical.

Within a year, multiple peer-reviewed rebuttals had appeared. The critiques ranged from the procedural to the ecological to the political, made by serious scholars whose concerns were grounded in real knowledge of conservation biology, invasion ecology, and the practical realities of land management. The critiques deserve to be engaged, not summarized, not caricatured, not dismissed. This chapter's job is to present the strongest version of each major critique, engage it on its merits, and arrive at a position the reader can evaluate for themselves.

I want to say at the outset what this chapter does not do. It does not decry the critics. The scholars who questioned the Donlan proposal were doing what scholars are supposed to do: subjecting an ambitious claim to rigorous scrutiny and identifying where the claim outran its evidence. Some objections I accept substantially. Some I think are answerable on empirical grounds. Some miss the larger point, for reasons I will try to articulate. In no case do I think the objections are trivial, and in no case do I think the person making them was wrong. The rewilding proposal asks for much. The scrutiny is proportionate to the ask.

## The Proxy-Equivalence Problem

The most ecologically grounded critique of Pleistocene rewilding was articulated by Tim Caro in a 2007 commentary in *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* titled "The Pleistocene Re-wilding Gambit."[^2] Caro is a behavioral ecologist whose career has been built on careful empirical work — mostly on African mammalian ecology, where the large-bodied fauna that the rewilding proposal seeks to restore or proxy is still intact. His critique cuts to the heart of the proxy-selection argument this book has been making.

Caro's central concern is what might be called the proxy-equivalence assumption: that a closely related living species will perform the same ecological function as its extinct relative when placed in the same landscape. The rewilding proposal depends on this assumption. Donlan and colleagues proposed that African or Asian elephants could serve as proxies for mammoths, African lions or cheetahs as proxies for the extinct American lion and cheetah, and Bactrian camels or dromedaries as proxies for *Camelops*. The assumption underlying each proposal is that phylogenetic proximity — the degree of relatedness between the proxy and the extinct species — predicts ecological equivalence. A close relative, the assumption holds, will occupy a similar niche, exert similar effects on vegetation and soil, and restore something like the ecological function the extinct species performed.

Caro argued the assumption is unsupported by available evidence. Ecological function is not a simple derivative of phylogenetic relatedness. Two closely related species can occupy very different niches if they have diverged in diet, behavior, habitat preference, or body size. An African elephant is not a mammoth. The lineages diverged millions of years ago and adapted to different climatic regimes, vegetation communities, and predator assemblages. Placing an African elephant on the Great Plains and expecting it to perform the ecological role of *Mammuthus columbi* is an extrapolation from phylogeny to ecology that available evidence does not license. The same concern applies to every proxy pairing the Donlan proposal advanced. A modern dromedary is not *Camelops*. A modern African cheetah is not *Miracinonyx*. The degree to which proxy actual behavior in the actual landscape would replicate the extinct species' ecological function is an empirical question that cannot be answered from phylogeny alone.[^2]

Caro went further. He pointed out that introductions of large-bodied species into novel ecosystems have, historically, produced ecological consequences that introducing parties did not anticipate and could not control. The history of species introductions is not reassuring. Cane toads in Australia, rabbits in Australia, Nile perch in Lake Victoria—each was introduced with some expectation of ecological benefit, and each produced cascading damage still being managed decades or centuries later. The proxy-species proposal, Caro argued, was asking conservationists to introduce large-bodied animals with enormous individual ecological footprints, capable of transforming vegetation structure and soil dynamics across large areas, into ecosystems that have not experienced those animals for twelve thousand years. The ecosystems have reorganized in the interim. The vegetation communities have shifted. The predator-prey dynamics have stabilized around a different cast of species. Introducing a proxy megaherbivore into a system operating without megaherbivores for a hundred and twenty centuries is not a restoration in any straightforward sense. It is an experiment with uncertain outcome, and the scale of potential consequences, both positive and negative, is proportional to the size of animals being introduced.

I find this critique serious and, in its strongest form, largely correct. The proxy-equivalence assumption *is* unsupported as a blanket proposition—phylogenetic proximity does not guarantee ecological equivalence. The history of species introductions warrants caution. Caro is right that the burden of evidence lies with proponents, not skeptics, and that evidence as of 2007 was not sufficient to justify continental-scale proxy introductions on phylogenetic reasoning alone.

Where the critique reaches its limit is in what follows from it. Caro's argument establishes that the proxy-equivalence question is empirical—that it cannot be assumed but must be tested. It does not establish that the question is unanswerable, or that the answer will necessarily be negative—it establishes only that the empirical work remains undone. The proposition that a dromedary camel would browse the thorny vegetation of the American Southwest in a manner ecologically similar to *Camelops* is not a proposition that can be confirmed or denied from phylogeny. It is a proposition that can be tested by putting dromedaries on Southwest rangeland and measuring what happens to vegetation, soil, the animal, and the surrounding ecological community. The test would need to be designed carefully, monitored rigorously, and conducted at a scale small enough that negative outcomes could be managed without ecological catastrophe. It would need control sites and long time horizons, the kind of sustained empirical attention that conservation biology gives to reintroduction programs for endangered species. What it would not need is certainty in advance. It would need a hypothesis worth testing and a methodology capable of testing it. And people with the nerve to actually do it.

The proxy-equivalence question is a question about experimental design rather than about ecological principle. The principle that an ecosystem missing its keystone herbivores functions differently than one in which they are present is well established in the ecological literature and has been demonstrated empirically in multiple contexts. The question is whether a specific proxy species, in a specific landscape, restores a specific ecological function to a measurable degree. That question is answerable by experiment, not by argument. The fact that the answer is not yet known is an argument for conducting the experiment, not for declining to conduct it.

Donlan and colleagues, in their fuller 2006 treatment in *The American Naturalist*, anticipated this line of response.[^3] They proposed a phased approach: beginning with species that are already present in North America in managed or feral populations (horses, burros, camels in small numbers), moving to species with closer ecological parallels to extinct fauna (bolson tortoises, which have since been the subject of a small-scale reintroduction program), and proceeding to larger introductions only as evidence accumulated from earlier phases. The phased approach directly responds to the proxy-equivalence concern: rather than assuming equivalence and introducing at scale, it proposes testing equivalence at small scale and scaling only what the evidence supports.

The phased approach is the right framework. It also requires honesty about what it can and cannot deliver. A small-scale experiment with dromedaries on a single Arizona ranch would tell us something about dromedary browsing behavior on Sonoran vegetation. It would not tell us what a self-sustaining population of dromedaries would do to the landscape over decades. The experimental evidence accumulates incrementally, and the increments may not resolve the proxy-equivalence question as cleanly as either side would like. What the experiments would produce is data: specific, empirical, measurable data about the interaction between proxy species and the landscapes they are being considered for. The current state of the conversation, in which advocates assert equivalence and critics deny it on the basis of argument rather than evidence, is less useful than a conversation in which both sides work from data generated by carefully designed field experiments.

## The Political and Cultural Obstacle

The second major critique came from Daniel Rubenstein and colleagues, most forcefully in a 2006 paper in *Biological Conservation* titled "Pleistocene Park: Does Re-wilding North America Represent Sound Conservation for the 21st Century?"[^4] Rubenstein, a behavioral ecologist at Princeton whose work on equid ecology is among the most cited in the field, brought different concerns than Caro had raised. Where Caro focused on the ecological risks of proxy introductions, Rubenstein focused on the practical, political, and cultural barriers.

The Rubenstein critique is, in essence, a feasibility argument. Suppose the ecology works. Suppose the proxy-equivalence question, when tested, yields favorable results for at least some species-landscape pairings. The proposal still requires land, regulatory approval, and the consent of communities living adjacent to the introduced animals, or at least their tolerance. It requires a political and cultural environment in which the introduction of large, potentially dangerous animals onto working landscapes is regarded as an acceptable use of public and private land. Rubenstein and colleagues argued that none of these conditions obtained in the United States of the early twenty-first century, and that the distance between the ecological vision and the political reality was large enough to make the proposal practically unworkable.

The argument was specific. Americans do not, on the whole, want large predators near their homes, livestock, or children. The political conflict over wolf reintroduction in the Northern Rockies, which had been ongoing for a decade at the time of the Rubenstein paper, illustrated the scale of resistance that even a single native predator species could generate. Extending the reintroduction concept to African lions, elephants, or cheetahs, the authors suggested, was to move from a politically difficult proposal to a politically impossible one. The same reasoning applied to megaherbivores. A free-ranging elephant on the Great Plains would be an extraordinary management challenge: property damage, liability exposure, risk to human safety, conflict with existing agricultural land use. None of these concerns were ecological. All were practical, and all were, in the judgment of the critics, sufficient to render the proposal dead on arrival in any real political context.

Rubenstein and Rubenstein returned to this line of argument a decade later, in a 2016 letter to *PNAS* prompted by Svenning and colleagues' synthesis on trophic rewilding.[^5] The updated critique noted that the intervening decade had not produced the political or cultural conditions necessary for the kind of introductions the Donlan proposal envisioned, and that the conversation about rewilding had largely shifted from Pleistocene-proxy introductions toward trophic rewilding, a framework that emphasized the restoration of ecological processes (top-down regulation, herbivory, seed dispersal) rather than the reintroduction of specific Pleistocene analogs. The shift, they suggested, itself acknowledged that the original proposal's ambitions exceeded its political reach.

I accept this critique in significant part. The cultural and political obstacles to large-scale megafaunal proxy introductions in the United States are real, substantial, and not likely to be resolved by ecological argument alone. A proposal that requires Americans to accept free-ranging elephants on the Great Plains is not a proposal that will be enacted in any political environment I can foresee. The obstacles are not irrational—they are the reasonable concerns of people who live on working landscapes and have legitimate interests in what lives on those landscapes alongside them. The rewilding conversation cannot proceed as though those interests do not exist or as though they will yield to the force of ecological reasoning.

Where the critique warrants a response, and here the response is more modest than the original Donlan proposal, is in the scope of what is being proposed. The political obstacles that Rubenstein identifies are real for the proposal as Donlan et al. stated it in 2005: a continental-scale reintroduction program involving African megafauna. The political obstacles are substantially different for a more modest version: small-scale proof-of-concept experiments, conducted on private or willing public land, using species that are already present in the Americas, at scales small enough that the management challenges are manageable and the risks are bounded.

Consider what a modest proof-of-concept program would actually look like. A rancher in southern Arizona, one of many whose land is degraded enough and whose relationship to the conventional cattle economy is marginal enough to be open to alternatives, places twenty dromedary camels on a thousand acres of Sonoran desert rangeland under a research protocol developed in partnership with a university. The camels are fenced. The vegetation response is monitored. The soil biology is sampled. The animal behavior is documented. The experiment runs for five years. At the end of five years, the data either support the hypothesis that dromedary browsing produces measurable ecological benefit on that landscape, or they do not. If they do, the experiment is expanded to additional sites, species, and scales. If they do not, the experiment is ended and the hypothesis is revised.

The political barriers to this version of the proposal are not zero. They include permitting, liability, neighbor relations, and the cultural unfamiliarity of camels on Arizona rangeland. But they are barriers of a different order than those to placing elephants on the Great Plains. They are navigable, and in fact are already being navigated in analogous contexts: Bolson tortoise reintroduction in New Mexico, experimental bison management on tribal and private land across the northern Plains, and the ongoing management of feral horse and burro populations on public land in the West. Each of these involves the placement of large-bodied animals on working landscapes under regulatory frameworks that already exist.

The political critique is strongest when aimed at the most ambitious version of the proposal. It is weakest when aimed at the most modest version. The distance between the two versions is not small. One asks a nation to accept a fundamentally different relationship to its landscapes. The other asks a handful of willing landowners and researchers to run a bounded experiment and publish the results. The book's ask is the modest version: not a continental program but a set of experiments, not certainty but data, not political transformation but the kind of incremental, evidence-based expansion that conservation biology already practices with endangered-species reintroduction programs.

## The Baseline-as-Fantasy Critique

The third critique cuts deeper because it challenges not a specific proposal but the frame within which all rewilding proposals are made.

The argument, advanced by various scholars with different emphases, is that the Pleistocene baseline is itself a kind of conservation fantasy, a longing for a world that existed under conditions so different from the present that its restoration is not merely difficult but conceptually incoherent. Conservation, under this view, should focus on protecting what currently exists: the species, ecosystems, and ecological processes that are present now, under current conditions, and that face measurable threats from current human activity. The late-Pleistocene assemblage is interesting as paleontology, not a useful reference point for conservation in the Anthropocene. The world has moved on. The ecosystems have reorganized. The question is not how to get back to a Pleistocene state that no living person has experienced but how to protect and improve the ecological communities we actually have.

This is not a fringe position. Versions of it appear in thoughtful conservation writing across the last two decades, and the version embedded in the trophic-rewilding framework that Svenning and colleagues articulated in their 2016 synthesis in *PNAS* is, in part, a response to the force of the objection.[^6] Svenning and colleagues shifted emphasis from "restore the Pleistocene assemblage" to "restore the ecological processes that large-bodied animals perform," a shift that sidesteps the historical-baseline question by making the argument functional rather than historical. If a landscape needs top-down regulation by large predators, the relevant question is whether large predators can provide that function now, not whether the specific predators being considered are phylogenetically correct proxies for Pleistocene species.

I respect the pragmatism of the trophic-rewilding reframe. It is a useful bridge between the Donlan proposal's ambitions and the political and ecological realities the proposal confronted. Where the baseline-as-fantasy critique ultimately falls short is in what it asks us to accept.

The argument that conservation should focus on protecting what currently exists takes the current ecological state as its reference point. But the current ecological state of North America, as Chapter 2 of this book argued at length, is not a stable equilibrium that happens to have arrived at its present form through natural processes—it is a depleted state, one that resulted from the loss of more than forty species of large mammals in near time, a loss that reshaped every terrestrial ecosystem on the continent. The vegetation communities are different. The soil dynamics are different. The predator-prey relationships are truncated. The seed dispersal networks are broken. The grasslands that evolved under proboscidean herbivory operate differently without it. The canyons that evolved under camelid browsing are being colonized by thorny vegetation the camelids used to suppress. The pronghorn still runs at speeds calibrated to a predator that no longer exists.

To argue that conservation should protect "what currently exists" is to argue that we should protect the depleted state, that we should defend the post-extinction baseline against further degradation without asking whether the baseline itself represents a condition worth defending on ecological grounds. It is the argument from Chapter 2 returning in a different register: the Columbian Curtain, extended. Except that here the curtain is not drawn at 1492. It is drawn at the present moment, and what it hides is the same thing the Columbian Curtain hides: the depth of the loss and the possibility that addressing the loss would produce ecological outcomes that the current baseline cannot.

Granted, the comparison has limits. The Pleistocene is gone in a way that 1491 is not. The ecosystems have had twelve thousand years to reorganize, and the reorganization is not nothing. New ecological relationships have formed, new equilibria have been reached, and disrupting those equilibria carries its own risks. Caro's concern about the consequences of introducing megafauna into systems that have stabilized without them is a version of this point, and it is a legitimate one. The twelve-thousand-year gap is real. The ecosystems on the other side of it are not frozen in time, waiting for the return of the animals they lost. They have moved, and the moving is itself an ecological fact.

But the moving has not been unconstrained. The ecosystems have reorganized within the limits of what was available to them—and what was available was a diminished cast of species. A grassland that loses its proboscideans does not replace them with an ecological equivalent. It reorganizes around the absence: vegetation structure shifts, nutrient cycling changes, fire regime adjusts, soil biology adapts to a world with less large-mammal disturbance. The reorganization is real, but it is a reorganization around a loss, not a recovery from one. The new equilibrium is a depleted equilibrium. To take the depleted equilibrium as the baseline and to argue that conservation should protect it against disturbance is to naturalize the depletion, to treat the consequence of a mass extinction as the reference condition for all subsequent ecological management.

I do not think this is a defensible position when stated plainly. The fact that it is the de facto position of most conservation practice in the United States does not make it defensible—it makes it familiar.

The response is not to demand that every conservation agency immediately adopt a Pleistocene baseline—that would be as politically unrealistic as the most ambitious version of the Donlan proposal, and for similar reasons. The response is to recognize that the current baseline is contingent, that it results from a specific historical event (the late-Pleistocene megafaunal extinction), that it represents a depleted state rather than a natural one, and that proposals to address the depletion deserve evaluation on their ecological merits rather than dismissal on the grounds that the baseline is the baseline.

## What the Wolf Taught Us

There is one case in which the rewilding conversation moved from argument to evidence, and the evidence is worth pausing on, because it is the closest thing the field has to a completed experiment.

In 1995 and 1996, gray wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone National Park after an absence of approximately seventy years. The reintroduction was politically contentious: ranching communities adjacent to the park opposed it vigorously, and the legal and regulatory battles surrounding it lasted years before and after the animals arrived. The wolves were not Pleistocene proxies. They were a species reintroduced to a landscape it had occupied within living human memory, under a regulatory framework that already existed for endangered-species recovery. It matters here not as a Pleistocene-rewilding experiment but as something simpler: a large-bodied animal returned to a landscape that had reorganized in its absence, with the reorganization measured before, during, and after the return.

What happened was substantial. William Ripple and Robert Beschta, in a 2012 synthesis covering the first fifteen years of wolf presence in the park, documented a series of measurable ecological changes that cascaded from the reintroduction through the park's trophic structure.[^7] Elk, which had been the park's dominant herbivore in the wolves' absence, altered their behavior and distribution in response to predation risk. Riparian areas that had been heavily browsed by elk—willows, aspens, and cottonwoods along streams and rivers—began to recover as elk browsing pressure declined. Luke Painter and colleagues, in a 2015 study in *Ecology*, documented that the proportion of young aspen browsed by elk in northern Yellowstone dropped from ninety percent in 1998 to thirty-seven to sixty-three percent by 2012, with corresponding increases in aspen recruitment and height growth in areas of high wolf activity.[^8] The vegetation recovery had downstream effects: stream-bank stabilization improved as willow and cottonwood root systems reestablished, channel morphology began to shift, and beaver — absent from much of the park's northern range for decades — returned to streams where the recovering riparian vegetation provided both food and building material.

The trophic cascade was not simple. Creel and Christianson, in a 2009 study also in *Ecology*, found that individual elk willow consumption actually increased in the presence of wolves, suggesting that vegetation recovery was driven primarily by elk population reduction rather than by behavioral avoidance alone.[^9] The mechanism was more complex than the initial "landscape of fear" narrative suggested. What was not in dispute was the outcome: the reintroduction of a top predator to a landscape that had reorganized in its absence produced measurable, cascading, and largely beneficial ecological effects within two decades.

The Yellowstone wolf case is not a perfect analogy for the proxy-species proposals this book has discussed. Wolves are a native species reintroduced to a landscape from which they had been absent for decades, not centuries or millennia. The ecological continuity between the pre-removal and post-reintroduction periods is much tighter than the continuity between the late Pleistocene and the present. The political and regulatory framework for wolf reintroduction, while contested, was grounded in the Endangered Species Act, a legal infrastructure that does not exist for Pleistocene-proxy introductions.

What the wolf case does demonstrate—and this is what I want us to pause on before moving to the chapter's close—is that the principle works. An ecosystem missing a keystone species reorganizes around the absence. When the species is returned, the ecosystem begins to reorganize around the presence. The reorganization is measurable, cascading, and ecologically significant. The fact that the wolf was absent for seventy years rather than twelve thousand does not change the principle; it changes the degree of uncertainty about the outcome. A twelve-thousand-year absence produces more reorganization, more ecological distance between the pre-removal and the present state, and more uncertainty about what reintroduction would produce. The uncertainty is real. It is not a reason to decline the experiment but a reason to design the experiment carefully.

## The Ask

The book has now made its case across eight chapters. It has argued that the dominant framework for evaluating food systems is incomplete. It has proposed a fourth axis — survivability — that captures what the existing framework misses. It has applied the framework to industrial systems, to regenerative agriculture, and to the rewilded food systems that score best across all four axes. It has taken the fossil record seriously as a guide to which animals belong in which landscapes. It has described what rewilded food systems look like where they already exist, and what they could look like with deliberate effort.

This chapter has taken the critics seriously. Caro's concern about proxy equivalence is valid: the assumption that phylogenetic proximity equals ecological equivalence is unsupported as a blanket claim and should be tested rather than assumed. Rubenstein's concern about political and cultural feasibility is valid: the most ambitious version of the rewilding proposal exceeds what the current political environment can absorb, and the conversation has to meet that reality where it is. The baseline-as-fantasy critique has force: the Pleistocene is gone, the ecosystems have reorganized, and any intervention carries risks that must be weighed against the potential benefits.

What the book asks for, in light of all three critiques, is not certainty—not a continental program, not the introduction of elephants to the Great Plains or lions to the Rockies, not a demand that the conservation establishment abandon its existing frameworks or that ranching communities accept megafauna on their land without consent.

What the book asks for is proof of concept. Rewilding hypotheses can be tested at small scales: camels on a single ranch in Arizona, managed burro populations in the Grand Canyon watershed, bison on tribal and private land across the northern Plains (where this is already happening, with measurable results), wolves in specific Northern Rocky Mountain ecosystems (where this has already been done, with the trophic cascade data to show for it). Each of these is a bounded experiment that produces data and can be expanded if the data are favorable and ended if they are not.

The proof-of-concept approach does not require resolving the proxy-equivalence debate in advance; it generates the data the debate needs. It does not require overcoming the political obstacles to continental-scale megafaunal introduction; it works within the political space that already exists for small-scale research and land-management experimentation. It does not require accepting the Pleistocene baseline as the one true reference point for conservation; it requires only the recognition that the current baseline is contingent, that the depletion it represents is real, and that proposals to address the depletion deserve to be evaluated rather than dismissed.

The experiments are not hypothetical. Some of them have already been run. Wolves in Yellowstone. Bison on tribal and conservation land across the northern Plains. Bolson tortoises in New Mexico. Feral horse and burro management—contested and imperfect, but generating ecological data in real time about what happens when equids occupy landscapes their lineage shaped. Each of these is a data point that says something about the interaction between large-bodied animals and the landscapes they are placed on. The data are not yet sufficient to answer the proxy-equivalence question for every species-landscape pairing the rewilding literature has proposed. They are sufficient to show that the question is answerable, that it yields to empirical investigation rather than resolving only to argument.

The critics have earned the book the obligation to state its ask carefully. The ask is this: try the experiments honestly, at small scales, with rigorous monitoring and genuine willingness to accept negative results. Try them on landscapes where the fossil record and the current ecological evidence both point to an animal that is not there. Measure what happens. Publish the results. Let the data accumulate. And then decide not from argument, not from longing, not from the fossil record alone, but from the evidence of what actually happens when the animals return to the land that shaped them.

The critics say the proposal is uncertain. They are right. The response is not to retreat from the uncertainty but to design experiments that reduce it. The response is not certainty.

The response is science.
