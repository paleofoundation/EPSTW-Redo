The structural shape of the chapter, per the outline:

1. Open with the recognition that Pleistocene rewilding is one frame but not the only one. Indigenous peoples in many regions managed landscapes in ways that look like rewilding-with-people-as-keystone-species rather than rewilding-as-absence-of-people. Engage the Traditional Ecological Knowledge literature seriously.

2. Do not collapse the two frameworks together. They are different:
   - The Pleistocene baseline asks: what existed before the late-Pleistocene losses?
   - The Indigenous management baseline asks: how did the people who lived here for the millennia between the Pleistocene and 1492 manage these landscapes?
   - Both questions are valid. Both produce different answers. Both should inform restoration, with the appropriate framework chosen based on the specific landscape and question.

3. Engage Indigenous management traditions on their own terms, not as illustration of the book's argument. Kimmerer, Johnston, Anderson — these voices represent a parallel intellectual tradition that the Pleistocene framework should be in dialogue with, not in competition with.

4. Second half: water-column rewilding. Kelp forest restoration, oyster reef restoration, intact pelagic fisheries, the difference between farmed salmon and wild Alaskan, the broader question of what marine ecosystems would look like restored to historical baselines. The marine domain has been almost entirely absent from the rewilding popular conversation. The chapter argues it should not be.

5. Close with the integration: two additional frameworks — Indigenous management and marine rewilding — that expand the book's argument beyond the terrestrial Pleistocene frame without contradicting it.

What this chapter does NOT do:

- Does not claim Indigenous management traditions are a subset of Pleistocene rewilding. They are a parallel framework.
- Does not speak for Indigenous peoples. It engages the published literature by Indigenous scholars on their own terms.
- Does not romanticize or flatten Indigenous practices into a monolith.
- Does not dismiss the Pleistocene frame in favor of the Indigenous frame or vice versa.
