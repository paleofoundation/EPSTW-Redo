# Chapter 4: The Fourth Axis

Picture a poultry operation in the Piedmont of Virginia: five hundred acres, rotationally grazed, solar panels on the barn roof, organic certification on the feed. The birds live outdoors in mobile shelters moved daily across perennial pasture. The soil organic matter has climbed two percentage points in a decade. The operation employs eight people from the surrounding county, pays above the local median, and sells direct to consumers within a hundred-mile radius. A journalist doing a sustainability profile would find everything in order. The economic numbers work at the operator's scale. The social indicators — employment, community retention, wage levels — are positive. The environmental metrics are strong: no synthetic pesticide runoff, measurable soil carbon gains, arthropod and avian biodiversity substantially above the row-crop fields across the road. By any standard assessment of sustainability, this is a model operation. It would score well on every axis the standard framework knows how to measure.

Now remove one thing. Not a catastrophe. Just one input. Suppose the commodity corn supply that feeds the birds becomes unavailable. Not forever. For one season. A drought in the Midwest reduces the corn harvest. Grain futures spike. The regional feed mill that supplies the operation cannot fill its orders at a price the farm can absorb without selling birds at a loss. The operation has no on-farm grain production. Its acreage is in perennial pasture, which is why the soil numbers are so good. The birds cannot eat grass. They are monogastrics. They require concentrated energy and protein, and the concentrated energy and protein comes from somewhere else, and the somewhere else has, for one season, failed to deliver.

The operation does not adapt. It does not pivot to an alternative feed source, because at the volumes required there is no alternative feed source available at a survivable price on the timeline the birds' metabolisms demand. It does not switch to a different species, because its infrastructure, its processing relationships, its customer base, and its brand are built around poultry. It does what an operation in this position does: it reduces the flock, absorbs the financial loss, and waits for the commodity market to restabilize. If the disruption lasts one season, the operation survives diminished. If it lasts two, the operation is in financial distress. If it lasts three, the operation that scored so well on every other measure of sustainability is gone.

This is not a hypothetical constructed for rhetorical convenience. Versions of it have played out across the pastured-poultry sector in every year the corn market has spiked significantly: in 2012, when Midwest drought drove corn futures above eight dollars a bushel for the first time; in 2022, when the disruption of Ukrainian grain exports compounded existing supply-chain stress to push US feed costs to levels that several direct-market poultry operations, in interviews and trade-press accounts, described as existential.[^1] The operations that survived those spikes survived them by drawing on reserves, raising prices past what some of their customers would pay, or reducing production. They did not survive them by being sustainable in the sense the word is usually used. They survived them by having enough financial margin to absorb a shock the system had not been designed to withstand.

The gap the example is meant to illustrate is not a gap in the operation. It is a gap in the framework we use to evaluate the operation. The three-axis sustainability model — economic, social, environmental — measured the operation and found it sound. What the model did not measure, because the model was not built to measure it, is what happens to the operation when one of its underlying conditions changes. The model assessed the system's performance under current conditions. It did not ask what the system requires to keep performing, or what happens when those requirements are not met. That question — the question of what the system is riding on and whether the ride continues — is the question this chapter exists to introduce.

## The Three Axes and Their Assumptions

The framework that most sustainability assessments operate within was not handed down as a finished schema. It accumulated. Its current shape — the familiar triad of economic viability, social equity, and environmental integrity — crystallized over roughly two decades of international development and environmental policy conversation, from the Brundtland Commission's 1987 report through the 1992 Rio Earth Summit and into the early-2000s proliferation of corporate sustainability reporting, triple-bottom-line accounting, and the institutional architecture that now supports the field.[^2]

The Brundtland Commission's formulation is worth returning to, because the formulation contains the assumption. The commission defined sustainable development as "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."[^3] The definition is elegant, widely cited, and quietly dependent on a premise it does not examine: that the conditions under which "needs" are being met remain roughly stable over time. The future generations in the Brundtland formulation are imagined as living under conditions recognizably continuous with the present, different in degree, perhaps, but not in kind. The framework asks whether a given system can keep going. It does not ask what happens if the ground the system stands on shifts.

This is not a criticism of the Brundtland Commission or of the researchers and policymakers who built the three-axis framework on its foundation. The framework was built during a period when the premise of continuity was reasonable, or at least more reasonable than it has since become. The late twentieth century was, by historical standards, a period of unusual stability in the global systems that food production depends on: fossil energy was cheap and reliably available, international commodity trade was expanding under relatively stable geopolitical arrangements, the Green Revolution's yield gains were still compounding, and climate variability, while present, had not yet produced the frequency and severity of disruption that the early twenty-first century has delivered. The framework was built for the world it was built in, and in that world its omission was less visible.

The three axes, as they came to be operationalized in sustainability assessment, each do real work. The economic axis asks whether the system generates sufficient returns to persist as an economic entity: whether the farm stays solvent, the business model holds, the value chain from producer to consumer functions at each link. The social axis asks whether the system's benefits and costs are distributed in ways that are equitable or at least defensible: whether workers are treated fairly, whether communities bear disproportionate externalities, whether access to the system's outputs is broadly available. The environmental axis asks whether the system's ecological footprint is within bounds that the surrounding ecology can absorb: whether soil is being built or depleted, whether water is being contaminated or conserved, whether biodiversity is being maintained or eroded, whether greenhouse gas emissions are being managed.

Each of these axes captures something real about a food system's relationship to the world it operates in. The scholarship behind each axis is extensive, the metrics have been refined over decades, and the institutional infrastructure for measuring them (from life-cycle assessment protocols to social-impact auditing to the environmental management standards codified in the ISO 14000 series) is among the most developed bodies of applied assessment methodology in existence.[^4] The framework is not casual. It is not superficial. It is, within its scope, serious and well built.

Consider what the framework produces in practice. Imagine a mid-sized organic dairy cooperative commissioning a sustainability assessment under the Global Reporting Initiative standards, one of the most widely adopted three-axis reporting frameworks in use.[^5] A competent assessment of this kind will document performance across all three axes: the cooperative's member farms are economically viable under the prevailing organic price structure, the social metrics show fair labor practices and positive community economic impact, and the environmental indicators — soil organic matter trends, water quality in adjacent watersheds, biodiversity surveys on member farms — are favorable. A reader encountering the report would conclude, reasonably, that the operation is sustainable. What the report does not document (because the framework it is built on does not ask) is that the cooperative's economic viability depends entirely on the organic price premium, which depends in turn on consumer willingness to pay a substantial markup over conventional milk, which depends in turn on household disposable income remaining stable. The organic dairy sector's recurring price-premium compressions (documented in USDA Economic Research Service reporting on organic milk markets across multiple years of the past two decades) have repeatedly produced the pattern in which farms that scored well on three-axis assessments exited production when the premium narrowed.[^6] The three-axis assessment finds the system sustainable. The system's dependency on a single economic condition (the premium) is not part of the assessment, because the framework does not measure dependencies. It measures performance.

The scope, however, has a boundary, and the boundary is the one the Brundtland premise draws. All three axes describe a system's performance under a given set of operating conditions. The economic axis asks whether the system is profitable *given current input costs, current market structures, current policy arrangements.* The social axis asks whether the system distributes its effects equitably *given current labor markets, current community structures, current regulatory environments.* The environmental axis asks whether the system's ecological footprint is sustainable *given current climate patterns, current hydrological regimes, current soil conditions.* Each axis takes the operating conditions as given and evaluates the system's behavior within them. None of the three asks what happens when the operating conditions themselves change.

This is not an esoteric distinction. It is the distinction between asking "is this building structurally sound?" and asking "is this building structurally sound in an earthquake?" The first question is answerable with one kind of engineering assessment. The second requires a different kind, because it asks about behavior under conditions the building was not specifically designed for. Both questions matter. The first tells you whether the building will stand on a calm day. The second tells you something about what the building is made of: what it depends on, where its tolerances are, what breaks first. A sustainability framework that answers only the first question tells you about calm days. It does not tell you about earthquakes. And the 2020s have been, for food systems, a decade of earthquakes.

## The Decade That Made the Gap Visible

The assumption of stable operating conditions did not break all at once. It frayed across a series of events that, taken individually, could each be absorbed as an anomaly, and taken together, constituted a pattern that the three-axis framework had no native vocabulary to describe.

The COVID-19 pandemic, beginning in early 2020, exposed the fragility of just-in-time supply chains across the US food system in a matter of weeks. Meat processing — concentrated into a small number of very large facilities, each handling volumes that made the facility a single point of failure for its region — shut down or dramatically reduced throughput as infection spread through workforces.[^7] The result was not a gradual adjustment. It was a simultaneous surplus of live animals on farms and shortage of meat in retail outlets, a condition that the supply chain's efficiency had made possible by eliminating the redundancy that would have buffered the shock. At the same time, direct-to-consumer vegetable operations that had built their distribution around restaurant sales and farmers' market attendance lost both channels simultaneously and had to rebuild their customer-facing infrastructure on the fly. The pandemic did not discriminate between conventional and alternative food systems. It discriminated between systems with slack and systems without it, and the decades-long optimization of the US food system for efficiency over redundancy meant that most systems, of whatever kind, had very little slack.

The Texas winter storm of February 2021 added a different lesson. Winter Storm Uri dropped temperatures across the Texas grid to levels that the state's energy infrastructure had not been designed for, producing cascading power failures that lasted days in some areas. Agricultural impacts were immediate and severe: an estimated $600 million in crop losses across the Rio Grande Valley citrus industry, widespread livestock losses in operations without backup power for water and heating systems, and disruption to the cold-chain infrastructure on which the state's export-oriented produce industry depends.[^8] The event was not a food-system crisis in the classic sense. It was an energy-system crisis that became a food-system crisis, because the food system's dependence on continuous energy had not been tested against a disruption of this magnitude. The three-axis framework, applied to the Texas agricultural sector before the storm, would have found operations scoring well on economic, social, and environmental metrics. The storm did not change any of those scores. It revealed that the scores were contingent on a condition (reliable grid power) that the scoring had taken as given.

The disruption of Ukrainian grain exports following Russia's invasion in February 2022 made the dependency structure of global commodity agriculture visible at a scale that domestic disruptions had not. Ukraine and Russia together accounted for roughly twenty-eight percent of global wheat exports and a significant share of corn and barley before the invasion. The blockade of Black Sea ports and the destruction of Ukrainian agricultural infrastructure removed a substantial fraction of global grain supply from the market in a matter of months.[^9] The price effects rippled through every system that purchased commodity grain as an input. In the United States, feed costs for livestock operations (including the pastured-poultry operations whose vulnerability the opening example described) rose sharply. In North Africa and the Middle East, where dependence on Ukrainian and Russian wheat imports was highest, the price spikes contributed to food-security crises that the affected governments had limited capacity to buffer. The event did not cause a global famine. It demonstrated that the global commodity system on which most food production depends is a single interconnected substrate, and that a disruption at one node propagates to every node that draws on the same supply.

Climate volatility has compounded the pattern. The 2012 US drought — still, at the time of writing, among the most severe and extensive single-year droughts in the modern record of the US Corn Belt — reduced the national corn yield by twenty-seven percent and pushed commodity prices to levels that imposed real financial stress on every operation in the country that purchased corn as a feed input.[^10] The drought was not unprecedented in a climatic sense; comparable events appear in the paleoclimate record and in the instrumental record of the twentieth century. What was new was the degree to which the US food system had been optimized around the assumption of a reliable corn harvest. The system had removed the buffers — the stored reserves, the regional crop diversity, the on-farm feed production — that would have absorbed the shock in an earlier era. The three-axis framework, evaluating the system before the drought, would not have flagged this removal as a sustainability concern. The removal improved efficiency. Efficiency, under stable conditions, looks like a strength. Under unstable conditions, it is the mechanism of transmission.

I do not want to make the 2020s sound unique in kind, because disruptions to food systems are not new. What is new — or newly visible — is the frequency and the compounding. Droughts, energy failures, pandemics, and geopolitical disruptions have always occurred. What has changed is that the food system's optimization for efficiency under stable conditions has simultaneously increased its vulnerability to disruption and reduced its capacity to absorb disruption when it occurs. The system has become better at performing under the conditions it was designed for and worse at surviving conditions it was not designed for. The three-axis framework captures the first quality. It does not capture the second.

The compounding is worth pausing on, because it is the feature that separates the current moment from earlier disruption episodes. In 2022, a pastured-poultry operator in the mid-Atlantic faced not one of these disruptions but several simultaneously: feed costs elevated by the Ukrainian grain-export disruption, diesel costs elevated by the same geopolitical event, labor costs elevated by the lingering effects of pandemic-era workforce contraction, and a customer base whose own purchasing power had been reduced by the inflationary environment all of these inputs were feeding. No single disruption was necessarily fatal. The combination was, for some operations, unmanageable. The system's dependency profile was not a single thread that could snap; it was a web of threads all under tension at the same time, and the three-axis framework had no mechanism for registering the difference between a system with one vulnerability and a system with five vulnerabilities compounding simultaneously.

This is the gap the opening example was designed to make tangible, and it is the gap this chapter proposes to name.

## The Fourth Axis: Survivability

The framework I want to introduce adds a fourth axis to the three the sustainability literature already uses. I call it survivability, because the word captures precisely what the axis measures: not whether a system functions under current conditions, but whether the system survives when conditions change.

The definition is worth stating with some care, because the precision matters for everything the framework is used for in the chapters that follow.

Survivability, as I use the term, asks two linked questions about any food system under evaluation. First: what does this system require to keep functioning? The requirements include inputs (energy, water, feed, fertilizer, seed, labor, capital, infrastructure), market conditions (commodity prices, consumer demand, distribution networks), policy conditions (subsidies, regulations, trade agreements), and ecological conditions (climate stability, soil fertility, hydrological patterns, pollinator populations). Every food system has a dependency profile: a set of things it needs from outside itself in order to continue operating. The first question maps that profile.

Second: what happens when one or more of those requirements is not met? This is the stress question, and it is the question the three-axis framework does not ask. A system whose dependency profile is narrow (one that requires only conditions the system itself produces or that are reliably available independent of any single external supply chain) scores high on survivability. A system whose dependency profile is broad — one that requires continuous diesel, continuous commodity feed, continuous federal subsidies, continuous just-in-time logistics, continuous stable climate — scores low on survivability, regardless of how well it performs on the other three axes when all of those requirements are being met.

The axis is not binary. It is a continuum, and the continuum has a direction. A system that requires fewer external inputs and can tolerate the loss of any one input without collapsing is more survivable than a system that requires many external inputs and collapses when any one of them is removed. A system that degrades gracefully under stress (that reduces output without failing catastrophically) is more survivable than one that operates at full capacity until a threshold is crossed and then fails entirely. A system whose dependency profile is composed of conditions that are themselves stable and likely to persist is more survivable than one whose dependency profile is composed of conditions that are volatile, contested, or historically contingent.

The concept is not, in itself, novel. Ecologists have been studying the behavior of systems under stress for decades. C. S. Holling's foundational 1973 paper on ecological resilience drew the distinction between stability (a system's tendency to return to an equilibrium state after a small perturbation) and resilience (a system's capacity to absorb disturbance without shifting to a qualitatively different state).[^11] Holling's insight was that the two qualities are not the same, and that a system can be stable (returning quickly to equilibrium after small shocks) while being fragile (collapsing entirely when the shock exceeds a threshold). The distinction has been enormously productive in ecology, and versions of it have been imported into food-systems thinking. Tendall and colleagues, in a 2015 review in *Global Food Security*, defined food system resilience as "the capacity over time of a food system and its units at multiple levels, to provide sufficient, appropriate and accessible food to all, in the face of various and even unforeseen disturbances," and noted that the concept remained underspecified in the applied literature, that the food-systems field had adopted the vocabulary of resilience without having fully operationalized it into assessment frameworks.[^12]

Nassim Nicholas Taleb's work on antifragility (the property of systems that gain from disorder rather than merely surviving it) has added a further conceptual layer. Taleb's 2012 *Antifragile* argues that the categories of fragile, robust, and antifragile describe a spectrum of system responses to volatility, and that most modern institutions and supply chains are fragile precisely because they have been optimized for efficiency under stable conditions at the expense of robustness under unstable ones.[^13] The argument applies to food systems with obvious force. A concentrated industrial supply chain is the paradigmatic fragile system in Taleb's vocabulary: it performs superbly under the conditions it was designed for and breaks catastrophically under conditions it was not.

Survivability, as I use it, draws on these intellectual predecessors without duplicating any of them. It is narrower than Holling's resilience, which is a property of ecological systems at scales ranging from a pond to a biome. It is narrower than Taleb's antifragility, which is a property of systems and institutions across domains. It is specific to food systems, and it is designed to function as an axis: a dimension of evaluation that can be applied alongside the existing three, producing a four-axis assessment that captures both a system's current performance and its behavior under changed conditions.

The four axes, stated together, are:

**Economic viability:** does the system generate sufficient returns to persist as an economic entity under current conditions?

**Social equity:** does the system distribute its benefits and costs in ways that are equitable or defensible under current conditions?

**Environmental integrity:** does the system operate within ecological bounds that the surrounding environment can sustain under current conditions?

**Survivability:** what does the system require to keep functioning, and what happens when those requirements are not met?

Notice the structural difference. The first three axes share a temporal orientation: they describe performance under *current* conditions. The fourth axis asks about performance under *changed* conditions. The first three axes tell you how the system is doing. The fourth axis tells you what the system is made of: what it is standing on, how deep the foundations go, and what happens when the ground moves.

A system can score well on three axes and poorly on the fourth. This is, in fact, the most common configuration in the contemporary US food system. A well-managed industrial grain operation can be economically profitable, can provide employment and community economic activity, and, under best-management practices with cover cropping, reduced tillage, and precision nutrient application, can score reasonably on environmental metrics. It can do all of this while being deeply dependent on continuous fossil energy, continuous synthetic fertilizer derived from natural gas, continuous federal commodity-support payments, and continuous favorable climate for the specific cultivars it has planted. The three axes find the system sustainable. The fourth axis finds it piggybacking on a set of conditions that are historically contingent, geopolitically vulnerable, and climatically uncertain. Both findings are accurate. They are measuring different things.

A system can also score moderately on the first three axes and well on the fourth. A household-scale subsistence polyculture, growing diverse crops and raising a few animals on its own feed with minimal purchased inputs, may not score impressively on economic viability (the returns are low by commercial standards), may have limited social reach (it feeds a family, not a community), and may have an environmental footprint that is positive but modest in scale. On survivability, however, it scores high: it requires little from outside itself, it can tolerate the loss of any single input without collapsing, and it can continue to function under a wide range of conditions because it was never optimized for any single set of conditions in the first place. The three-axis framework would rank it below the well-managed industrial operation on most metrics. The four-axis framework reveals that the two systems have fundamentally different relationships to the future.

## What the Axis Does Not Do

I want to be precise about the limits of the framework, because precision about limits is what distinguishes a tool from a sales pitch.

The survivability axis does not measure moral worth. A system that scores poorly on survivability is not evil; it is structurally vulnerable. The industrial grain system that feeds most of the planet scores poorly on survivability for reasons that are structural and historical, not because anyone set out to build a fragile system. The fragility is a side effect of the optimization for efficiency, and the optimization for efficiency was, in its historical context, a rational response to the problems it was designed to solve: feeding a rapidly growing global population at declining real cost. The system succeeded at what it was built to do. Its survivability deficit is a feature of its design, not evidence of malice.

The survivability axis does not predict specific failures. It identifies dependency profiles and maps vulnerability, but it does not tell you which specific disruption will occur or when. A system that depends on continuous diesel, continuous commodity corn, continuous federal subsidies, and continuous stable climate has four distinct vulnerability surfaces. The axis can identify all four. It cannot tell you whether the disruption will come from diesel, corn, subsidies, or climate, or whether it will come from something the analysis did not anticipate. The axis is a diagnostic tool, not a crystal ball. It tells you where the fault lines are. It does not tell you when the earthquake comes.

The survivability axis does not produce a single numerical score. Attempts to reduce complex systems to single scores are, in my experience, more useful for marketing than for understanding. The axis produces a profile: a description of what the system depends on, how deep each dependency runs, and how the system behaves when each dependency is stressed. The profile can be compared across systems, and the comparison is informative, but the comparison is qualitative and contextual rather than a simple ranking. A system that depends on diesel is vulnerable in a different way than a system that depends on federal subsidies, and collapsing both vulnerabilities into a single number would obscure more than it reveals. The framework is a tool for thinking, not a scoreboard.

The framework is also not personally branded. I developed the core of this argument in a 2016 essay, where I proposed that sustainability assessments should include survivability as a dimension and sketched the evaluative logic that the current formulation extends. The essay named the framework with my own name, which I no longer think was the right choice. The framework is a tool. Tools are described by what they do, not by who made them. What matters is whether the four-axis assessment produces useful distinctions that the three-axis assessment does not. I believe it does, and the next two chapters are the test of that belief.

## What the Framework Reveals

The value of a framework is in the distinctions it makes visible. The three-axis framework makes visible whether a food system is currently functioning well: whether it is profitable, equitable, and ecologically responsible under the conditions it is operating in. These are real and important distinctions. The four-axis framework makes visible an additional distinction: the difference between a system that functions well under current conditions because it is well designed and a system that functions well under current conditions because the conditions happen to be favorable.

This distinction matters because conditions change. They have always changed — climate has oscillated, trade routes have opened and closed, energy regimes have shifted, empires have risen and collapsed and taken their agricultural systems with them. What is different now is not that conditions change but that the food systems we have built are more sensitive to change than most of their predecessors, because they have been more thoroughly optimized for a specific set of conditions than most of their predecessors. The global commodity system is the most efficient food-production apparatus in human history. It is also, by the metric of survivability, among the most dependent on the persistence of its specific operating conditions. The efficiency and the dependency are the same feature, viewed from different angles.

The chapters that follow apply the framework. Chapter 5 takes the two dominant industrial food systems — industrial feedlot animal production and industrial grain monoculture — and runs them through the four-axis assessment in detail, with the survivability axis producing findings that the three-axis assessment alone would not. Chapter 6 has already done the same for regenerative agriculture, and the finding there is instructive in a different way: the system scores substantially better than industrial production on the first three axes and meaningfully better on the fourth, but the survivability score is still constrained by the structural ceiling the chapter identified: the dependency on industrial commodity feed for monogastric animal production, which is a survivability deficit embedded in the system's relationship to its inputs rather than in its own management practices. The framework does not declare winners and losers. It maps the dependency profiles and shows where the vulnerabilities live. What the reader does with the map is the reader's decision.

The work of Part II of this book, which this chapter opens, is to use the framework and see what it shows. The survivability axis is the analytical addition the book proposes. It is not the book's conclusion. The conclusion lives in Part III, where the framework is brought to bear on a set of food-system possibilities that the current sustainability conversation has mostly overlooked: possibilities that score well on all four axes, including the fourth, because their dependency profiles are structured differently from anything the dominant food system currently offers.

There is one further point worth making before the framework is put to use, because it bears on how the rest of the book's argument is constructed. The survivability axis does not exist to condemn any existing food system. It exists to ask a question that the existing assessment framework does not ask, and to make visible a set of distinctions that the existing framework cannot see. An industrial operation that scores poorly on survivability is not thereby a failure. It may be, under current conditions, the most productive, most affordable, most efficient food-production system available. The survivability axis does not erase those achievements. It adds a dimension to the assessment: a dimension that asks whether the current conditions on which those achievements depend are conditions we can count on persisting. If they are, the three-axis assessment is sufficient and the fourth axis adds nothing of practical consequence. If they are not — if diesel prices are volatile, if commodity supply chains are geopolitically exposed, if climate patterns are shifting in ways that affect crop yields, if the policy environment that subsidizes current production is politically contestable — then the fourth axis is measuring something the first three cannot, and the measurement matters.

The framework is a lens. The next chapters are what the lens is pointed at.

The argument that optimization for efficiency under stable conditions produces fragility under unstable conditions is developed throughout the book and is directly relevant to the industrial food-system case.
