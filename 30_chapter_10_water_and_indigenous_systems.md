# Chapter 10: Indigenous Food Systems and the Water Column

The argument this book has been making rests on a particular frame. The Pleistocene baseline, the proposal that restoration should be guided by what the fossil record tells us about the ecological assemblages that preceded the near-time extinctions, is the frame the previous nine chapters have developed, applied, and defended against its serious critics. The frame is, I believe, defensible. It is not the only one.

This chapter exists because intellectual honesty requires saying so. The Pleistocene baseline is one way of answering the question "what should this landscape look like?" It is not the only way the question has been answered, and it is not the only way the question should be answered. Indigenous peoples across the Americas managed their landscapes for millennia between the terminal Pleistocene extinctions and the arrival of Europeans, and the management traditions they developed constitute a parallel intellectual framework for thinking about restoration, one that the Pleistocene frame should be in dialogue with rather than in competition with, and one that the rewilding conversation has been slower to engage than it should have been.

The chapter also exists because the rewilding conversation, as conducted in the conservation biology literature and in this book through Chapter 9, has been almost entirely terrestrial. The oceans are missing. Kelp forests, oyster reefs, pelagic fisheries, the entire marine domain: the water column has been largely absent from popular rewilding discourse, and the absence is a gap the book needs to address before it moves into practical application. The marine systems are as depleted as the terrestrial ones, and the case for their restoration is, if anything, more empirically advanced.

Two expansions, then. The first is a framework expansion: from the Pleistocene baseline alone to the Pleistocene baseline in dialogue with Indigenous management traditions. The second is a domain expansion: from terrestrial rewilding alone to terrestrial rewilding alongside marine restoration. Both expansions strengthen the book's argument by making it less parochial. Neither contradicts what has come before.

## A Parallel Tradition

Robin Wall Kimmerer opens *Braiding Sweetgrass* with a creation story, the Skywoman story of the Potawatomi, and then spends the next three hundred pages demonstrating what it looks like when Indigenous ecological knowledge and Western scientific training are held in the same mind simultaneously.[^1] The book is not an argument against Western science. It is an argument that Western science, when practiced in isolation from the traditions of people who have lived with and managed specific landscapes for thousands of years, is working with an unnecessarily thin dataset. The observation has force. It has force because Kimmerer is not making it from outside the scientific tradition. She is a plant ecologist at the SUNY College of Environmental Science and Forestry, a researcher who publishes in peer-reviewed journals, and she is making the argument from inside both traditions at once.

What Kimmerer describes, and what a substantial body of scholarship by Indigenous researchers has documented across multiple ecosystems, is a form of landscape management that does not fit cleanly into either the conservation or the agriculture categories that Western land-use thinking provides. The management is neither preservation (leaving the landscape alone) nor cultivation (reorganizing the landscape around a production target). It is something in between, and the in-between space is where the framework this book has been building meets a tradition it needs to reckon with.

M. Kat Anderson's *Tending the Wild*, a study of Native American management of California's ecosystems, provides the most thoroughly documented case for the scale and sophistication of this management.[^2] Anderson spent years working with Indigenous elders and with the ethnobotanical and fire-ecology literatures to reconstruct what California's landscapes looked like under active Indigenous management before European contact. The picture that emerges is not wilderness in the sense that the word is usually used. It is a managed landscape: managed through fire, through selective harvesting, through the cultivation of specific plant communities, through the deliberate maintenance of habitat for specific animal species. The management was not incidental. It was systematic, it was transmitted across generations, and it produced landscapes that early European observers described as paradisiacal (open, productive, teeming with game) without understanding that the condition they were admiring was the product of human management rather than the absence of it.

Anderson documented that California's Indigenous peoples used fire as a primary management tool across virtually every vegetation type in the state. Grasslands were burned to maintain open habitat and to promote the growth of seed-bearing grasses and bulb-producing plants. Oak woodlands were burned to reduce competition from conifers, to promote acorn production, and to clear the understory for ease of harvest. Chaparral was burned on rotation to produce a mosaic of successional stages, each of which provided different resources: early successional stages for basket materials and herbaceous food plants, later stages for browse and mast.[^3] The burning was not random. It was timed, targeted, and calibrated to the specific ecology of each vegetation type. The resulting landscapes were not natural in the sense of being unmanaged, and they were not agricultural in the sense of being reorganized around a single crop. They were something the Western vocabulary does not have a clean word for: managed ecosystems that produced food, fiber, and habitat as functions of ecological management rather than as alternatives to it.

The scale of this management was not marginal. Anderson estimates that Indigenous burning in California shaped millions of acres annually, a figure that challenges the assumption, embedded in the word "wilderness," that the landscapes Europeans encountered were unmanaged. The ecological consequences of the management's cessation after European contact were rapid and, in many cases, still unfolding. The dense conifer forests that now dominate much of California's Sierra Nevada, forests that burn catastrophically in modern wildfires because they have accumulated fuel loads that historical fire regimes would have prevented, are not the forests that existed under Indigenous management. They are the forests that grew in the absence of Indigenous management, the same way the grasslands that Chapter 7 described are the grasslands that exist in the absence of their Pleistocene grazers. The parallel is structural. In both cases, the current state of the landscape is a consequence of a relationship that has been interrupted, and the interruption is visible in the landscape's present condition.

The parallels to the rewilding argument are striking, and they are also incomplete. The rewilding framework this book has developed asks: what did this landscape look like before the near-time losses, and how do we restore the ecological relationships the losses disrupted? The Indigenous management framework asks a different question: how did the people who lived on this landscape between the Pleistocene and 1492 manage it, and what can that management tradition teach us about the landscape's productive capacity under human stewardship? Both questions are valid. Both produce different answers. The difference matters, and collapsing the two frameworks together would be a mistake that serves neither well.

## Two Baselines, Not One

The Pleistocene baseline and the Indigenous management baseline are not the same thing, and I want to be precise about why.

The Pleistocene baseline looks backward past human presence on the continent: past the near-time extinctions, past the arrival of the first peoples, to the ecological assemblages that existed before the megafaunal losses. The question it asks is ecological in the deep sense: what was the full complement of species and relationships this landscape evolved under, and what has been lost? The answer involves animals (mammoths, ground sloths, camelids, equids) that no living human on this continent has ever seen in the wild. The restoration it envisions is a restoration of ecological function that predates human management of any kind.

The Indigenous management baseline looks at a different window: the millennia between the Pleistocene losses and European colonization. During that window, Indigenous peoples across the Americas were not passive inhabitants of a depleted landscape. They were active managers of it. The management traditions they developed were responses to the landscape as it existed after the megafaunal extinctions, a landscape already missing its mammoths and ground sloths, already reorganized around a reduced fauna, and managed within those constraints by people whose knowledge of the land's ecology was deep, specific, and accumulated over hundreds of generations. The restoration this framework envisions is a restoration of human-landscape relationships that were disrupted not by the Pleistocene extinctions but by European colonization, forced removal, and the suppression of Indigenous land-management practices.

The two baselines produce different prescriptions. A Pleistocene baseline applied to the American Southwest asks about camelids and equids, the animals the fossil record identifies as co-evolutionary partners of the landscape's vegetation, as Chapter 7 described. An Indigenous management baseline applied to the same landscape asks about fire regimes, about the cultivation of agave and mesquite, about the management of riparian corridors, about the specific practices of the O'odham, the Diné, the Apache, and other peoples whose management shaped the region's ecology for thousands of years before Spanish contact. Both sets of questions are worth asking. They are not the same questions, and they lead to different places.

Lyla June Johnston, a scholar of Diné and Tsétsêhéstâhese descent whose work on Indigenous regenerative land management has drawn attention across disciplinary boundaries, has articulated a version of this distinction with particular clarity. Johnston's research documents Indigenous land management practices across North America that produced what she describes as food forests: complex, multi-story, species-diverse managed ecosystems that provided food, medicine, fiber, and habitat simultaneously.[^4] The management was not the management of wild landscapes by people who happened to live in them. It was the management of landscapes by people who understood themselves as participants in the ecological relationships the landscape maintained, people who saw their role not as external managers imposing order on nature but as keystone species whose management was itself a form of ecological function.

The keystone-species framing is the point at which the two traditions come closest to each other, and it is also the point at which the distinction between them is most important. The Pleistocene rewilding framework asks: which keystone species are missing from this landscape, and how do we restore them? The Indigenous management framework asks: what happens when humans function as keystone species, when human management is itself the ecological process the landscape depends on? The first question imagines restoration as the return of non-human animals to landscapes they have been absent from. The second imagines restoration as the return of human management practices to landscapes they have been absent from. Both involve restoring something the landscape has lost. What they propose to restore is different.

Notice that the two frameworks produce different models of what restoration looks like in practice. The Pleistocene frame, in its strongest version, imagines large tracts of land managed for ecological function with relatively minimal human intervention: proxy species reintroduced and then allowed to interact with the landscape in something approaching the self-organizing dynamics the Pleistocene assemblage would have produced. The Indigenous management frame imagines something closer to what Kimmerer calls the "honorable harvest": a landscape under continuous, attentive, knowledgeable human management, in which the management itself is the ecological process and the food is what the management yields. The two models are not opposites. They exist on a continuum, and any real landscape restoration would likely draw from both. But the emphases are different, and the difference in emphasis reflects a difference in what each tradition identifies as the primary loss to be restored.

I do not think one framework is correct and the other mistaken. I think they are complementary in a way that neither is sufficient to capture alone. A landscape like the American Southwest has lost both its Pleistocene megafauna and its Indigenous management traditions. A complete restoration framework for that landscape would need to engage both losses: the twelve-thousand-year absence of the camelids and equids, and the five-hundred-year suppression of the fire regimes, the managed harvesting, and the cultivation practices that Indigenous peoples maintained for the intervening millennia. The two losses are different in kind, different in timescale, and different in what their restoration would require. They are both real.

## What This Chapter Does Not Do

I want to be explicit about the limits of this engagement, because the limits matter.

This chapter does not speak for Indigenous peoples. It engages the published work of Indigenous scholars (Kimmerer, Johnston, Anderson) on the terms those scholars have set. Their work represents a parallel intellectual tradition with its own epistemology, its own standards of evidence, and its own relationship to the landscapes it describes. The Pleistocene framework this book has developed is not a superior framework into which Indigenous knowledge can be slotted as supporting evidence. It is a different framework, developed from a different disciplinary tradition, asking a different set of questions. The dialogue between the two is valuable precisely because it is a dialogue between genuinely different perspectives, not because one subsumes the other.

This chapter does not romanticize Indigenous land management as a monolith. The practices Anderson documents in California are specific to California's ecosystems and to the peoples who managed them. The practices Johnston describes across the continent vary enormously by region, by nation, by ecosystem, and by the specific cultural and ecological knowledge of the specific peoples involved. There is no single "Indigenous land management practice" any more than there is a single "Western agricultural practice." The diversity is the point. The scholarship this chapter engages is valuable because it is specific: specific peoples, specific landscapes, specific practices, specific ecological outcomes.

This chapter does not instrumentalize Indigenous knowledge as support for the book's argument. The Pleistocene rewilding case stands or falls on its own evidence, as the previous chapters have presented it. What the Indigenous management literature adds is not evidence for the Pleistocene case but a parallel framework that the Pleistocene case should be in conversation with, a framework that reveals dimensions of the restoration question the Pleistocene frame alone cannot see. The conversation is additive. It is not appropriative.

## The Water Column

The Indigenous management framework just described establishes something the Pleistocene frame alone cannot: a model of restoration in which the restored relationship, not just the restored species, is the unit of ecological function. Fire regimes, managed harvests, food forests maintained across centuries: these are relationships between people and landscapes that produced measurable ecological outcomes and that were lost not in the Pleistocene but within the last five hundred years. The question the framework raises is whether restoration of this kind can be demonstrated at scales and on timescales that make the case clear to policymakers and skeptics, not just to ecologists and Indigenous scholars who already understand it.

The marine domain is where that demonstration is furthest along. The rewilding conversation, in both the popular and the peer-reviewed literature, has been overwhelmingly terrestrial. The animals that dominate the discussion (mammoths, bison, wolves, camels, horses) are land animals. The landscapes that dominate the discussion (grasslands, forests, deserts, savannas) are land surfaces. The oceans have been present in the book only as a source of analogy (Daniel Pauly's shifting baseline syndrome in Chapter 2, the Alaska salmon fisheries in Chapter 8) rather than as a domain with its own restoration framework and its own set of losses to reckon with. That absence is a gap, and the gap matters for a specific reason: marine restoration operates on a faster clock than terrestrial restoration, and the results are correspondingly more concrete. If the argument this book has been making, that restoring ecological relationships produces food systems that score well on all four axes, needs a proof of concept, the water column is where the proof is most advanced.

The marine losses are enormous. They are also, unlike the Pleistocene megafaunal extinctions, primarily historical rather than prehistoric. They occurred within the window of human record-keeping, often within the last two centuries, and the documentation of what was lost is correspondingly detailed.

Kelp forests are the marine equivalent of old-growth terrestrial forests: structurally complex, biologically diverse, and foundational to the ecosystems they anchor. Kira Krumhansl and colleagues, in a 2016 analysis in *PNAS*, assembled the most comprehensive global dataset on kelp forest change to date, drawing on more than a hundred time series spanning multiple decades across the world's temperate and subpolar coastlines.[^5] The synthesis found that kelp forests had declined in thirty-eight percent of the ecoregions studied, with particularly severe losses in parts of the North Atlantic, the southern coast of Australia, and several Pacific regions. The declines were driven by a combination of ocean warming, sea urchin population explosions following the removal of urchin predators (otters and large predatory fish), and coastal water-quality degradation from nutrient runoff and sedimentation. The pattern echoes the terrestrial trophic cascades this book has described: remove the predator, and the herbivore population expands, and the foundation species collapses, and the entire community reorganizes around the loss.

The difference between the kelp story and the mammoth story is that the kelp story is reversible on timescales that matter for policy. Where sea otter populations have recovered, through legal protection under the Marine Mammal Protection Act and through active reintroduction programs along the Pacific coast, kelp forests have recovered with them. James Estes and colleagues documented this relationship in foundational work beginning in the 1970s, showing that the presence or absence of sea otters was the primary determinant of whether a given stretch of coastline supported a kelp forest or an urchin barren.[^6] The mechanism is straightforward trophic regulation: otters eat urchins, urchins eat kelp, and the presence of the predator controls the herbivore, which allows the foundation species to persist. Remove the otters and the system collapses to an urchin barren. Return the otters and the system recovers to a kelp forest. The time required for recovery, once the predator is restored, is measured in years rather than in millennia. The marine trophic cascade operates on a faster clock than the terrestrial one, and the restoration results are correspondingly more visible within a human career.

Oyster reefs present a parallel case with an additional dimension: the harvested organism is simultaneously performing ecological work. Michael Beck and colleagues, in their 2011 synthesis in *BioScience*, documented that approximately eighty-five percent of the world's oyster reef habitat had been lost, primarily through overharvest, disease, and habitat degradation.[^7] The loss is not merely a fisheries loss. Oyster reefs are ecosystem engineers, the marine equivalent of the keystone herbivores this book has described on land. A healthy oyster reef filters water: a single adult oyster can filter approximately fifty gallons of water per day, removing suspended sediment, excess nitrogen, and algal biomass from the water column.[^8] Oyster reefs provide structural habitat for hundreds of species of fish, crustaceans, and invertebrates. They stabilize shorelines against wave erosion. They sequester carbon and nitrogen in their shell matrix. The loss of eighty-five percent of the world's oyster reefs is the loss of far more than one species: it is the loss of an ecosystem function that maintained water quality, supported fisheries, and protected coastlines across every temperate and subtropical estuary where oysters once grew.

The restoration case is as strong for oyster reefs as for any terrestrial system the book has described. Federal and state partnership programs in the Chesapeake Bay, the Gulf of Mexico, and Puget Sound have rebuilt reef acreage on substrates of recycled shell and limestone, and the restored reefs have shown rapid colonization by native oyster populations, with measurable improvements in water clarity, fisheries productivity, and shoreline stability within years of construction.[^9] The food-system dimension is direct: restored oyster reefs produce a harvestable food product while simultaneously performing the ecological work the estuary depends on. The dependency profile is thin in exactly the way the survivability framework measures: the oyster needs the water column, and the water column needs the oyster, and neither requires commodity feed, antibiotics, diesel, or federal subsidies to keep the relationship functioning.

The food-system dimension of kelp forest restoration is also worth noting. Kelp forests are productive ecosystems that support fisheries directly, not merely habitat. The rockfish, lingcod, sea urchin, and abalone populations that kelp forests sustain are commercially harvested species. A coastline that supports a kelp forest supports a fishery. A coastline that has collapsed to an urchin barren supports neither. The restoration calculus is not an abstraction: it is a calculation about whether a specific stretch of coast produces food or does not, and the variable that determines the answer is whether the trophic structure is intact.

The broader marine picture extends the argument. The distinction between farmed salmon and wild Alaskan salmon goes well beyond culinary preference or marketing category: it is a distinction between two fundamentally different food systems with fundamentally different dependency profiles. Farmed Atlantic salmon, produced in net-pen aquaculture operations predominantly in Norway, Chile, Scotland, and British Columbia, requires continuous inputs of processed fish meal and fish oil derived from wild forage-fish harvests, continuous antibiotic and antiparasitic administration to manage the disease pressure inherent to high-density aquaculture, and continuous management of the waste stream the operations produce.[^10] A major farmed-salmon operation is, in structural terms, the marine equivalent of a terrestrial CAFO: a confined animal feeding operation in the water column, with many of the same dependency vulnerabilities the terrestrial version carries. Wild Alaskan salmon, managed under the stock-assessment methodology Chapter 8 described, requires the river system to remain intact, the ocean ecosystem to remain productive, and the harvest to remain within the bounds the stock's reproductive capacity sets. The dependency profile is the ecosystem itself. The difference between the two systems, scored on the survivability axis, is the same difference the book has documented on land: a system organized around industrial inputs versus a system organized around ecological function.

The salmon case connects, moreover, to the Indigenous framework this chapter has been developing. Pacific Northwest salmon fisheries were managed by Indigenous peoples for thousands of years before European contact, under systems that sustained both the harvest and the ecological function the salmon perform: nutrient transport from ocean to riparian forest, bear and eagle feeding, marine-derived nitrogen deposition in inland soils. The industrial replacement of those management systems with commodity extraction is a marine parallel to the terrestrial replacement of Indigenous fire management with fire suppression. In both cases, a management tradition that sustained the ecosystem's productive capacity across millennia was replaced by an extractive regime that degraded it within decades. In both cases, the restoration question is not merely ecological but cultural: whose knowledge of the system is being restored alongside the system itself?

What marine ecosystems would look like restored to historical baselines is a question the fisheries-history literature has begun to answer in remarkable detail. Jeremy Jackson and colleagues, in a landmark 2001 paper in *Science*, assembled historical, archaeological, and paleontological evidence to reconstruct the ecological state of coastal marine ecosystems before the onset of industrial exploitation.[^11] The reconstruction was sobering. Coastal ecosystems that are now managed as though their current depleted state were normal had, within the last few centuries, supported populations of marine vertebrates (whales, sea turtles, sharks, large predatory fish, seabirds) at densities that no living marine scientist has observed in the wild. The Chesapeake Bay, before industrial oyster dredging, contained oyster reefs sufficient to filter the bay's entire water volume in approximately a week; the current remnant population takes approximately a year to filter the same volume.[^12] Caribbean coral reefs, before the sequential removal of green turtles, manatees, and parrotfish, maintained algal communities in states that no modern reef ecologist has studied in the absence of the animals whose grazing controlled them. The marine shifting baseline, in Jackson's reconstruction, is at least as severe as the terrestrial one this book has documented.

The convergence of the marine and Indigenous frameworks is worth noting here, because it is not incidental. Many coastal Indigenous peoples managed marine systems with the same sophistication Anderson documented for terrestrial ones. The clam gardens of the Pacific Northwest, intertidal rock-wall terraces constructed by First Nations peoples to increase the productivity of clam beds, illustrate this pattern precisely. Research by Dana Lepofsky, Nicole Smith, and colleagues has documented that clam gardens increased clam productivity by factors of two to four compared with unmodified beaches, through deliberate modification of substrate, tidal exposure, and sediment dynamics.[^13] The management was not fishing in the extractive sense. It was mariculture: the active management of a marine ecosystem to increase its food-producing capacity while maintaining the ecological relationships the system depended on. The clam gardens were, in structural terms, the marine equivalent of the managed fire regimes Anderson documented on land: human management that enhanced rather than degraded the ecosystem's productive capacity, transmitted across generations, and disrupted by colonization.

The marine domain, in short, has its own version of every argument this book has made about land. It has its own missing keystone species. It has its own depleted baselines mistaken for natural states. It has its own trophic cascades demonstrating what happens when the missing species return. It has its own food systems — wild fisheries, restored shellfish, intact pelagic ecosystems — that produce food as functions of ecological health rather than as alternatives to it. And it has been almost entirely absent from the popular rewilding conversation, which has focused on charismatic terrestrial megafauna to the exclusion of the water column that covers seventy percent of the planet's surface.

## Integration

The Indigenous management framework adds something the Pleistocene frame alone cannot provide: a model of restoration that includes people. The Pleistocene rewilding conversation, as it has developed in the conservation biology literature, has tended to imagine restoration as the return of non-human animals to landscapes from which humans are largely absent, or at least from which human management is minimized. Indigenous management traditions offer a different vision: restoration as the return of human-landscape relationships in which human management is itself an ecological function. The two visions are not incompatible. A landscape could support both the return of proxy megafauna and the restoration of Indigenous fire and harvesting practices. The proxy species and the management practices would interact (as they always have, throughout the history of human habitation of every landscape on Earth), and the interaction would be the ecology of a managed, inhabited, restored system rather than the ecology of a wilderness emptied of people.

The marine framework adds something the terrestrial frame cannot: completed experiments. The terrestrial rewilding proposals this book has described operate on timescales of decades to centuries. The marine domain has already demonstrated what restoration looks like when it works: kelp forests recovering in years, oyster reefs becoming functional habitat within seasons, fish populations rebounding within a few reproductive generations under calibrated harvest. The clam gardens of the Pacific Northwest sit at the intersection of both expansions: Indigenous mariculture that enhanced marine productivity for centuries before colonization, disrupted by contact, and now being documented in ways that make its restoration empirically tractable.

Both expansions operate within the four-axis framework the book has developed. Indigenous management traditions, where they have been practiced and documented, produce food systems that score well on all four axes — economically viable within their scale, socially equitable by design, environmentally generative rather than extractive, and highly survivable because their dependency profiles are thin and their relationship to the landscape is constitutive rather than instrumental. Marine restoration programs produce food systems with the same profile: the ecosystem remains intact, the management remains disciplined, and the food is what the intact ecosystem produces when the management holds.

The book's argument is not weaker for admitting that the Pleistocene frame is one frame among several. It is stronger. A single-frame argument is brittle: it stands or falls on the validity of one analytical lens. An argument that holds the Pleistocene frame in dialogue with Indigenous management traditions and with marine restoration is an argument with multiple foundations. The frames support each other not by agreeing on everything but by covering each other's gaps. If the Pleistocene proxy-selection hypothesis proves more difficult to test than its advocates hope, the Indigenous management framework and the marine restoration framework remain. If the political obstacles to terrestrial rewilding prove as formidable as Rubenstein has warned, marine restoration offers a domain where the ecological case is already demonstrated and the political barriers are lower.

Part IV of this book, which this chapter opens, is about doing. The preceding chapters made the case. The remaining chapters ask what follows from it: how the framework adapts to where readers actually live, what practical guidance it offers, what must change in policy and culture for the trajectory to shift. The Pleistocene frame asks what the land was before the losses. The Indigenous frame asks what the land was under the management of the people who knew it best. The marine frame asks what the water was before the baselines shifted. Each question produces a different restoration target. Together, they produce a picture of what this continent could be, on land and at sea, in managed landscapes and in rewilded ones, that is richer, more honest, and more defensible than any single frame can draw alone.
