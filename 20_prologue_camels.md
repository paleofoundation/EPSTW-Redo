# Prologue: One Hundred Camels

It is not lost on me that for three million years, millions of camels lumbered through the very place where I first sat down to write this book. A cafe in Santa Monica called 212. The cafe is gone now. The camels have been gone much longer.

The camel was *Camelops hesternus*, "yesterday's camel," the Western camel, native to North America for almost the entire existence of its genus. It stood roughly seven feet at the shoulder—somewhat larger than a modern dromedary—built for the shortgrass and sage of the late Pleistocene. Its bones turn up across the American West, from the La Brea tar pits in southern California to kill sites in the Great Basin to arroyos in the southern Plains. It ate mesquite and prickly pear. It browsed juniper. It moved in herds across a continent where horses, mammoths, ground sloths, American lions, and American cheetahs shared the same country. Then, about thirteen thousand years ago, it was gone.[^1]

Most readers know this story, if they know it at all, in reverse. The camels Americans picture are Old World animals: dromedaries and Bactrians, the camels of the Silk Road and the Sahara, introduced to the Americas once, briefly, by the US Army Camel Corps in the 1850s, then failed out of service and absorbed into folklore. The ancestors of those Old World camels evolved on the North American continent and migrated out across the Bering land bridge millions of years ago. They diversified in Eurasia and Africa. They returned to their continent of origin only as imports. The camel's deep home is here. We have forgotten.

The forgetting is not personal. It is structural, and it is the reason I am writing this book.

The framework most of us have inherited for deciding what belongs on this continent begins in 1492. Animals present here before Columbus are "native." Animals introduced after Columbus are "non-native," "invasive," "exotic" — the vocabulary varies with the disposition of the speaker, but the baseline does not. The line is drawn at the date of European contact. What was here at that moment became the reference point against which all subsequent ecological change is measured.

It is an oddly narrow window. Camels had been absent from North America for roughly twelve and a half thousand years by the time Columbus made landfall. Horses had been absent for about the same span. Mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, American cheetahs, American lions, giant beavers, dire wolves, and most of the continent's megafaunal assemblage were already gone. The continent the Spanish sailed into was not a pristine ecosystem frozen at its baseline—it was a depleted one, long depleted, more than half of its large-bodied fauna already missing. The Columbian reference date captures the moment of European contact. It does not capture anything ecological about the land itself.

Paul S. Martin spent most of his career at the University of Arizona trying to put this observation into wider circulation. Martin was a paleoecologist who had worked on the Late Pleistocene extinctions for decades. By the time he published *Twilight of the Mammoths* in 2005, he had arrived at a position that is still, twenty years later, outside most popular conservation conversation. He proposed that the question "what belongs here" should be answered on a deeper timescale than 1492. He called the conservation paradigm that stops at Columbus "the Columbian Curtain." He proposed dropping it.[^2]

On the other side of the Curtain is a different continent. Mammoths in the boreal north. Ground sloths the size of bears browsing the forest edges. American horses on the plains. American cheetahs in the canyons (which is why the pronghorn, the fastest land animal in North America, still runs at speeds no living predator requires; its legs are calibrated to a cat that no longer exists). And camels. *Camelops* across the basin and range. *Hemiauchenia*, a larger, stouter relative, through the woodlands. Smaller llamas along the southwestern edge. A continent arranged around a set of animal relationships that have, almost entirely, gone unreciprocated for a hundred and twenty centuries.

Martin was not sentimental about this. He was a scientist with the fossil record to work with. His argument was that ecological function does not care about the date on which a species disappeared. A prairie that evolved with proboscideans behaves differently in their absence than one that evolved without them. A predator-prey dynamic misses both parties when either one is removed. A fruit tree whose seeds evolved to be eaten by ground sloths is still waiting for the ground sloths. The ecosystem is a set of relationships, and most of the relationships on this continent in the late Pleistocene have been one-sided for a very long time.

If you grew up in the American Midwest or the mid-South, you have almost certainly met an osage orange tree. *Maclura pomifera*. It is common along fence rows and in old hedgerow plantings — it was planted massively in the nineteenth century as a thorny living fence before barbed wire made it obsolete — and its fruit is the size and approximate color of a softball, grainy-skinned, slightly fragrant, faintly sticky with a white latex when cut. To any modern animal of the continent, that fruit is essentially inedible. Horses will nose one and move on. Cattle will not eat them. Squirrels pick through the seeds if they can reach them, which is rarely. The fruit falls, rots in place, and stains the ground. A tree with a bumper crop looks like it is producing for no one.

Because, in a real sense, it is. In 1982, Daniel Janzen and Paul Martin published a paper in *Science* titled "Neotropical Anachronisms: The Fruits the Gomphotheres Ate."[^3] The argument was straightforward; once you have read it, you cannot un-see it. Many of the large, sugary, otherwise-puzzling fruits of the Americas (avocados, pawpaws, honey locust pods, osage oranges, a long list of tropical species) carry the structural signatures of having co-evolved with large-bodied dispersers that are no longer there. A gomphothere, a mastodon-relative proboscidean that ranged widely across North and Central America until the end of the Pleistocene, would have taken an osage orange whole, walked some miles, and deposited the seeds in a dung pile prepared for germination. Without the gomphothere, the tree continues to produce the fruit the tree evolved to produce. The fruit is a message to a mouth that no longer exists.

Janzen and Martin called these trees ecological anachronisms. The phrase stayed with me.

An anachronism, strictly, is something out of its time—a character in the wrong century, a gesture from the wrong era. The osage orange is out of its time because its partner is out of its time. The tree is stranded in a present that has forgotten what it is for. Multiply this by every species with a large-fruit architecture, every grassland assemblage that evolved under the hooves and digestive tracts of animals weighing tons, every predator community whose prey has been gone for longer than there has been writing, and you have something like the actual ecological state of this continent. The landscape is not depleted in a vague or poetic way. It is missing specific animals. Their absence has specific consequences. The consequences have compounded across twelve thousand years of quiet rearrangement that most of us have not been taught to notice.

This is what the Columbian Curtain hides. Drop the baseline back to the end of the Pleistocene and the continent you are standing on is not recovering from European contact; it is recovering from something much older, and most of that recovery has not yet begun.

I should say that I have held various positions on this material over the years. I first read Martin in the late 2000s, and my initial reading left me convinced of a simpler story than I now think the evidence supports. For a long time I believed that the extinctions had been driven primarily by humans arriving across the Bering land bridge (the "overkill hypothesis," which Martin himself had done much to establish in his earlier career) and that the case for rewilding followed from a kind of collective human debt. Later work on rapid climate oscillation during the Younger Dryas, on the paleoclimate record more broadly, and on the difficulty of cleanly dating the extinctions against human arrival, has made me less confident. The question of cause is not settled, and I no longer think the rewilding argument depends on its resolution. Chapter 3 returns to this in the detail it deserves. The prologue does not need to.

What I want to put on the table first, before any framework or argument, is the shape of the absence itself. The animals are not here. The fruit is still being made. The grasslands still stand in the assemblages that evolved around grazers that are gone. The pronghorn still runs from a cheetah. The continent is a library that has lost most of its librarians, and it is somehow still trying to operate.

I would like, somewhere in my life, to see one hundred camels in an osage orange orchard.

This is plainly said because there is no more elegant way to say it. I do not mean it as policy, or as provocation, or as a headline. I mean it as a wish—a specific one, with specific animals, in a specific arrangement. A hundred camels because that is enough for a small, real herd; large enough to be a population and small enough to manage honestly. An osage orange orchard because I would like to know what those trees look like when their fruit has a mouth. Camels are not gomphotheres, but they are closer in body plan, gut size, and browsing habit than anything now available on the continent, and they are already here—feral in parts of the American West, stabled on hobby ranches elsewhere, just not in the ecological role they once had. I would like to know whether the fruit gets eaten. I would like to know whether the seeds, passed through a large mammalian gut and deposited with fertilizer, germinate at higher rates than the current state of abandonment allows. I would like to know whether the ground around the trees begins, even slightly, to resemble something other than rot.

I am not sure anyone would fund the experiment. I am not sure the permits would be approved, or the ecological risk assessments would come out favorably, or the neighbors would agree. I know what serious critics of Pleistocene rewilding would say about it. I have read their arguments, and the book takes them seriously in the chapters where they belong. The wish is not a program. It is the thing the program would be for.

This book is, for the most part, an analytical argument about how to evaluate food systems. It proposes a framework, applies it, and reaches conclusions I think are defensible. But the analytical case is not what drives it. What drives it is this: the orchard, the camels, the fruit with a mouth. The sense that we have been living for twelve thousand years on a continent whose ecological vocabulary has been spoken at half-volume, and that there is no reason to treat that half-silence as a permanent condition.

Paul Martin spent his career trying to make the silence legible. I am writing this book, more than anything else, to return his argument to a conversation that has mostly moved on without it.

The framework comes later. The camels come first.

[^1]: Martin, P. S. (2005). *Twilight of the Mammoths: Ice Age Extinctions and the Rewilding of America.* University of California Press. Martin synthesizes the Late Pleistocene loss of more than forty North American large-mammal species, including *Camelops hesternus* and *Hemiauchenia*, dated to approximately 13,000 years before present in the North American fossil record.

[^2]: Martin (2005). The "Columbian Curtain" is Martin's own framing for the conservation paradigm that uses 1492 as an ecological baseline, treating European contact as the effective starting line for questions of native and non-native status.

[^3]: Janzen, D. H., & Martin, P. S. (1982). Neotropical anachronisms: the fruits the gomphotheres ate. *Science*, 215(4528), 19–27. The foundational paper on megafaunal fruit syndrome and ecological anachronism.
