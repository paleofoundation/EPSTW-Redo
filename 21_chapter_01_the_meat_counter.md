# Chapter 1: The Meat Counter

In September of 2009 I was standing at the meat counter in the Whole Foods in Overland Park, Kansas, with my mother, scanning the selection and trying to make sense of what I was looking at.

Overland Park is a suburb of Kansas City, the kind of sprawling Johnson County development where the grocery stores are large and clean and lit like operating theaters. The Whole Foods was relatively new to the area. It occupied the spot in the local retail ecology that a Whole Foods always occupies: the store where the prices are higher and the assumption, embedded in the brand, is that the higher prices are buying you something the other stores cannot guarantee. I was there because I believed that assumption. I had reason to.

I had been sick for the better part of a year. By September I was on the other side of the worst of it, eating carefully, paying attention to what I put in my body in a way I had never done before. The wrong food could set me back weeks. The right food (clean protein, known sourcing, no hidden grains) was not a preference but a medical necessity, and I was standing in the meat section of a store I trusted because the store's entire brand proposition was that it had already done the vetting for me. The grass-fed beef was what I had come for. The label said grass-fed. The price reflected grass-fed. The display case was arranged with the quiet confidence of a store that believed it was selling you exactly what you thought you were buying.

I picked up a package, turned it over, and asked the person behind the counter a question that would not have occurred to me six months earlier. "Is this a hundred percent grass-fed?"

He said yes.

I asked again — politely, but with the specificity that comes from having spent months learning that the wrong food could put me back in a condition I did not want to revisit. "A hundred percent? Grass the whole way through?"

He paused. "It's grass-fed," he said, "but it's grain-finished for the last three months."

My mother and I looked at each other. She said what I was thinking before I could say it: "If they're feeding them grain to fatten them up, how can you call it grass-fed?"

The man behind the counter was not being evasive. He was, as far as I could tell, repeating what he had been told. The beef was marketed as grass-fed. It had been raised on grass for most of its life. It had then been transitioned to grain for the final ninety days before slaughter (a practice called grain finishing, common in the beef industry, which puts weight on the animal quickly and produces the marbling American consumers have been trained to prefer). The man behind the counter was not the person who had decided to call this product grass-fed. He was the person standing between the decision and the customer, doing his job, and his job did not include explaining the regulatory architecture that made the label possible.

I did not buy the beef. I stood there for another minute, looking at the case — the chicken, the turkey, the pork, the lamb, all of it arranged under labels I had been reading as promises — and something shifted. Not dramatically. Not with any sense of revelation. Quietly, the way a crack appears in a wall you have been looking at for a long time without seeing it. The label said one thing. The practice was another. And if this label, on this product, in this store (the store whose entire value proposition rested on the reliability of its sourcing claims), could mean something other than what it appeared to mean, then what else in the case was I reading wrong?

The gap between the label and the practice was not an accident or an error. It was structural. I did not yet have that word for it. I only had the feeling, which was the feeling of having trusted a system that had not earned the trust, without knowing that the trust was unearned until I asked one specific question on one specific afternoon.

## What "Grass-Fed" Meant

At the time I was standing in that Whole Foods, the term "grass-fed" on beef packaging in the United States was governed by a voluntary marketing claim standard administered by the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service. The standard, finalized in 2007, defined grass-fed as meaning that the animal's diet had been derived solely from forage (grass, legumes, hay, silage) with the exception of milk consumed prior to weaning.[^1] Under this definition, grain finishing would have disqualified the beef from a grass-fed claim. The standard existed. On paper, it was clear.

In practice, the standard was voluntary, and its enforcement relied on a verification process that most producers did not undergo. The USDA did not inspect every operation making a grass-fed claim. It certified those that applied for certification and met the documentation requirements; those that did not apply were subject to the same labeling regulations that governed all meat products, which is to say, subject to review only if a complaint was filed or an audit was triggered. The gap between the standard's existence and its application was wide enough to drive a grain truck through. A retailer could sell beef labeled "grass-fed" that had been grain-finished, and unless someone with standing complained to the right agency, the label stood.[^2]

The standard was later withdrawn entirely. In January 2016, the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service revoked its grass-fed marketing claim standard, citing concerns about the verification process and the scope of the standard's applicability.[^3] After the withdrawal, the term "grass-fed" on beef packaging became, in regulatory terms, essentially unregulated at the federal level: any producer could use it, under any definition they chose, provided they did not make a claim that was demonstrably false under the Federal Trade Commission's general deceptive-practices authority. The practical effect was that "grass-fed" became a term whose meaning was determined by whoever was printing the label.

I did not know any of this in 2009. I was a consumer standing in a store, reading a label, and trusting the label to mean what I thought it meant. What I thought it meant was that the animal had eaten grass. What the label actually certified — to the extent it certified anything — was something narrower, more hedged, and more dependent on the specific practices of a specific producer than the two words on the package could convey. The gap between my assumption and the reality was not a gap I had been invited to notice. It was a gap the label was designed to bridge without my having to look down.

## The Pattern

I might have treated the Whole Foods moment as an isolated problem — a single misleading label on a single product in a single store — if the same pattern had not kept appearing.

Consider "free-range," as applied to poultry. The term appears on egg cartons and chicken packaging across the country, and its presence reliably commands a premium. Consumers pay more for free-range eggs than for conventional eggs, often significantly more, on the reasonable assumption that the word describes a meaningfully different life for the bird that produced them.

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service defines free-range, for the purpose of poultry labeling, as meaning that the birds have been "allowed access to the outside."[^4] The definition does not specify the size of the outdoor area. It does not specify the duration of access. It does not specify the quality of the outdoor space: whether it is grass or concrete or bare dirt. It does not specify whether the birds actually go outside, only that a door exists through which they could, if they chose to, if they could reach it, if they knew it was there. A producer can raise ten thousand birds in a single enclosed structure, open a small door at one end of the structure for some portion of the birds' lives, and label the resulting product free-range. The birds nearest the door may occasionally step through it. The birds at the far end of the structure — hundreds of feet from the opening, in a flock so dense that individual movement through the crowd is physically constrained — may never see daylight. The label does not distinguish between these birds. It certifies the existence of a door.

Most consumers, asked what "free-range" means, will describe something that looks like a farm: birds on grass, moving freely, doing the things birds do when they have room and sunlight and ground to scratch. The mental image is not irrational. It is the image the language invites. "Free-range" is a compound of two words that each carry spatial meaning: freedom and range, openness and distance. The phrase produces a picture. The regulatory definition behind the phrase produces a door.

I came to understand this only gradually, in the months after the meat counter, as I began reading labeling regulations with the attention I had previously reserved for ingredient lists. The gap between the picture and the door is not a failure of consumer education. It is not a case of consumers being too lazy or too credulous to read the fine print. There is, in most cases, no fine print to read. The label says "free-range." The regulation defines it. The consumer encounters only the label. The regulation sits in the *Federal Register*, available to anyone who knows where to look, written in the language regulations are written in, useful to anyone with the time and the training to parse it. The system is not hiding the definition. It is relying on the distance between the definition and the consumer to do the work that active deception would do if the system were less sophisticated.

"Pasture-raised" is a variation on the same theme. The term has no federal regulatory definition at all. It appears on packaging because producers choose to put it there, and its meaning is whatever the producer's own practices happen to be. Some operations using the "pasture-raised" label run birds on genuinely open pasture in mobile shelters, moved daily, with real access to grass and insects and sunlight. Others use it more loosely. The consumer cannot tell the difference from the package. Third-party certifications (Certified Humane, Animal Welfare Approved, and others) have stepped into the regulatory gap with their own standards for what "pasture-raised" means, and some of those standards are rigorous. But the label itself, the two words on the carton, certifies nothing in particular. It is a description without a definition, a promise without a guarantor.

Or consider "organic," which is the most comprehensively regulated of the major food labels and still produces a version of the same gap. The USDA's National Organic Program, established under the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 and administered through a body of regulations codified at 7 CFR Part 205, defines organic production as a system that integrates cultural, biological, and mechanical practices to foster cycling of resources, promote ecological balance, and conserve biodiversity.[^5] The standards are real, detailed, and enforced through a third-party certification process that is substantially more rigorous than anything governing "grass-fed" or "free-range." An organic label on a food product means something. It means the producer has been inspected, has met specific input and practice requirements, and has documentation to support the claim.

What the organic label does not mean, and has never meant, is what many consumers take it to mean. Organic certification does not certify that the food was produced locally. It does not certify that the animals were raised on pasture. It does not certify that the soil is being built rather than depleted, only that certain prohibited inputs (most synthetic fertilizers, most synthetic pesticides, most antibiotics, genetically engineered organisms) are absent from the production process. A large-scale organic operation can look, structurally, a great deal like a conventional industrial operation: confined animal housing, monoculture crop rotations, long-distance commodity distribution, significant fossil-fuel inputs for field work and transportation. The organic label certifies what is not being used. It does not certify how the system as a whole is functioning.

This is not a criticism of organic certification. The organic standard was designed to do what it does, and what it does is useful: it removes a specific set of chemical inputs from the production process and provides a verification mechanism for that removal. The problem is not that the standard fails at its own task. The problem is that the label, as encountered by a consumer in a grocery aisle, communicates something substantially broader than the standard certifies. The word "organic" carries associations (with naturalness, with ecological care, with a certain quality of attention to the land) that the certification process was not designed to guarantee and does not guarantee. The consumer's assumption is reasonable. The label's scope is narrower than the assumption. The gap is the same gap I encountered at the meat counter, wearing different clothes.

## The Architecture of the Gap

Standing in the Whole Foods that September, I did not have the vocabulary for what I was noticing. I had a feeling, which was the feeling of having been misled without anyone having lied to me, and I had a question, which was how a system this large and this well-funded could consistently produce labels that communicated less than consumers believed they were being told.

The answer, which I arrived at slowly over the years that followed, is that the labels are not failing. They are succeeding at a task different from the one consumers assume they are performing.

A food label in the United States is, in regulatory terms, a marketing claim. It exists at the intersection of the producer's desire to differentiate a product, the retailer's desire to command a price premium, and the regulatory apparatus's desire to prevent outright fraud without unduly burdening commerce. The label's job, within this intersection, is to communicate *enough* to support the marketing claim while remaining narrow enough to survive regulatory scrutiny. "Grass-fed" communicates pastoral images. "Free-range" communicates open space. "Organic" communicates naturalness. Each term does this reliably, for the majority of consumers, and each term is defensible under the regulatory definition that governs it. The system works. It works for the producer, who can differentiate a product. It works for the retailer, who can charge a premium. It works for the regulator, who can point to a definition. The only party for whom it does not quite work is the consumer who assumed the label was telling them something about the *system* their food came from, rather than about a single certified attribute of that system.

This is, I think, the structural observation the meat counter moment eventually led me to. The food labeling system is not broken. It is designed to communicate less than consumers want to know. Each label certifies one thing — one attribute, one absence, one practice verified at one point in the production chain. Consumers want to know many things — how the animal lived, what it ate, how the land was managed, what inputs were used, what the operation's relationship to its surrounding ecology looks like, what would happen to the system if one of its dependencies were removed. No single label, even a good one, can answer all of those questions. The labels were not designed to answer all of those questions. They were designed to answer one question each, narrowly, defensibly, in language that happens to evoke a broader picture than the narrow answer supports.

The gap between the narrow answer and the broad picture is not a conspiracy. It is an architecture. It is the predictable result of a system in which labeling is designed to facilitate market differentiation rather than to provide comprehensive transparency about production practices. Producers are not villains for using the labels as designed. Retailers are not villains for stocking labeled products. Regulators are not villains for writing narrow definitions. Consumers are not fools for reading the labels the way the labels invite themselves to be read. Everyone in the system is doing what the system was designed to let them do. The system itself is the problem, and the problem is not that it fails but that it succeeds at something less than what most of us need it to do.

The architecture has a further consequence worth naming, because it bears on everything the rest of this book discusses. A label that certifies a single attribute can, by its presence, create the impression that the system behind the product has been evaluated comprehensively. A package of chicken bearing an organic label and a free-range label and a "no antibiotics ever" label looks, to the consumer scanning the case, like a product that has been thoroughly vetted. Three labels. Three assurances. Surely the system has been looked at from multiple angles. In fact, what has happened is that three narrow attributes have each been verified independently, with no requirement that anyone has ever looked at the operation as a system: as a whole set of practices, dependencies, inputs, outputs, and vulnerabilities functioning together. The labels are point measurements. The consumer needs a map. The multiplication of labels can actually make the map harder to read, because it produces the feeling of comprehensiveness without the substance of it.

## What I Did Not Know Yet

I want to be careful, here, about what I am not claiming. I am not claiming that I understood any of this at the meat counter. I understood almost none of it. I understood that the label had misled me, and I understood that I was angry, and I understood that the anger was not usefully directed at the person behind the counter, who was doing his job and had answered my question honestly when I pressed him on it. Beyond that, I was a sick person in a grocery store who had just learned that the food she trusted was not what she thought it was.

What happened next took years, not minutes. I went home. I started reading. I read about labeling standards. I read about certification processes. I read about USDA definitions and the regulatory comment process and the difference between a mandatory standard and a voluntary marketing claim. I read about organic certification's scope and limits. I read about the word "natural," which has no regulatory definition at all on most food products and functions as pure marketing language attached to nothing.[^6] I read, eventually, about the deeper questions underneath the labeling problem: about food systems, about sustainability, about what it means to ask whether the way we produce food can continue to work under conditions different from the ones we are used to.

The reading took me to Paul Martin. It took me to Allan Savory and Joel Salatin and the regenerative agriculture literature. It took me to the paleontological record, to the Columbian Curtain, to the near-time extinctions, to the question of what this continent looked like before most of its large-bodied fauna disappeared. It took me to questions I could not have anticipated from a meat counter in suburban Kansas: questions about timescale, about ecological baselines, about what it means for a food system to survive under conditions different from the ones it was designed for.

It took me, eventually, to the framework this book proposes (a framework I did not have in 2009, did not begin to develop until several years later, and did not fully articulate until the middle of the last decade). The path between the meat counter and the framework was not a straight line. It included a number of positions I have since revised, a number of arguments I made with more confidence than the evidence warranted, and a number of assumptions I held longer than I should have. The authorial revisions that have shaped this book (about dietary universality, about the causes of the Pleistocene extinctions, about the tone in which disagreement should be conducted) are part of the trajectory. The meat counter was not the moment I found the answer. It was the moment the question became unavoidable. The distance between the question and whatever answer this book offers has been covered on foot, over sixteen years, with wrong turns.

I am noting this because the trajectory matters to the book's honesty. The temptation, in a chapter like this one, is to present the early moment of recognition as though it contained the later understanding, as though I stood at the meat counter and saw, fully formed, the structural inadequacy of the labeling system and the deeper questions it pointed toward. That is not what happened. What happened was small and specific: a label that did not mean what I thought it meant, a feeling of having been misled, and a question that did not resolve itself for years. The understanding came later, incrementally, through reading and experience and — as the revisions that have shaped this book will show — through being wrong about a number of things along the way. The meat counter was the beginning of a question, not the arrival at an answer.

## What Labels Cannot Tell Us

The labeling problem is, in the end, a problem about information: about what the consumer is given and what the consumer would need. The gap between the two is not a gap that better labels can fully close, because the questions a thoughtful consumer wants answered about a food system are not questions that fit on a package.

A label can tell you that an animal was fed grass. It cannot tell you whether the grassland the animal grazed on is building soil or losing it (whether the land is gaining organic matter year over year or slowly losing the biological capital that makes it productive). It cannot tell you whether the operation depends on purchased inputs from industrial commodity markets for parts of its production cycle, or whether it is genuinely self-sustaining on its own acreage. It cannot tell you whether the operation could survive the loss of a single input (a subsidy, a commodity feed source, a supply chain) or whether it would collapse. It cannot tell you what the land looked like before the operation was there, or what it will look like after. It cannot tell you whether the system the food comes from is one that could continue to function under conditions meaningfully different from today's, or whether it is riding on a set of assumptions about stable inputs, stable climate, and stable policy that may or may not hold.

These are not unreasonable questions. They are, I would argue, the questions that matter most: not just for the consumer deciding what to buy on a Tuesday evening, but for the society deciding what kinds of food production to support, to subsidize, to build infrastructure around, to invest in for the next century. The questions are about systems, not about attributes. They are about how the whole thing works, not about whether one variable meets one standard. And the labeling system, by design, answers attribute questions. It was built for attributes. It does attributes well. It does not do systems at all.

There is a reason the labeling system was built this way, and the reason is not sinister. Attributes are verifiable. You can inspect an operation and determine whether the birds have access to the outdoors, whether synthetic pesticides were applied, whether antibiotics were administered. These are binary or near-binary questions, and a regulatory system can answer binary questions through documentation and inspection. Systems-level questions — is this operation ecologically sustainable, is it resilient to disruption, could it continue to function under changed conditions — are not binary. They require frameworks of evaluation that the regulatory apparatus was not designed to perform and that the labeling system was never intended to communicate. Asking a food label to convey systems-level information is asking it to do something it was not built to do. The question is not why the labels fail at this. The question is what else we have.

This is where the meat counter moment, small as it was, connects to the larger argument the book is about to make. The label's inadequacy is not a labeling problem in the narrow sense. It is not a problem that can be solved by writing better definitions for "grass-fed" or tightening the enforcement of "free-range" or adding more labels to packages that are already covered in them. It is a problem about the kind of information we have access to when we make decisions about food, and the kind of information we would need. The gap between the two is where most of the interesting questions in food-systems thinking live. It is the gap the rest of this book is written to address.

I do not have a better label to offer. I want to be clear about that, because this chapter could easily be read as a setup for a pitch — here is what is wrong with existing labels, and here is the label that fixes it. That is not where this book goes. The problem is not that the right label has not been invented. The problem is that labels, as a category of communication, cannot carry the weight of what consumers need to know about the systems their food comes from. The solution, if there is one, is not a better label but a better framework: a way of evaluating food systems that addresses the questions labels were never designed to ask.

The prologue planted an image: camels in an osage orange orchard, a continent missing its animals, a longing for restoration. This chapter has planted a different kind of image: a woman at a meat counter, reading a label, discovering that the label is not enough. Both images are starting points. The prologue's starting point is emotional — the feeling that something is missing from the landscape. This chapter's starting point is practical — the feeling that something is missing from the information. The two feelings converge on the same set of questions. If the labels cannot tell us what we need to know about our food systems, what can? If the dominant framework for evaluating those systems is measuring the wrong things, or measuring the right things too narrowly, what would a more adequate framework look like? And if such a framework exists, what does it reveal about the systems we rely on — and about the ones we have not yet tried?

The next chapter begins the work of answering those questions, by going back much further than a meat counter in Kansas, to the moment an entire continent's ecological vocabulary was quietly, catastrophically reduced.
