# Chapter 5: Scoring the Industrial System

The previous chapter introduced a framework. This chapter puts it to work.

The four-axis assessment was designed to do something the three-axis model cannot: to distinguish between a food system that functions well under current conditions and a food system that functions well under current conditions because those conditions happen to be favorable. The distinction matters only when the framework is applied to specific systems and the application produces specific findings. Abstractions do not feed anyone. The question is what the framework shows when it is pointed at the systems that actually feed most of the people reading this book.

Two systems dominate the American food landscape, and between them they account for the overwhelming majority of calories and protein consumed in the United States. The first is industrial feedlot animal production, the confined animal feeding operation (CAFO), which finishes roughly ninety-nine percent of the meat animals sold in US retail markets on concentrated grain rations in confined facilities.[^1] The second is industrial grain monoculture: the corn, soy, and wheat production system that supplies both the feed for the first system and the caloric base for most processed food consumed in the country. They are structurally linked. The feedlot depends on the grain monoculture for its feed inputs. The grain monoculture depends in turn on the feedlot, on the export market, and on the ethanol mandate for its demand. Neither system, in its current form, would exist without the other. Scoring them separately is an analytical convenience; in practice they are two faces of a single industrial substrate.

The chapter takes each system through all four axes in turn. The work is analytical, and I want to be clear about what that means. The findings are structural, not moral. A system that scores poorly on a given axis is not thereby condemned. It is described: its dependency profile mapped, its vulnerabilities identified, its genuine achievements registered alongside its genuine costs. The framework is a diagnostic instrument rather than a verdict.

## Industrial Feedlot Animal Production

### Economic Viability

At the point of sale, the industrial feedlot system is an economic success by the metric most consumers encounter: price. Inflation-adjusted retail prices for beef, pork, and chicken in the United States have declined substantially over the last half century. The USDA's Economic Research Service tracks this in real terms: between 1960 and 2020, the inflation-adjusted retail price of chicken fell by roughly forty percent, and beef and pork prices have remained lower in real terms than they were at mid-century peaks, despite periodic spikes driven by drought, disease, or commodity-market disruption.[^2] For a consumer standing in a grocery store, the system delivers protein at a price point that most household budgets can absorb. This is not a trivial achievement. Cheap animal protein has been one of the defining features of the American diet for two generations, and the system that produces it has been remarkably effective at the narrow task of minimizing per-unit cost at the point of purchase.

The point-of-purchase price, however, is not the full economic accounting, and the gap between the two is where the system's economic picture changes. The Pew Commission on Industrial Farm Animal Production, an independent panel convened by the Pew Charitable Trusts and the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, published its comprehensive assessment in 2008, and its finding on this point was direct: the retail price of industrial animal products does not reflect the external costs the system imposes on public health, environmental quality, and rural economies.[^3] The externalities are real, they are large, and they are borne by parties other than the producer or the consumer at the point of sale.

The antibiotic-resistance externality is among the most economically consequential. A 2019 review published by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimated that antibiotic-resistant infections cause more than 2.8 million illnesses and more than 35,000 deaths annually in the United States, with associated healthcare costs and productivity losses running into the tens of billions of dollars.[^4] The connection to agricultural antibiotic use is not casual. The FDA reported that in 2021, approximately sixty-six percent of medically important antibiotics sold in the United States were sold for use in food-producing animals, administered not primarily to treat clinical disease but to promote growth and prevent illness in the high-density conditions that confined operations require.[^5] The mechanism by which sub-therapeutic antibiotic use in livestock selects for resistant bacteria that subsequently affect human health has been documented across multiple peer-reviewed studies and was identified as a significant public health concern by the World Health Organization as early as 2001.[^6] The cost of that resistance is externalized. It does not appear on the price tag of the chicken breast or the ground beef, but it appears in the healthcare system, in the insurance premiums, and in the reduced efficacy of antibiotics that were once reliable clinical tools.

Pollution remediation adds a second layer of externalized cost. The EPA estimated in 2003 that animal feeding operations were contributing to water quality impairment in more than twenty thousand miles of rivers and streams and had contaminated groundwater in seventeen states.[^7] The cleanup costs for that contamination are borne by municipalities, by taxpayers, and by downstream agricultural and recreational operations rather than by the CAFOs whose waste produced the contamination. Property values in communities adjacent to large-scale confined operations decline measurably: studies in North Carolina, Iowa, and Minnesota have documented decreases ranging from six to eleven percent in residential property values within a three-mile radius of a CAFO, with effects persisting for years after the operation's establishment.[^8]

Federal subsidies add a further economic distortion. The commodity support system, administered through the farm bill and its various iterations since the 1970s, has kept the price of corn and soybeans below the full cost of production for most of the last four decades, and those below-cost commodity grains are the primary input for confined animal feeding. Researchers at Tufts University calculated that between 1997 and 2005, the industrial livestock sector saved an estimated $35 billion through below-cost feed made possible by federal commodity support payments.[^9] That saving is a transfer from the taxpayer to the industrial system rather than an efficiency gain in any meaningful economic sense, and it renders the retail price of industrial meat artificially low relative to what the system actually costs to operate.

The economic axis, then, produces a split finding. At the point of sale, the system scores well: it delivers affordable protein to consumers. When the externalities are accounted (healthcare costs from antibiotic resistance, pollution remediation, property value losses, and taxpayer-funded commodity subsidies), the economic picture reverses. The system's apparent efficiency is substantially subsidized by costs it does not pay.

### Social Equity

The social axis asks whether the system distributes its benefits and costs equitably or defensibly. For industrial feedlot production, the distribution is sharply asymmetric.

Worker conditions in meat processing have been documented extensively. The Government Accountability Office reported in 2005 that meatpacking workers experienced injury and illness rates substantially above the national average for manufacturing, with the Bureau of Labor Statistics recording injury rates roughly two and a half times the average across all private industry.[^10] The COVID-19 pandemic made the workforce conditions newly visible: the CDC documented infection clusters at 115 meat and poultry processing facilities across 19 states in the first two months of the pandemic alone, with facility-level attack rates that reflected the combination of close working proximity, high line speeds, and inadequate protective measures.[^11] The workers in these facilities are disproportionately immigrant, disproportionately non-English-speaking, and disproportionately employed under conditions where reporting injuries or unsafe practices carries economic and sometimes legal risk. The social cost of industrial meat processing is concentrated in the workforce that makes the system operate.

Community health impacts extend beyond the processing facilities. Residents of communities adjacent to large-scale confined operations report elevated rates of respiratory symptoms, headache, nausea, and psychological distress in epidemiological studies conducted across North Carolina, Iowa, and other states with high CAFO concentrations.[^12] The communities bearing these health effects are disproportionately rural, disproportionately low-income, and disproportionately communities of color, a pattern that has led environmental justice researchers to identify CAFO siting as a significant environmental equity concern.[^13]

The displacement of family farmers is a structural feature of the system's economic logic. Vertical integration in the poultry and pork industries has consolidated production into contract-grower arrangements in which the processor owns the animals, the feed formula, and the processing relationship, while the grower owns the capital infrastructure: the houses, the land, the debt. Congressional Research Service analyses have documented that the number of independent hog farms in the United States declined from more than 660,000 in 1980 to fewer than 67,000 by 2012, while total hog production increased over the same period.[^14] The consolidation eliminated not pork production but the people who had been doing it, replacing them with a smaller number of larger-scale contract operations producing more animals at lower margins under tighter corporate control. The social cost of that consolidation has been documented by rural sociologists for decades, and the industry itself does not seriously contest it. It shows up in rural depopulation, in the loss of small-town economic activity, and in the civic infrastructure that contracts when the people who supported it leave.

The social axis scores net negative: the system's benefits flow primarily to consumers in the form of cheap protein and to the corporate entities that control the processing and distribution chain; its costs are concentrated in workers, in adjacent communities, and in the rural regions whose agricultural economies the system has restructured.

### Environmental Integrity

The environmental axis is where the industrial feedlot system's costs are most extensively documented and least disputed.

Waste management is the entry point. The EPA's regulatory definition of a large CAFO, an animal feeding operation confining more than 1,000 animal units at 1,000 pounds each, means that a single large cattle feedlot can produce waste volumes equivalent to a small city. The USDA estimated in 2009 that confined animal operations in the United States produce approximately 500 million tons of manure annually, roughly three times the volume of human sanitary waste produced by the entire US population.[^15] That waste is typically stored in anaerobic lagoons or applied to surrounding cropland at rates that frequently exceed the land's absorptive capacity for nitrogen and phosphorus. When the application exceeds the capacity, the surplus runs off into surface water.

The water quality consequences are measurable at continental scale. Nutrient runoff from agricultural operations, of which confined animal waste is a leading component, is the primary driver of the hypoxic zone in the Gulf of Mexico, a seasonal dead zone that covered approximately 6,334 square miles in 2024 according to NOAA survey data, making it one of the largest areas of oxygen-depleted water in the world.[^16] The Chesapeake Bay, closer to the concentrated poultry production of the Delmarva Peninsula, has experienced a parallel pattern of nutrient-driven hypoxia that has degraded its fisheries for decades.

The numbers are real. What they describe is also visible. Louisiana shrimpers working the waters off the Atchafalaya Delta have watched the dead zone's expansion reshape their industry over three decades. In peak hypoxia years (midsummer, after the spring flush of nitrogen from the Mississippi watershed reaches the Gulf), trawlers that once worked nearshore waters are pushed miles further out, burning more diesel to reach the oxygenated water where the shrimp still congregate. The catch does not disappear. It migrates, and the boats follow it, and the economics tighten. In 2023, the dock-side price for Gulf brown shrimp hit its lowest point in over a decade, driven in part by import competition but compounded by the operational costs of fishing around a dead zone the size of Connecticut. The hypoxic zone is not an abstraction measured only by NOAA research vessels. It is a feature of the working landscape that the people who fish the Gulf navigate around every season, and its growth tracks the nutrient load from the agricultural system upstream with a fidelity that the science has been documenting since the early 1990s.

Greenhouse gas emissions from confined animal production are significant in the global accounting. The FAO's widely cited 2013 assessment estimated that global livestock supply chains contribute approximately 14.5 percent of all anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, with the confined production segment contributing disproportionately through enteric methane from cattle, nitrous oxide from manure management, and carbon dioxide from feed crop production and transportation.[^17] The feedlot system, specifically, contributes on the production side through methane from enteric fermentation and manure storage, and on the input side through the full emissions profile of the commodity grain system that feeds it: the diesel for planting, harvesting, and transporting the corn, the natural gas for synthesizing the nitrogen fertilizer the corn requires, and the emissions from the land-use practices under which the corn is grown.

The system sequesters no carbon. Feedlot pens are bare dirt or concrete. The land on which the animals stand contributes nothing to soil carbon accumulation. The land from which the feed is grown, under annual row-crop cultivation with conventional tillage, is losing soil carbon rather than gaining it, as the next section documents.

The environmental axis scores net negative across every metric the framework measures: water pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, soil degradation via feed production, and zero contribution to carbon sequestration.

### Survivability

The survivability axis asks what the system requires to keep functioning and what happens when those requirements are not met. For industrial feedlot production, the dependency profile is broad and deep.

The system requires continuous commodity grain inputs. A single large cattle feedlot finishing 30,000 head per year requires, at roughly six to eight pounds of grain per pound of weight gain over the finishing period, millions of pounds of corn annually.[^18] That corn must be available, at a price the feedlot can absorb, delivered on a schedule that matches the metabolic demands of the animals currently on feed. A disruption to the corn supply (drought, export-market competition, ethanol-mandate pressure on available supply) translates directly and immediately into financial stress on the feedlot.

The system requires continuous federal subsidies for its feed inputs. As the economic section documented, below-cost commodity grain has been the structural condition of the feedlot's profitability for decades. Without the commodity-support system, feed prices would reflect the full cost of production, and the feedlot's margins, already thin in most years, would narrow further or invert. The policy structure is the floor the system stands on.

The system requires continuous diesel for transportation. Commodity grain moves from the Corn Belt to feedlots across the country by truck, rail, and barge. Finished animals move from feedlots to a small number of large processing facilities. Processed meat moves from those facilities to distribution centers and retail outlets nationwide. Each link in this chain requires petroleum-derived fuel, and the chain is long. A sustained increase in diesel prices propagates through every link.

The system requires continuous antibiotics for animal health at the stocking densities the system operates at. Confining thousands of animals in close quarters at the stress levels inherent to the feedlot environment creates disease pressure that sub-therapeutic antibiotic administration manages. Remove the antibiotics and the disease pressure reasserts itself at densities that make clinical treatment of individual animals economically unfeasible.

Remove any one of these requirements — grain supply, federal subsidies, diesel, antibiotics — and the system cannot continue to function at its current scale and cost structure. This describes the system's dependency profile rather than predicting imminent failure. The system operates successfully because all four of these conditions are currently being met. Its survivability score reflects what happens when any one of them is not.

The survivability axis scores very low, not because the system is poorly managed, but because the system's design requires continuous external inputs that are historically contingent, geopolitically exposed, and, in the case of climate-dependent grain supply, increasingly volatile.

## Industrial Grain Monoculture

The second system is, in a structural sense, the first system's substrate. Industrial grain monoculture (the large-scale, chemically intensive production of corn, soybeans, and wheat that dominates the American agricultural landscape) supplies the feed for the feedlot system, the caloric base for most processed food, and the raw material for the ethanol industry. It covers roughly 250 million acres of US cropland, and the three crops together account for the majority of planted acreage in the country.[^19]

### Economic Viability

The economic picture for industrial grain monoculture mirrors the feedlot's in structure: positive at the point of sale, substantially different when externalities are accounted.

For centralized producers (the large-scale operations with enough acreage to capture economies of scale in equipment, input purchasing, and commodity marketing), the system is profitable in most years, though the margins are sensitive to commodity prices, input costs, and the federal support structure. The farm bill's commodity support programs (direct payments, counter-cyclical payments, crop insurance subsidies, and their successive reformulations) have provided a floor under commodity grain profitability for decades, smoothing the volatility that would otherwise make large-scale monoculture economically unsustainable in low-price years.[^20]

For family-scale operators and local economies, the picture is different. The consolidation of US grain production has followed the same trajectory as livestock production: fewer, larger operations producing more output. The number of farms in the United States has declined from approximately 6.8 million in 1935 to roughly 2 million today, while average farm size has more than tripled, and the majority of production value is now concentrated in a small fraction of operations.[^21] The economic benefits of the system flow disproportionately to the largest operators and to the input suppliers, processors, and commodity traders that the system depends on. Rural communities in the Corn Belt and the wheat-growing regions of the Plains have experienced the same pattern of depopulation, retail contraction, and civic thinning that the livestock consolidation produced in poultry- and pork-producing regions.

The system delivers cheap calories to urban consumers. The calories come from corn syrup, soybean oil, and refined wheat flour, and their prices have kept food expenditure as a share of household income at historically low levels. This is a real benefit to household budgets. It is also a benefit whose costs appear elsewhere: in the healthcare system, where diet-related chronic disease driven in part by the overconsumption of cheap refined carbohydrates and seed oils imposes costs that the National Institutes of Health has estimated at hundreds of billions of dollars annually; in the environmental costs documented below; and in the rural economic hollowing the consolidation has produced.[^22]

### Social Equity

The social axis for grain monoculture is structurally similar to the feedlot case: the system's benefits are distributed broadly to consumers in the form of cheap food, and its costs are concentrated in the communities where the production occurs.

Rural communities in intensive monoculture regions bear the environmental health burden: pesticide exposure, nitrate contamination of drinking water, respiratory effects from dust and chemical drift. They bear it without capturing a proportional share of the economic value the production generates. The value is captured at the processing and distribution stages, which are physically and economically distant from the fields. Farm labor in large-scale grain operations has shifted from a human workforce to a mechanical one; the tractor, the combine, and the chemical application system have replaced the hands, and the people those hands belonged to have, for the most part, moved elsewhere.

The displacement narrative for grain monoculture is quieter than for livestock. There are no CAFO siting controversies, no concentrated processing facilities with high-visibility labor conditions. The underlying structural change is the same. Fewer people are needed to produce more grain, and the communities that were organized around the people are contracting as the people leave.

### Environmental Integrity

The environmental costs of industrial grain monoculture are extensive, well documented, and, in some dimensions, cumulative in ways that compound across decades.

Topsoil loss is the foundational concern. David Pimentel, whose career at Cornell established much of the empirical basis for energy-input analysis in agriculture, estimated that US cropland loses topsoil at an average rate of roughly ten times the natural soil-formation rate, with annual erosion rates on conventionally tilled cropland averaging approximately 1.8 billion tons across the country.[^23] The Natural Resources Conservation Service's more recent modeling using the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation places the figure at roughly four to five tons per acre per year on average for conventionally managed cropland, against a natural formation rate of roughly half a ton per acre per year.[^24] The loss is quiet. It does not produce dramatic events in most years. It produces a slow, cumulative thinning of the soil horizon that reduces the land's productive capacity over generational timescales. The soil that took centuries to build is being removed in decades.

Biodiversity reduction is a direct consequence of the monoculture model. A cornfield is, by design, one species of plant over a very large area. The organisms that would normally occupy the ecological niches of a diverse plant community (the insects, the birds, the soil microbiota, the mycorrhizal fungi) are either absent or suppressed by the chemical regime required to maintain the monoculture. Tilman and colleagues' landmark 2002 paper in *Nature* documented that intensification of agricultural production practices was the single largest driver of biodiversity loss in terrestrial ecosystems globally, operating through habitat conversion, chemical inputs, and the simplification of plant communities from diverse assemblages to single-species stands.[^25]

Groundwater contamination from nitrogen fertilizer and pesticide leaching is a chronic and geographically extensive problem. Nitrate contamination of drinking water from agricultural sources affects millions of Americans, particularly in rural areas overlying the shallow aquifers of the Corn Belt and the Great Plains. The USGS National Water-Quality Assessment has documented nitrate concentrations exceeding the EPA's maximum contaminant level in a significant fraction of shallow groundwater wells in agricultural areas, with the highest concentrations correlated with the most intensive corn and soybean production.[^26]

Mycorrhizal damage from fungicide application is a less visible but ecologically significant consequence. Mycorrhizal fungi, the symbiotic networks that connect plant root systems to soil nutrient pools and to each other, are suppressed by the fungicide applications that modern grain production relies on for disease management. The disruption of mycorrhizal networks degrades the soil's capacity to cycle nutrients, retain water, and support the biological activity that builds soil organic matter over time. The consequence is a system that becomes progressively more dependent on synthetic inputs to replace the biological functions it has disabled, a dependency spiral that the environmental literature has documented across multiple cropping systems.[^27]

Nutrient runoff from grain monoculture is the leading cause of non-point source water pollution in the United States. Synthetic fertilizer is applied at rates calibrated to maximize yield in an average year, which means applied in excess of what the crop uses in any below-average year. The nitrogen and phosphorus that the crops do not absorb moves through the soil profile into groundwater and across the surface into streams, rivers, and eventually into the coastal waters where it drives the eutrophication and hypoxia described in the feedlot section. Foley and colleagues, in their 2011 *Nature* paper surveying the environmental footprint of global agriculture, identified nutrient management in intensive grain systems as one of the highest-priority leverage points for reducing agriculture's environmental impact.[^28]

The environmental axis scores net negative: topsoil loss, biodiversity reduction, groundwater contamination, mycorrhizal disruption, and nutrient runoff as the nation's leading non-point source water pollutant.

### Survivability

The survivability axis for grain monoculture reveals a dependency profile that is structurally parallel to the feedlot's.

The system requires continuous chemical inputs. Synthetic nitrogen fertilizer, produced from natural gas through the Haber-Bosch process, is the single largest energy input in grain production. Pimentel and Pimentel's analysis in *Food, Energy, and Society* documented that roughly forty percent of the total energy input in corn production is accounted for by nitrogen fertilizer alone.[^29] Without it, corn yields fall to a fraction of their current levels, because the monoculture regime has depleted the biological nitrogen-fixation capacity that diverse systems maintain through legume rotation and mycorrhizal function. The system has substituted a chemical input for a biological process, and the substitution is not reversible on a short timeline.

The system requires continuous fossil fuel for field operations and irrigation. Every pass of the tractor (planting, cultivating, applying chemicals, harvesting) requires diesel. In the western Corn Belt and the Great Plains, where rainfall is insufficient for reliable yields, irrigation from the Ogallala Aquifer and other groundwater sources requires energy for pumping, and the Ogallala itself is being drawn down at rates that exceed recharge in much of its extent, making the water supply a diminishing resource rather than a renewable one.[^30]

The Ogallala drawdown is worth pausing on, because it is the survivability axis made physically visible. The aquifer underlies approximately 174,000 square miles across eight states from South Dakota to the Texas Panhandle, and it accumulated its water over millions of years, recharged slowly by precipitation filtering through the High Plains soils above it. In southwestern Kansas (Grant, Stanton, and Haskell counties, the heart of the irrigated corn and feedlot corridor), the saturated thickness of the aquifer has declined by more than sixty percent since large-scale pumping began in the 1950s. The Kansas Geological Survey has documented wells that produced reliably for decades going dry or dropping below the threshold at which irrigation is economically viable, and the projection for much of the southwestern Kansas portion of the aquifer is functional depletion within a generation.[^30a] This is a measurement, not a speculative scenario. Farmers in the region can tell you the rate their water table is dropping; many of them track it year to year. The arithmetic is not ambiguous. The system that depends on this water is drawing down its supply faster than geology can replace it, and when the water is gone, the irrigated agriculture it supports goes with it. The dependency the survivability axis measures is, in this case, a physical quantity with a known depletion rate and a calculable endpoint.

The system requires continuous federal subsidies. Commodity grain production in the United States has operated under federal price support, crop insurance subsidy, and conservation-compliance payment structures for decades. These programs smooth the volatility of commodity markets and keep production profitable in years when market prices fall below the cost of production. Without the subsidy structure, the marginal operations, those farming the least productive land at the highest per-acre cost, would exit production, and the system's total output would contract.

The system requires continuous favorable climate for predictable yields. The cultivars planted across the Corn Belt are selected for performance under a specific range of temperature, moisture, and growing-season conditions. Deviations from the expected range produce yield losses that propagate through the entire commodity system. The 2012 drought reduced the national corn yield by roughly twenty-seven percent in a single season.[^31] Climate projections for the central United States indicate increasing frequency and severity of growing-season heat stress and drought stress over the coming decades, which means the climate condition the system was optimized for is itself becoming less reliable.[^32]

Remove any one of these (chemical inputs, fossil fuel, federal subsidies, favorable climate) and the system cannot continue to produce at its current scale and cost structure. As with the feedlot system, this maps the dependency profile rather than predicting when the disruption occurs. The system's survivability score reflects the breadth and depth of its dependencies, each of which is contingent on conditions that are volatile, contested, or trending in directions unfavorable to the system's continuation in its current form.

## The Improvement Acknowledgment

I want to register something that the axis-by-axis scoring, taken alone, might obscure.

Industrial grain monoculture is not a fixed technology. It is a set of practices, and the practices are changing. Cover cropping means planting a secondary crop between cash-crop seasons to hold soil, fix nitrogen, and maintain mycorrhizal networks. The practice has expanded substantially in the United States over the last decade, and the evidence for its benefits across multiple dimensions is strong and growing. The USDA's Census of Agriculture reported that cover crop acreage in the US increased from roughly 10 million acres in 2012 to over 18 million acres by 2022, a pace of adoption that suggests the practice is moving from the margins of the system toward something closer to standard practice in progressive operations.[^33] Reduced tillage and no-till systems, which leave crop residue on the soil surface rather than turning it under, reduce erosion rates dramatically and improve soil biological activity. Integration of perennial vegetation into row-crop systems (buffer strips, alley cropping, silvopasture adjacent to grain fields) can restore some of the habitat complexity and biological function that pure monoculture eliminates. Precision agriculture, variable-rate application of inputs guided by GPS mapping and soil sensing, can reduce fertilizer and pesticide use per unit of output by applying inputs where the crop needs them rather than uniformly across the field.

These improvements are real, and they are meaningful. Gabe Brown's operation near Bismarck, North Dakota, is the case that cover-crop advocates cite most often, and the case is worth citing because the numbers are specific. Brown transitioned a 5,000-acre grain and livestock operation from conventional tillage and continuous monoculture to a no-till, diverse cover-crop, integrated-grazing system over roughly two decades. His soil organic matter measurements, documented in NRCS technical visits and in Brown's own published accounting,[^34] rose from less than two percent to over six percent on fields that had been under the system longest. His input costs dropped substantially: no synthetic fertilizer purchases, no fungicide, no insecticide, herbicide use reduced to a fraction of the county average. His water infiltration rates, a direct measure of soil biological health, increased severalfold, meaning his fields absorb rainfall that would run off a conventionally managed field carrying topsoil and nitrogen with it. The operation is profitable, and the profitability does not depend on commodity support payments in the way his neighbors' operations do, because his input costs are low enough that the margin holds without the subsidy floor.[^35]

Brown's case is not typical. It is a best-case demonstration of what the improving trajectory produces when a skilled operator commits to the transition fully. But it establishes that a grain monoculture operated with winter cover cropping, no-till practices, precision nutrient application, and integrated perennial buffer strips would score materially better on the environmental axis than the same acreage operated under conventional practices. It would build soil rather than lose it, support biological activity rather than suppress it, and reduce nutrient runoff rather than contribute to it. The practices exist, they work, and their adoption is expanding.

The book does not present monocultures as categorically destructive. It presents the current dominant industrial implementation, the version of monoculture that operates across most of the 250 million acres of US cropland without these improvements, as scoring poorly on the four-axis framework for specific, structural reasons. The distinction matters because the structural analysis and the technological trajectory are both true at the same time. The dominant implementation has the vulnerabilities the framework identifies. The improving implementation has the potential the evidence supports. Both findings are accurate. They describe different populations of practice on the same landscape.

What the improvement trajectory does not resolve, however, is the survivability question. A cover-cropped, no-till, precision-managed corn operation is still a corn operation. It still requires seed, fertilizer, and diesel, in reduced quantities but still required, and a climate regime compatible with corn production. It still sells into a commodity market whose price is shaped by global supply and demand and by federal policy. The improvements narrow the dependency profile. They reduce the system's dependence on any single input by making the system more biologically self-sustaining. But they do not eliminate the profile. A system that depends on fewer external inputs is more survivable than one that depends on more, and the improving monoculture is more survivable than the conventional one. It is not, by the framework's measure, as survivable as a system that depends on very few external inputs and can tolerate the loss of any one of them without collapsing. The improvement is real. The structural constraint remains.

## What the Scoring Shows

Two systems, eight axis scores, one pattern.

Industrial feedlot animal production and industrial grain monoculture both score positively on the economic axis at the point of sale, and both score negatively when externalized costs are accounted. Both score net negative on the social axis, with benefits flowing to consumers and corporate entities and costs concentrated in workers, adjacent communities, and the rural regions whose economies the systems have restructured. Both score net negative on the environmental axis across essentially every metric. Both score very low on the survivability axis, with dependency profiles that are broad, deep, and contingent on conditions that are volatile.

The pattern is structural rather than incidental. The two systems were not designed by malevolent architects. They evolved through seventy years of economic incentive, agricultural policy, technological development, and consumer demand, each reinforcing the others, producing a food system that is extraordinarily efficient at converting fossil energy and federal dollars into cheap calories under stable conditions. That efficiency is real. It feeds people. The framework does not deny this. What the framework adds is the observation that the efficiency is built on a specific set of conditions, and that the conditions are not guaranteed.

The question the scoring raises is whether an alternative exists that escapes these structural constraints: a food system that scores well on the first three axes without depending on the specific set of external conditions that make the industrial system's performance possible. The next chapter takes up that question, and the answer it finds is instructive in a way I did not expect when I began this work. The leading alternative (regenerative agriculture, holistic management, the pasture-based model) scores substantially better than industrial production on every axis. It also scores better on the survivability axis, which is the axis where the industrial system is most vulnerable. But it does not score as well on survivability as its advocates might hope, and the reason it does not is a structural one that the framework makes visible in a way that three-axis assessment cannot.

The industrial system's survivability deficit is the floor. The question is what can be built above it.
