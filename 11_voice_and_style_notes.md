# Voice and Style Notes

**Purpose:** This file describes the voice the book should be written in, with concrete examples drawn from the author's current published work. It is the positive companion to the anti-voice reference (`16_anti_voice_reference.md`).

**How to use:** Read this file in full before drafting any chapter. After drafting, read the draft against the patterns described here. If a passage does not feel like it belongs in the same book as the example passages, revise.

## I. Register

The book sits at two related but distinguishable registers, calibrated by chapter type.

### Higher register: framework and analysis chapters

Used for Chapters 4, 5, 9, 10, 12, 13, and the analytical portions of any chapter. This is the register of the microbiome piece.

The defining qualities:

- Sentences carry their own reasoning rather than asserting and defending
- First-person is present but does not call attention to itself
- Specialized vocabulary appears where the subject matter requires it, without softening
- Concession to opposing views is structural, not occasional
- Long compound sentences alternate with short crystallizing ones

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> Consider the metallomics bubble in the diagram. Not many endometriosis talks would mention metallomics – the role of trace metals – but here it was front and center. Why? By incorporating metallomics into the framework, I could illustrate a truly interdisciplinary scaffold: one in which a gynecologist, a microbiologist, an immunologist, and an environmental health scientist would all see their respective pieces of the puzzle, as well as how those pieces connect.

Notice: a short opening directive, a parenthetical clarification, a single-word question doing real work, and then a long sentence that does the explanatory lifting. Four sentences, four different lengths, each doing a different job.

### Lower register: personal scenes and the frame

Used for the Prologue, Chapter 1 (the meat counter), Chapter 7 (the Savory food truck), and the Epilogue. This is the register of the ivermectin commentary.

The defining qualities:

- More direct sentence construction
- Personal narrative carried in plain language without being romanticized
- Confrontation handled by adjusting standing of the exchange rather than by attacking the person
- First-person more present, but still not performative
- The reader is addressed as someone who is following along, not someone being persuaded

**Example from the ivermectin commentary:**

> It's out of character for me to correct misspellings or grammatical errors because it's pedantic. However, since you admit to marginalizing my position and credibility for this reason, I suppose you have to intellectually tangle with me now that we are on equal footing, then: it's "label", not "lable." You walked yourself into that one.

Notice: the sentences are shorter and more direct than the framework chapters use, but the voice is unmistakably the same person. The confrontation does not produce hostility. It produces a recalibration of the conversation.

### Range, not jump

The two registers are calibrations of one voice, not two voices. A chapter should not jump between them within a single section. Chapter 1 (the meat counter) sits primarily in the lower register but moves into the higher register in its closing analytical paragraphs. Chapter 5 (scoring industrial systems) sits primarily in the higher register but allows the lower register in its concrete examples. The transition is gradual, not abrupt.

## II. Sentence Patterns

### The long-then-short rhythm

The author's current voice uses a structural pattern: long compound sentences for working through reasoning, followed by short sentences for arrival.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> Teaching someone what to think yields a finite answer (which might become obsolete or may not generalize). Teaching someone how to think equips them with a process to find new answers endlessly.

Two sentences, both medium-length, but the second one *lands* — it's the crystallization the first sentence was building toward.

**Another example, with greater length variation:**

> But allow me to be clear—it is exactly this thought process that drives innovation. It's the same process behind notable scientific breakthroughs, where old models were transcended rather than tweaked.

The em dash interruption signals that what follows is the point. Then the second sentence extends the claim with its consequence.

**Drafting application:** When a paragraph is making an argument, the structural shape should usually be longer sentences doing the work and shorter sentences marking arrival. A paragraph composed entirely of medium-length sentences feels muddled because nothing rises above the rest. A paragraph composed entirely of short sentences feels staccato and assertive in the wrong way (this is the 2012 manuscript's failure mode).

### Sentences that show their own thinking

The author's current voice writes sentences that *enact* the reasoning rather than reporting its conclusions.

**Example from the ivermectin commentary:**

> It is possible that although a drug didn't have a direct effect on the pathogen itself, in vivo, there may have been an additional environmental factor that explains efficacy, or a different target that was hit that allowed greater viral clearance.

This sentence does not say "drugs can have indirect effects." It walks through the thought: it is possible — although the direct effect was absent — that something else might have been operating — and here are the candidates. The reader follows the reasoning rather than evaluating a finished claim.

**Drafting application:** Where the book is making a non-obvious analytical move, the sentence carrying it should show the reasoning, not just announce the conclusion. This slows the prose down in the right way — readers move through analytical chapters at the speed of thought rather than at the speed of skimming.

### Em dashes used purposefully

The current voice uses em dashes to insert clarification, qualification, or emphasis without breaking the sentence's structural integrity.

**From the microbiome piece:**

> What I actually presented was a systems-level integration of multiple research domains, centered on a visual "Microbiome Signature of Endometriosis" diagram.

> Each bubble in that diagram represented a different disciplinary domain – environmental toxicology, microbiome signatures findings, metallomic signatures findings, microbial metallomics, immunology, estrobolomics and metabolomics, and microbial ecology – all interconnected as a scaffold for generating hypotheses, building disease models, and designing interventions.

The em dashes set off a list mid-sentence, allowing the sentence's main structure to remain visible. The reader can follow both the local content and the larger sentence shape.

**Drafting application:** Em dashes are appropriate for parenthetical material that is too important for parentheses but not important enough for its own sentence. Use them to insert specifics, examples, or qualifications that would otherwise force a fragmented sentence structure.

## III. How the First-Person Operates

### The "I" is present but not performed

The author's current voice uses "I" frequently but never as a flourish. The "I" appears when it is doing genuine work — claiming an experience, taking a position, naming a stance — and is otherwise absent.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> Although I did touch on the fact that I personally had resolved multiple chronic conditions over a decade ago, I also made it clear that the talk was not about my personal recovery methods or my n=1 experiments. It was about the thought process that led to integrative insights in research.

The "I" appears three times in the first sentence because the sentence is *about* the author's actions. The second sentence drops "I" because it is making a claim about content rather than about authorship. This is the right pattern.

**Failure mode to avoid:** "I" used as a soft transition or as a way to lower the stakes of a claim. "I think the framework matters" is weaker than "the framework matters" if what follows is an argument rather than a personal preference.

### Personal narrative as evidence, not as memoir

The author's current voice uses personal narrative when it does specific argumentative work and then moves on.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> I briefly referenced my personal health journey – resolving multiple chronic conditions by following evidence across disciplines – as an existence proof that there was real power in this systematic thinking approach.

Notice the framing: the personal material is described as evidence ("an existence proof"), not as story. The sentence acknowledges the personal material exists, says what it was for, and moves on. The book should do the same — when personal narrative appears, it should be doing argumentative work, not adding texture.

This is true of the meat counter scene, the Savory food truck scene, and the camels-and-osage-orange longing in the prologue and epilogue. Each of these is personal material doing specific argumentative work. None of them is memoir for its own sake.

## IV. How Confrontation Works

The author's current voice can be confrontational without becoming hostile. The mechanism is to *adjust the standing of the exchange* rather than to attack the interlocutor.

**Example from the ivermectin commentary:**

> It's out of character for me to correct misspellings or grammatical errors because it's pedantic. However, since you admit to marginalizing my position and credibility for this reason, I suppose you have to intellectually tangle with me now that we are on equal footing, then: it's "label", not "lable." You walked yourself into that one.

The interlocutor had used the author's grammar as grounds to dismiss her position. The response does not call the interlocutor an idiot. It identifies what the interlocutor did, follows their own logic to its consequence, and now requires the interlocutor to engage on equal footing. The "you walked yourself into that one" is sharp but accurate; the interlocutor did walk themselves into that one.

**Drafting application:** Where the book disagrees with a named figure (the Salatin chapter, the engagement with Pleistocene rewilding's critics, the implicit disagreement with the dominant grain-based sustainability framing), the model is the same. Identify what the interlocutor has claimed. Take their claim seriously in its strongest form. Show what follows from it. Engage on the merits.

The model is not "you are wrong because you are stupid." The model is "your argument has these specific consequences, which I want to walk through with you."

### The rhetorical question, used substantively

The author's current voice asks rhetorical questions only at moments where the reader should actually pause to evaluate.

**Examples from the microbiome piece:**

> "Is this good science?"

> "Is a lack of curiosity, in the face of something so surprising, good science?"

These are placed at the ends of paragraphs that have done substantial argumentative work. They ask the reader to render a verdict on what they have just read. They are not filler.

**Failure mode to avoid:** The 2012 manuscript used rhetorical questions constantly, often as transitions or as snide commentary on opposing positions. The current voice uses them sparingly and substantively. A chapter should contain at most two or three rhetorical questions, each placed at a moment of genuine evaluative weight.

## V. How Concession Works

The author's current voice concedes the strength of opposing positions before pushing past them. This is structural, not occasional.

**Examples from the microbiome piece:**

> "Granted, thinking in systems is harder than thinking in checklists."

> "While rigor and skepticism are pillars of science, they should not become blinders that prevent us from seeing the forest for the trees."

> "Such conditioning is reinforced by how we're taught to evaluate information."

> "An interdisciplinary, hypothesis-generating talk can feel 'unsolidified' by comparison."

In each case, the author acknowledges what is genuinely true about the position she is about to argue past. This is not appeasement. It is intellectual seriousness — the recognition that the position she is critiquing has strengths, and that engaging with the strongest version of it is the only honest way to argue.

**Drafting application:** Every chapter that critiques an existing framework, figure, or practice should include explicit concession of what is genuinely strong about the thing being critiqued. The Salatin chapter requires this. The chapter on industrial systems requires this. The chapter engaging Pleistocene rewilding's critics requires this.

The concession is not performative. It is intellectually substantive. The reader should leave each critical chapter understanding what is strong about the position being critiqued, not just what is weak about it.

## VI. How Specialized Vocabulary Operates

The author's current voice uses specialized vocabulary directly, without apology or softening.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> Each domain bubble – be it Environmental Toxicants, Gut/Vaginal Microbiome, Trace Metals, Immune Dysfunction, or Microbial Ecology – was supported by specific studies. More importantly, the interdisciplinary intersections between those bubbles are where new hypotheses bloom.

> By incorporating metallomics into the framework, I could illustrate a truly interdisciplinary scaffold: one in which a gynecologist, a microbiologist, an immunologist, and an environmental health scientist would all see their respective pieces of the puzzle.

The terms are used because they are correct. Where definition is needed, it is provided in line ("metallomics – the role of trace metals"). Where definition is not needed, the term stands.

**Drafting application:** The book engages technical material — paleontology, ecology, agricultural science, microbiome science. The voice should use the correct technical vocabulary directly. Where a term will be unfamiliar to a general reader, define it in line, briefly, and continue. Do not over-define. Do not signal embarrassment about using technical language. Do not dumb down where precision is required.

The reader the book is written for is intelligent and willing to learn new terms. Trust them.

## VII. How Citations Operate in the Voice

The author's current voice cites sources in line, often with the source's argument briefly summarized as part of the prose.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> A 2025 review reinforces this view, calling endometriosis a "chronic, inflammatory, and heterogeneous condition" with systemic impacts, not solely a localized gynecological disease.

> A 2023 review in Pathogens bluntly pointed out that our "continued reliance on taxonomic indicators to define microbiomes" has become an impediment – it can confound analyses and cause us to miss true causal relationships.

The citation is integrated into the sentence's argument. The reader learns what the source says and how it supports the larger point in the same breath. The footnote provides the reference; the prose does the intellectual work.

**Drafting application:** Citations in the book should be integrated this way wherever possible. "Studies show" is not adequate. "A 2024 review by [author] found that…" is the floor. The footnote contains the full reference; the sentence contains the substantive content.

This is also why every scientific claim must have a real citation: the voice depends on being able to point to specific findings by specific authors. Vague attribution breaks the voice and the credibility together.

## VIII. Specific Lexical Choices

A short list of vocabulary patterns to follow:

### Words and phrases the current voice uses comfortably

- "Granted," "However," "Nonetheless," "Indeed" as transitional moves
- "It is possible that…" as a hedge that signals genuine uncertainty
- "Notice that…" as a directive to the reader to attend to what was just said
- "I dare say" as a soft escalation of a claim
- "Allow me to be clear" or "Let me be clear" as a marker that what follows is the central claim
- "It is entirely possible that…" as an opening for an alternative explanation
- "This is precisely…" as a moment of arrival
- Concrete proper nouns (specific people, specific studies, specific events) used freely

### Words and phrases to suppress (most are also covered in the anti-voice reference)

- "Folks" — too informal for the book's register
- "Look," as a sentence opener — performs informality without doing work
- "The thing is" — colloquial filler
- "Honestly," "frankly," "to be honest" — these signal that the rest of the prose was less honest, which is not the case
- "Just" used as a softener ("I just think," "we just need to") — weakens claims unnecessarily
- "Really" used as intensifier — almost always cuttable
- Exclamation points — almost never appropriate in the book's register
- "Of course" used as a way to elide reasoning — if it is of course, demonstrate why

## IX. Paragraph Construction

The author's current voice writes paragraphs with internal architecture: an opening claim or observation, development through one or two sentences of reasoning or evidence, and a closing sentence that either crystallizes the paragraph's point or hands off to the next.

**Example from the microbiome piece:**

> One of the key messages was that the real value lies in demonstrating how to approach complex problems, not prescribing exactly what to think or do in every scenario— because those prescriptions effectively squander creative intelligence.

> This was a talk about mindset and methodology more than a list of results. I briefly referenced my personal health journey – resolving multiple chronic conditions by following evidence across disciplines – as an existence proof that there was real power in this systematic thinking approach. How else could I have had such drastic improvements, when modern medicine doesn't see this kind of resolution?

> Nonetheless, I did not explicitly share my personal regimen or a cookbook for others to copy.

Three paragraphs. The first opens a thread. The second develops it through specific evidence. The third hands off to the next section by stating what was *not* done, which sets up what follows. Each paragraph is a unit; the units stack into an argument.

**Drafting application:** Paragraphs in the book should be coherent units. A paragraph that contains an opening claim, a single piece of supporting evidence or reasoning, and a closing sentence is doing its job. Paragraphs that contain too many ideas should be broken up. Paragraphs that contain only assertions without development should be expanded or cut.

Length: paragraphs in the framework chapters should average four to seven sentences. Paragraphs in the personal-narrative chapters can be shorter. One-sentence paragraphs are appropriate occasionally, for emphasis or transition; they should not be a habit.

## X. Pace and Density

The author's current voice writes at a consistent pace — neither rushed nor slow. The microbiome piece runs roughly 4,000 words and develops a complex framework without ever feeling either too fast (skipping over necessary steps) or too slow (over-explaining).

The mechanism is calibration of *density*. Each paragraph carries roughly one major idea, supported by one or two pieces of evidence or reasoning, with appropriate connection to what came before and what follows. The reader is never asked to hold more than one major idea per paragraph, but is also never given less than that.

**Drafting application:** Chapters in the book should aim for this consistent density. A 5,000-word chapter contains roughly twenty-five major ideas, each carried by a paragraph, sequenced into an argument. Chapters that try to carry more than that become exhausting. Chapters that carry less become thin.

When in doubt, prefer fewer ideas more thoroughly developed over more ideas thinly sketched.

## XI. The Voice's Underlying Posture

Beneath the specific patterns, the author's current voice has a consistent underlying posture that is worth naming explicitly:

**The voice trusts the reader.** It does not over-explain, does not lecture, does not perform expertise. It assumes the reader is intelligent, paying attention, and capable of following an argument that takes some work.

**The voice is calibrated about what it knows.** It states confidently what it has good grounds to claim and hedges genuinely where uncertainty exists. The reader can trust the difference because the voice is not constantly inflating its certainty.

**The voice is willing to be wrong.** It engages opposing positions in their strongest form, concedes what is genuinely true about them, and pushes past them only on the grounds it can actually defend. It does not need to defeat opposing positions; it only needs to make its own case.

**The voice is interested in the subject matter.** Curiosity is present throughout. The reader can feel that the author cares about the questions being asked and finds them genuinely worth pursuing.

**The voice is generous with credit.** Specific people, specific studies, specific predecessors are named. The voice does not pretend to have invented what it has built upon.

**The voice has revised itself over time and acknowledges this.** Where the author has changed her mind, she says so. Self-revision is treated as evidence of seriousness, not as an admission of weakness.

These postures are not separable from the voice. They produce it.

## XII. Compliance Process

Before drafting any chapter:

1. Read this file in full.
2. Read the anti-voice reference (`16_anti_voice_reference.md`) in full.
3. Identify which register (higher framework / lower personal) the chapter operates in primarily.
4. Identify which postures from Section XI are most relevant to the chapter's content.

After drafting:

1. Read the draft against this file's patterns. Specifically check:
   - Are sentences carrying their own reasoning, or just asserting conclusions?
   - Is the long-then-short rhythm present?
   - Is the first-person doing work, or merely present?
   - Are concessions to opposing positions structurally integrated, or absent?
   - Are rhetorical questions placed substantively, or used as filler?
   - Is the technical vocabulary used directly, or softened?
2. Read the draft against the anti-voice reference. Search for any forbidden patterns or phrases.
3. Read three random paragraphs aloud. They should sound like the same person who wrote the microbiome piece and the ivermectin commentary. If they don't, revise.
