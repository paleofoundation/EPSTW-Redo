# Chapter 11: Bioregional and Urban

The framework this book has developed does not produce a single answer. It produces different answers for different places, and the difference is the framework working correctly rather than a weakness in it.

The previous chapters built the argument in general terms: a four-axis assessment, a proxy-selection principle guided by the fossil record, a set of restoration frameworks (Pleistocene, Indigenous, marine) that converge on the proposition that food systems organized around ecological function score better across every axis than food systems organized around industrial inputs. The argument, as stated, is continental in scope. It applies, in principle, to every landscape on the continent. What it does not do, stated at that level, is tell anyone what the argument means for the specific piece of ground they are standing on. A rancher in southern Arizona, a fisherman on the Gulf Coast, a family in the hardwood hills of New England, and a software engineer in a Chicago apartment all inhabit different ecological realities, and the framework, if it is worth anything, must be able to speak to each of them in terms that are specific rather than abstract.

This chapter does two things. First, it walks through specific bioregional examples to show what the four-axis framework looks like when it is applied to particular ground: specific landscapes, specific species assemblages, specific food-system possibilities. Second, it addresses the readers who do not have ground to apply it to: the urban readers, the apartment dwellers, the people whose relationship to the food system runs through a grocery store rather than through a pasture. Most readers of this book will be those people. The chapter takes them seriously.

## The Arid Southwest

Chapter 7 established the proxy-selection argument for the Southwest through the Savory food-truck conversation and the Grand Canyon fossil record. I will not repeat that material here. What I want to do is take the argument one step further: from the proxy-selection principle to a worked picture of what a food system guided by that principle would look like across the region.

The arid Southwest (the Sonoran and Chihuahuan basins, the Colorado Plateau, the sky-island ranges of southeastern Arizona and southwestern New Mexico) is a landscape whose vegetation is still calibrated to animals that have been absent for twelve thousand years. The thorny, browse-heavy plant communities that dominate the region (creosote, mesquite, catclaw acacia, palo verde, saltbush, yucca, prickly pear) evolved under browsing pressure from camelids and ground sloths whose foraging kept the woody vegetation in check and whose dung cycled nutrients through soils that receive less than twelve inches of rainfall per year.[^1] The current management regime puts cattle on this landscape. The cattle graze what grass exists, avoid the browse, and leave the woody species to expand unchecked. The result, documented across decades of range-science literature, is mesquite encroachment: the steady conversion of open grassland to dense mesquite thicket, driven not by climate change but by the absence of the browsers that historically suppressed the mesquite.[^2] The range is degrading not because it is being used but because it is being used by the wrong animal.

A food system guided by the fossil record would look different. Dromedary camels browsing mesquite and palo verde on Sonoran rangeland; Bactrian camels in the cold-winter and saline basins to the north and west. Horses and burros (already present in the region by the hundreds of thousands, currently managed as nuisance populations on BLM land) reframed as proxy species occupying an ecological role their lineage held for millions of years before a twelve-thousand-year interruption.[^3] The browse species that cattle refuse are the species the fossil record's animals consumed. The mesquite pods that accumulate uneaten under the current regime are a calorie-dense feed source for camelids whose digestive systems evolved to process exactly that kind of material. The prickly pear fruits that drop and rot are fruits the ground sloths once dispersed. The landscape is not short of food; it is short of the animals the food was made for.

The products of such a system would be unfamiliar to the American consumer but not to the global one. Camel milk (higher in fat and protein than cow's milk, lower in lactose, and already a significant commercial product in the Middle East, East Africa, and parts of Central Asia) is produced by an animal that thrives on arid-adapted vegetation without irrigation or supplemental feed.[^4] Horse and burro meat, while culturally unfamiliar in the United States, is consumed widely in Europe, Central Asia, and South America, and the protein is lean, low in intramuscular fat, and nutritionally comparable to venison. The cultural barriers are real, and I do not dismiss them. The ecological logic is also real. The framework does not insist that everyone eat camel. It observes that a food system organized around the animals the Southwest's landscape evolved under would score better on every axis (environmental integrity, survivability, and the ecological dimension of social equity) than the cattle-based system currently imposed on that landscape.

> **DEFINITION: Bactrian and Dromedary**
>
> The proxy literature names "camels" as the candidate replacement for *Camelops*, and the book has followed that convention. The proxy decision is, in fact, two decisions. There are two living Old World camel species, they occupy distinct ecological niches, they tolerate different climates, and the difference matters for which American landscape gets which animal.
>
> The dromedary, *Camelus dromedarius*, is the single-humped camel of the Sahara, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Horn of Africa. It is heat-adapted: a sparse coat, body mass typically four to seven hundred kilograms, and a metabolism optimized for high-temperature, low-humidity environments. Dromedaries thrive on hot-desert browse, including acacia, mesquite, palo verde, and the thorny succulents, and tolerate water deprivation that would kill cattle or sheep within days. They are the species the Saudi-Gulf trade is built around, and the species the US Camel Corps experimented with in the 1850s.
>
> The Bactrian, *Camelus bactrianus*, is the two-humped camel of the Central Asian steppe, the Gobi, and the Mongolian and northern Chinese deserts. It is the wide-temperature-tolerance specialist of the camelid lineage. A dense winter coat, body mass commonly six hundred to a thousand kilograms, and a tolerance range that runs from forty degrees below zero in steppe winters to over a hundred degrees Fahrenheit in summer rangeland use. Bactrians eat plants other camels treat as marginal: saxaul, salt-stressed *Atriplex*, and the halophytic shrubs of the Amaranthaceae and Chenopodiaceae families that store sodium at concentrations toxic to most herbivores. They drink saline water that other livestock will not, with documented tolerance above two percent salinity. The Bactrian's foot pad, broader and heavier than the dromedary's, also performs a service the dromedary does less of: it fractures the biological soil crusts and physical caps that form on overgrazed and compacted desert ground, opening the surface to water infiltration and seedling establishment.
>
> The halophyte-eating ability is the unsung edge. Halophytes (*Atriplex*, *Salicornia*, *Halocnemum*, *Salsola*) accumulate salt in their tissues at concentrations that exclude most herbivores from using them. The plants are dominant on salt-affected soils across the western United States: irrigated farmland where a century of input water has accumulated sodium and chloride beyond what conventional crops can tolerate, and natural saline playas where the geology has produced the same outcome. The Bactrian, alone among large livestock species, can convert halophyte biomass into meat, milk, and other commodities while removing salt from the system through the animal's tissues and excreted in urine. This is phytoremediation with a productive output, and the working land it could be applied to is not abstract: several million acres of the western US carry soil salinity sufficient to limit conventional agriculture, and most of that acreage is not currently producing food.
>
> The utility extends well beyond restoration. Camel meat is lean, high in protein, and an established staple across much of the Old World. Camel milk is higher in protein and several minerals than cow's milk, lower in lactose, and increasingly used in the United States by the autism community for behavioral and gastrointestinal symptoms; the demand is anecdotal in clinical literature but real and rising in the marketplace. Lactoferrin, a milk protein with antimicrobial, antiviral, and immunomodulatory activity, occurs in camel milk at high concentration; recent work has identified that camel tears carry it as well, and a small extraction industry has begun to form around it. Bactrian wool, comparable in fineness to cashmere, supports the substantial commercial fiber industry of Mongolia and northern China. The animal is a multi-product animal in a regulatory environment that does not yet recognize multi-product animals as a category.
>
> The bioregional decision follows. The hot southern deserts (Sonoran, Chihuahuan, lower Mojave) are dromedary country: the climate matches the species. The cold-winter Great Basin, the Colorado Plateau, the Wyoming Red Desert, and the saline-affected playas across the basin-and-range are Bactrian country: the cold tolerance, the halophyte browse, the soil-crust breaking, and the salt-water tolerance all line up. *Camelops*, the extinct American camel the proposal is actually about, was probably closer to the Bactrian in body mass and in temperature tolerance. The right answer to "which camel" is therefore both, allocated by landscape.

> **CASE: A Million Camels Looking for a Continent**
>
> The supply question for the Southwest's camelid scenario has a counterintuitive answer: there are too many camels in the world already, and one continent is killing them rather than using them.
>
> Australia carries the world's largest population of feral dromedaries, descended from camels imported in the nineteenth century to support exploration and freight across the interior, released into the bush after the rail and motor lines came in, and reproducing across a continent whose vegetation suits them well. By the late 2000s, federal and state surveys placed the population at approaching one million animals, expanding at roughly ten percent per year, and producing the kind of damage to fences, water infrastructure, and native vegetation that puts a species into the legal category of pest.
>
> The federal response was the Australian Feral Camel Management Project, a four-year program (2009–2013) funded at roughly nineteen million Australian dollars, which culled more than one hundred forty thousand camels, primarily through aerial gunning from helicopters across the central deserts. The program reduced the population to an estimated three hundred thousand by program end. By 2020, with reproduction outpacing residual culling effort, the population had recovered enough that the Anangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara Lands authorized an emergency cull during drought, killing approximately ten thousand additional camels over five days when the animals began damaging community water infrastructure searching for moisture. The current population is again estimated in the high hundreds of thousands and rising.
>
> The Gulf states are running the inverse experiment. Saudi Arabia is the world's largest importer of camel meat and live camels, anchored in a regional camel-meat economy that runs through the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait, and Bahrain at multi-billion-dollar scale. The kingdom's interest is not nostalgic. Camel meat is a staple protein, camel milk a premium commodity, and camel racing a substantial industry. The trade has scaled to the point that the regional shipping infrastructure now includes dedicated multi-deck livestock-carrier vessels (purpose-built ships, not chartered aircraft) moving live animals across the Indian Ocean at volumes that air freight cannot economically support.
>
> The contrast is the same contrast the feral-hog discussion will draw at smaller scale and inside one country. Australia treats the surplus animal as a damage vector and pays its taxpayers to kill it. Saudi Arabia treats the same animal as a food source and invests in infrastructure to import it. The United States, which has the landscape that wants the animal and a regulatory architecture that does not yet have a category for it, treats the question as not yet asked. Three continents, three outcomes, one species.
>
> The animal-welfare reading writes itself. If the choice for a particular cohort of feral Australian camels is between aerial gunning and live transport across an ocean, the second outcome is the better outcome for the animal. If the choice is between aerial gunning and a transport pathway that ends with the camels released onto American Pleistocene-functional rangeland under a research protocol of the kind Chapter Thirteen describes, the gap between actual and possible widens further.
>
> The supply problem the Southwest scenario faces is not, in the end, a supply problem. It is a regulatory and logistical problem: which animals are permitted to cross which oceans on which paperwork. The animals exist. The market for them exists. The transport infrastructure exists. What does not yet exist is the American regulatory category that would permit the third option.

The survivability advantage is particularly sharp. A camel on Sonoran rangeland requires no irrigation, no commodity feed, no antibiotics, and no subsidized grain. It drinks infrequently, tolerates heat that would kill cattle, and feeds itself on vegetation the landscape produces without human input. The dependency profile is as thin as anything the framework can measure. When the corn supply is disrupted, when the diesel price spikes, when the aquifer drops another foot, the camel is still eating mesquite. The landscape is still producing. The system is still functioning, because the system's primary input is the landscape itself, and the landscape has been producing browse for twelve thousand years without asking anyone for help.

## The Northern Great Plains

The Great Plains are the bioregion where the framework's prescription comes closest to what is already being practiced. The shortgrass prairie evolved under bison, and bison are still here: roughly 500,000 of them, most in commercial herds, roughly 20,000 in conservation herds managed for genetic diversity and ecological function.[^5] The proxy-selection question, for this landscape, is largely answered. The animal the prairie evolved under is the animal that should be on the prairie. The question is not whether bison belong on the Plains but what happens when you take the relationship seriously.

American Prairie, in northeastern Montana, is the most ambitious attempt to answer that question on the ground. The project, which began acquiring private ranchland and grazing leases in Phillips County in 2004, is assembling a contiguous landscape of more than three million acres (private land, BLM grazing allotments, and the Charles M. Russell National Wildlife Refuge) and managing it for a functioning prairie ecosystem with bison as the keystone grazer. The herd, as of 2025, numbers roughly 800 animals on approximately 90,000 acres of deeded and leased land, managed under a protocol that allows the bison to graze freely across the landscape without the rotational confinement that cattle operations impose. The results are visible from satellite imagery: the bison-grazed sections show a mosaic of close-cropped and tall-grass patches (the heterogeneous structure the prairie's plant diversity depends on) that the adjacent cattle-grazed sections, managed under standard rotational grazing, do not produce. The difference is the behavior of the animal, not management philosophy. Bison graze selectively, return to preferred patches repeatedly, wallow in depressions that become ephemeral wetlands used by breeding shorebirds and amphibians, and move across the landscape at scales that create the spatial variability the prairie's full community of grasses, forbs, insects, and ground-nesting birds requires. Cattle, even under the best rotational management, do not produce this mosaic because they are not bison and do not behave like bison on grass.

The food-system dimension of American Prairie is still developing, but the economics of bison production on native grassland are already legible from the commercial herds that have been operating across the northern Plains for decades. A bison cow on native prairie feeds herself year-round. She requires no commodity grain, no supplemental hay in most winters if the range is adequate, no antibiotics (bison are notably resistant to the respiratory diseases that plague cattle in confined conditions), and no subsidized inputs of any kind. The animal's maintenance cost is the grass, and the grass is what the prairie produces without being asked. The calves grow on their mothers' milk and on the prairie's forage, and the harvested animals (processed through the small but growing network of bison-certified processing facilities in Montana, South Dakota, and Colorado) produce a lean, nutrient-dense meat that commands a retail premium of roughly two to three times the price of commodity beef. The premium reflects consumer demand for a product perceived as healthier and more environmentally responsible, and it is large enough to sustain the operations at their current scale.

The framework adds specificity to what this arrangement means in survivability terms. The cattle ranching economy of the northern Plains operates under the dependency structure the book has documented: commodity markets set the price, federal subsidies smooth the volatility, and the rancher's margin depends on conditions the rancher does not control. A bison operation on native prairie operates under a different structure. The animal feeds itself on the prairie's own production. The harvest is governed by the herd's population dynamics and the range's carrying capacity rather than by the commodity cycle. The ecological services the bison provide while alive (the grazing that maintains grass vigor, the wallowing, the selective foraging) are not incidental. They are the ecological process that keeps the prairie functioning as prairie, and the food the operation produces is a byproduct of that maintenance rather than an extraction from it. The survivability score is high not because bison are exotic or novel but because the relationship between bison and prairie is the oldest working partnership on the landscape, and it requires the least from outside itself to keep functioning.

The tribal dimension is equally important and should not be treated as secondary. The InterTribal Buffalo Council, a consortium of more than seventy tribal nations, manages bison herds across reservations from Montana to Oklahoma, and the herds serve cultural, ecological, and food-sovereignty purposes simultaneously. For the Blackfeet, the Fort Peck Assiniboine and Sioux, the Rosebud Sioux, and other northern Plains nations, the bison's return to tribal land is more than an ecological restoration: it restores a relationship that was deliberately severed as an instrument of colonial policy. The nineteenth-century slaughter of the bison herds was, among other things, a military strategy to deprive Plains nations of their primary food source and force dependence on federal commodity rations. The return of the bison to tribal management is, in this context, simultaneously a food-system intervention, an ecological restoration, and a reassertion of sovereignty over a relationship the federal government spent a century trying to destroy. The framework scores the tribal bison operations well because the operations score well. The historical weight behind the scores is something the framework does not capture but the reader should know.

The scaling question for the Plains is not ecological but institutional. The land exists. The northern Great Plains contain millions of acres of native grassland, much of it under cattle grazing leases that could, in principle, support bison instead. The animal exists. The ecological relationship is intact. What does not yet exist at scale is the market infrastructure, the processing capacity, and the policy framework that would allow bison production on native grassland to move from a conservation-niche market to a meaningful fraction of the region's protein supply. The USDA's inspection requirements, the geographic concentration of bison-certified processing facilities, and the absence of bison from the commodity-support programs that underwrite cattle production all create friction that is institutional rather than ecological. The constraint is the institutional architecture built around cattle rather than the landscape itself, and the question is whether that architecture can be expanded to accommodate the animal the landscape actually wants.

## Two Further Bioregions, Briefly

The Gulf Coast and the Pacific Northwest each deserve extended treatment, and each illustrates a different dimension of the framework's application. I will sketch them here rather than develop them fully, because the principles the Southwest, Plains, and New England sections have established apply to these regions with modifications of detail rather than of structure.

The Gulf Coast (from the Texas barrier islands through the Louisiana marshes to the Florida panhandle) is where the marine and terrestrial frameworks converge most visibly. On land, the region's Pleistocene fauna included tapirs, capybaras, llamas, and ground sloths, a Neotropical assemblage whose proxy-selection question is more complex than for the arid Southwest or the grassland Plains.[^6] On water, the Gulf is where the oyster-reef restoration arguments from Chapter 10 come into sharpest focus: reef restoration programs across four states are rebuilding habitat that simultaneously filters water, stabilizes shoreline, and produces a harvestable food product whose dependency profile is the estuarine ecosystem itself.[^7] The wild-caught Gulf shrimp fishery, battered by competition from farmed imports whose dependency profile runs through the industrial substrate, is a food system the framework can distinguish from its competitor on the fourth axis alone.[^8]

The Pacific Northwest (western Oregon and Washington, coastal British Columbia) is the bioregion where all three frameworks converge on a single watershed. The salmon is the organizing species: Pacific salmon connect the ocean to the interior through their spawning migrations, transporting marine-derived nitrogen upstream into riparian forests where it is measurable in the growth rings of old-growth trees miles from the coast.[^12] The terrestrial side runs through the region's extraordinary soil productivity and the Indigenous management traditions Chapter 10 described: the fire-maintained prairies of the Willamette Valley, the camas cultivation, the oak savannas that Europeans mistook for natural meadows.[^13] The shellfish dimension (Puget Sound's geoduck, Dungeness crab, and Pacific oyster industries) adds marine food systems whose dependency profiles run through ecosystem health rather than the industrial supply chain. The Northwest's food-system potential, viewed through the framework, is a network of interlocking marine and terrestrial systems whose collective survivability depends on the health of the watershed that connects them. Restore the salmon, and the riparian forests receive their nitrogen. Restore the otters, and the kelp forests recover, and the nearshore fisheries follow. The systems are coupled, and restoring one restores the conditions the others depend on.

## The New England Hardwoods

New England is a landscape that has been farmed, logged, abandoned, and reforested within the span of written American history. The stone walls that run through second-growth forest across Connecticut, Massachusetts, Vermont, and New Hampshire mark the boundaries of fields that were cleared in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries and released back to forest as New England agriculture moved west or collapsed under competition from the Midwest's deeper soils.[^9] The forest that has regrown is young by ecological standards (most of it is less than a hundred and fifty years old), and it is missing most of the large-bodied fauna that the region's Pleistocene and early-Holocene forests supported.

The mastodon was the eastern hardwoods' dominant large browser. Its range extended from the Great Lakes to the Gulf Coast, and its feeding behavior (browsing the understory, stripping bark, creating canopy gaps) shaped the structure of the deciduous forest in ways the current forest does not reflect.[^10] There is no living proxy for a mastodon. The framework does not pretend otherwise. What the framework does suggest is that the New England food system should lean into the ecological character of the landscape it sits on (a forest landscape, not a grassland) and that the food-system possibilities of managed forest are more extensive than the region's current agricultural economy recognizes.

The food-system potential of the New England forest is not hypothetical. It is already being demonstrated at small scale across the region, though the formal economy barely registers it. Consider what the forest produces without being asked: mast crops (acorns, beechnuts, hickory nuts, black walnuts) that fall by the ton across millions of acres every autumn and are consumed primarily by deer, turkeys, and squirrels because no commercial infrastructure exists to harvest them at scale for human use. Maple syrup, already a $150-million-per-year industry in the Northeast, produced from trees that require no planting, no irrigation, no fertilizer, and no annual disturbance: a food product whose dependency profile is the forest itself and a late-winter freeze-thaw cycle. Wild mushrooms (chanterelles, hen-of-the-woods, black trumpets, morels) that fruit reliably in the same forests, sought by restaurant buyers who pay $15 to $30 per pound for a product that grows on dead wood without human intervention. Ramps, fiddleheads, sumac, elderberry, and the broader category of wild edibles that the foraging economy has begun to rediscover but that the formal food system still treats as marginal curiosities rather than as products of an ecosystem's inherent productivity.

What distinguishes these products from niche delicacies is that the ecosystem produces them as a function of its own ecological processes. The maple sap flows because the tree is cycling nutrients between root and crown in late winter. The mushrooms fruit because the mycelial network is decomposing wood and cycling carbon. The mast falls because the oaks and hickories are reproducing. None of these require the forest to be anything other than a forest. The food is what the forest does. The economic question is not whether the forest produces value (it does, continuously) but whether the institutional and market infrastructure exists to capture that value as food rather than leaving it on the ground.

Silvopastoral systems in the New England hardwoods take the argument one step further: not merely harvesting what the forest produces passively but managing the forest as a food-producing ecosystem through deliberate integration of animals under the canopy. Pigs on forest floor, processing mast and rooting through the understory in a pattern that mimics the soil disturbance the extinct peccaries once produced. The heritage breeds suited to this work (Berkshires, Large Blacks, breeds whose foraging behavior and fat-deposition patterns were selected over centuries for woodland production rather than confinement) produce a pork whose flavor and fat profile reflect the mast-based diet in ways that commodity pork, raised on corn and soy in climate-controlled buildings, does not. A pig finished on acorns and beechnuts under a New England hardwood canopy is producing Ibérico-grade pork (the same mast-finishing principle that makes Spanish *jamón ibérico* among the most valued cured meats in the world) from an ecosystem that is reproducing the mast without human input. The economics of mast-finished pork are not commodity economics. The product commands a premium because it is a different product, and the premium is supportable because the input costs are low: the feed is what falls from the trees.

Sheep and goats browsing the understory in managed rotation add a second layer. The Janzen-Martin anachronism fruits (pawpaws, honey locust, Kentucky coffee tree) are eastern species whose oversized fruits, thick pods, and large seeds are calibrated to dispersal by animals that have been absent for twelve thousand years.[^11] Cultivating these species in silvopastoral systems would begin to restore a plant-animal relationship that has been one-sided since the mastodons disappeared. The pawpaw, in particular, is a fruit whose commercial potential has been recognized only recently: a custard-textured, tropical-flavored native fruit that grows in the understory of eastern hardwood forests and requires almost no management once established. The tree is shade-tolerant, pest-resistant, and productive for decades. It is also almost entirely absent from the commercial food system because the fruit is soft, perishable, and does not ship well in the cold-chain infrastructure built for apples and oranges. The market limitation is logistical, not ecological. The fruit exists. The demand exists. What does not exist is the supply-chain infrastructure calibrated to a forest product rather than an orchard product.

The New England case illustrates the framework's sensitivity to history in a way the other bioregions do not. The region's agricultural landscape was created by clearing forest. Its ecological trajectory, in the absence of active management, is reforestation. A food-system framework that asks New England to maintain open pasture at scale is asking the landscape to maintain a condition it is actively trying to leave. A framework that asks New England to produce food from its forest (the ecological state the landscape is returning to on its own) is working with the trajectory rather than against it. The survivability implications are direct: a food system aligned with the landscape's own ecological direction requires less energy to maintain than one that fights that direction.

The stone walls in the woods are the evidence. They mark the boundaries of a food system that required constant labor to hold the forest back (clearing, plowing, fencing, draining) on soils that were thinner and rockier than the Midwest bottomland that ultimately outcompeted them. The forest reclaimed the fields within decades of abandonment, and the walls now run through maple and oak and birch as markers of a food system that could not be sustained against the landscape's own trajectory. The next food system for this landscape should not repeat that mistake. It should ask, instead, what the forest will produce. And the answer, if you look at what the forest is already producing without being asked, is more than the formal economy has begun to measure.

## The Urban Reader

I have been writing, so far in this chapter, as though the reader has land. Most readers of this book do not. Most readers live in cities, in apartments or houses without acreage, and their relationship to the food system runs through a grocery store, a farmers' market, a restaurant menu, or a delivery app. If the book's argument ends at the pasture gate, it loses its audience. The argument does not end there.

The framework this book has developed is, at its core, an evaluative tool. It tells you how to assess a food system (any food system, at any scale) by asking four questions: is it economically viable, is it socially equitable, is it environmentally sound, and will it survive when conditions change? Those questions are as useful to a consumer in a grocery aisle as they are to a rancher on a thousand acres. The consumer cannot raise bison or manage a kelp forest. The consumer can ask, of the food they are buying, what system it comes from, what that system depends on, and whether what it depends on is likely to persist. The framework does not require land. It requires attention.

The urban reader has three forms of agency that the book's argument speaks to directly, and none of them require moving to the country.

The first is purchasing. Every food purchase is a vote for the system that produced it. This is not a novel observation, but the framework gives it specificity that the familiar formulation lacks. The question is not merely "is this organic" or "is this local" (labels whose limitations Chapter 1 documented) but "what does the system behind this product depend on, and how does it score on the fourth axis?" Wild-caught salmon from a managed fishery and farmed salmon from a net-pen operation sit side by side in the same case. The framework distinguishes them. Bison from a conservation herd on native prairie and beef from a feedlot finished on commodity corn both produce a burger. The framework distinguishes them. The distinction is not a matter of taste or preference. It is a structural difference in the systems' relationships to the conditions the next century will impose, and the consumer who understands the distinction is making a different kind of choice than the consumer who does not.

I do not want to overstate this. Individual purchasing decisions, even aggregated across millions of consumers, will not by themselves restructure the food system. The structural constraints (subsidy architecture, commodity markets, processing infrastructure, regulatory frameworks) are larger than any individual's grocery cart. The consumer who buys bison instead of feedlot beef has made a choice that the framework supports. That choice, multiplied, shifts demand. Shifted demand creates market signals. Market signals, over time, influence what gets produced and how. The mechanism is real but slow, and it operates within a larger system that individual choices alone cannot redirect. The purchasing power of urban consumers is a lever. It is not the only lever.

The second form of agency is political. The policy architecture that sustains the current food system (the farm bill, the commodity support programs, the regulatory definitions that Chapter 1 described, the land-management frameworks that determine what happens on public land) is built and rebuilt by political decisions that urban voters participate in. The farm bill is reauthorized roughly every five years. Its provisions determine which crops are subsidized, which conservation programs are funded, which land-management practices are incentivized. An urban voter who understands the survivability framework understands that the subsidy architecture is propping up systems that score poorly on the fourth axis while systems that score well receive little or no support. That voter can advocate for policy that redirects support toward the systems the framework identifies as more durable.

Conservation policy is equally relevant. The management of public lands (BLM rangeland, national forest, national wildlife refuge) is governed by plans that are subject to public comment and political influence. The question of whether feral horses on BLM land are managed as nuisance animals or reframed as proxy species occupying an ecological role is a policy question, not an ecological one. The ecology is what it is regardless of the policy. The policy determines whether the ecology is managed in a way that produces food, restores function, and scores well on the framework, or whether it continues to be managed under assumptions the book has spent ten chapters questioning. Urban voters participate in these decisions. Their participation matters, not least because urban voters constitute the majority of the electorate and the majority of the political pressure on the representatives who write the farm bill and confirm the heads of the agencies that manage public land.

The third form of agency is advocacy. The experiments this book has called for (proof-of-concept trials with proxy species on willing private land, expanded bison restoration, marine-restoration programs that produce food alongside ecological recovery) require funding, institutional support, and public attention. Urban readers are the primary audience for the public-attention component. They are the readers of the journalism, the supporters of the nonprofits, the donors to the research programs, the consumers whose demand signals tell producers what is worth producing. The book's argument reaches the landscape through the people who care about the landscape, and most of those people live in cities.

I want to be direct about this because the alternative is a book that talks past its own readers. A food-systems book that speaks only to people with acreage is a book written for a small and shrinking fraction of the American population. The framework this book has developed is relevant to everyone who eats, which is everyone. The urban reader is not a bystander to the food-system conversation. The urban reader is the majority of the food-system conversation, and the conversation will go nowhere without them.

## Specificity as Feature

The chapter has walked through five bioregions, and the five have produced five different pictures. The Southwest wants camelids and equids on browse. The northern Plains want bison on grass. The Gulf Coast wants silvopastoral systems and restored marine ecosystems. New England wants food from its forests. The Pacific Northwest wants its salmon and its managed lowland agriculture integrated across a single watershed.

The variation is the point. The four-axis framework does not produce a single prescription for every landscape. It produces a method for arriving at the prescription the landscape calls for, and the prescriptions differ because the landscapes differ. The arid basins of Arizona and the rain-soaked valleys of Oregon have different evolutionary histories, different vegetation communities, different fossil records, different Indigenous management traditions, and different food-system possibilities. A framework that produced the same answer for both would be ignoring everything that makes each landscape what it is. The framework's value lies precisely in its sensitivity to the specific. It asks: what did this landscape evolve under? What does this landscape need? What does this landscape produce when its ecological relationships are allowed to function? The answers are local. They have to be.

This specificity is also, I think, what distinguishes the framework from the approach it is meant to improve upon. The industrial food system treats the continent as a single production surface: corn from Iowa to feed cattle in Texas to produce beef for consumers in New York. The commodity system is designed to erase local difference, to make a bushel of corn from Nebraska interchangeable with a bushel from Indiana, to make a pound of beef from a Kansas feedlot indistinguishable from a pound from a Colorado one. The erasure of local difference is the system's efficiency, and the efficiency is real. It is also the mechanism by which the system's survivability deficit is produced, because a system that treats every landscape the same cannot respond to the ways in which landscapes are not the same.

The framework proposed in this book asks the opposite question. It asks what each landscape is, specifically, and what each landscape can do, specifically, when its ecological relationships are allowed to operate. The answers are diverse. The diversity is the feature. A food system that produces camel milk in Arizona, bison on the Montana plains, oysters in the Chesapeake, forest-raised pork in Vermont, and salmon in the Columbia River watershed is a food system that is not vulnerable to a single point of failure, because it is not organized around a single commodity, a single animal, a single supply chain, or a single set of assumptions about what the future will look like. It is a food system that survives because it is many food systems, each adapted to its own place, each drawing on its own ecological relationships, each capable of continuing to function when the conditions that any one of the others depends on change.

The four-axis framework does not produce a universal answer. It produces the right question — what does this place need, what does this place produce, what will this place still be doing in a hundred years? — and the right question, asked of every landscape on the continent, produces a set of answers that are collectively more durable, more diverse, and more honestly sustainable than any single answer could be.

The next chapter asks what these answers look like at the scale of an individual meal, an individual household, an individual set of economic constraints. The framework has been applied to landscapes. It is time to apply it to the plate.
