# Chapter 6: The Pasture, Honestly

Joel Salatin's Polyface Farm sits in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, on about five hundred and fifty acres of rolling pasture, woodlot, and pond that have been in his family since the early 1960s. The operation is the most publicly described small farm in the United States. A steady stream of agricultural interns, journalists, documentary crews, and Polyface's own mail-order customers have made the farm into the de facto reference site for what has come to be called, interchangeably, regenerative agriculture, holistic management, or pasture-based farming. Salatin has earned that attention. By his own published accounting, the soil organic matter on Polyface rose from roughly one and a half percent when his parents bought the land in the early 1960s to around eight percent by the time he was writing *You Can Farm* at the end of that century, against a continental cropland average closer to one to three percent.[^1] That is not a trivial gain. An inch of topsoil takes a century or more to form under conventional agriculture. Polyface has built measurable inches within a human career.

Nothing in this chapter should be read as denying that it is working. The previous chapter applied the four-axis framework, economic, social, environmental, survivability, to the two dominant industrial systems and watched them score poorly on each axis, for reasons that were structural rather than incidental. The obvious next question is whether the leading alternative scores better. It does. By a substantial margin, on the axes where it is easiest to measure, it does. The harder question, which is what this chapter is actually about, is whether scoring better is the same thing as escaping the structure. I have come to think it is not, and I have come to think this in a way that takes Salatin seriously.

## What the Work Actually Achieves

The case for regenerative agriculture, as a practice rather than a brand, is strongest when it is presented specifically. I want to take the time to present it specifically before anything else.

Start with soil. The conventional row-crop regime in the United States has been losing topsoil for a century. USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service modeling using the Revised Universal Soil Loss Equation places average annual erosion rates across conventionally managed cropland at roughly four to five tons per acre, against natural soil-formation rates of a half ton or so. The result, on aggregate, is the loss of something on the order of ninety centimeters of topsoil across the major US cropping regions since the start of European farming on the continent.[^2] Rotationally grazed pasture, by contrast, does not erode at anything like that rate. Machner and colleagues, reviewing long-term grazing trials across the southern and central United States, reported that well-managed perennial pasture under adaptive multi-paddock grazing can build soil organic carbon at rates on the order of 0.3 to 1 ton of carbon per hectare per year in the upper soil horizon over multi-decadal time frames, in a system from which the ruminants are simultaneously producing meat.[^3] The soil carbon and the meat are produced together rather than traded against each other.

The animal-welfare comparison is not close. A broiler chicken in a conventional confined house lives thirty-five to forty-two days in a structure whose stocking density is regulated, in the United States, by the industry's own standards rather than by federal law.[^4] The bird typically cannot turn around without stepping on another bird and cannot exhibit most of its species-typical behaviors at all. A broiler on pasture lives in daylight on grass, in a covered mobile structure moved once a day, with access to insects, seeds, and whatever small animals it can catch. The life is shorter than wild chickens' lives and is still ended by slaughter, but within the window of the bird's life the quality of that life is, by any reasonable behavioral measure, incomparable. The same comparison holds for laying hens, for pastured pigs, and for cattle, though with different gradients. Confined animal feeding operations have imposed a kind of welfare compression that has no real historical precedent in Western agriculture, and pasture-based systems do not operate at that compression. On the welfare axis, the difference is not a matter of degree but of kind.

The operation's internal design deserves credit on its own terms as well. Polyface's signature move, and the move most imitated across the pastured-livestock literature, is the integration of species in rotation across the same ground. Cattle graze first. The pasture is then rested for a set interval, during which the cow manure ages and fly larvae develop in the pats. The broilers follow in mobile field shelters, eating the larvae and other insects mobilized by the cattle, adding their own manure to the surface, and continuing to be moved forward. Laying hens, in a separate mobile structure Salatin calls the "eggmobile," circulate through the same ground in a slightly different phase of the rotation, completing the fly-cycle disruption and contributing a different manure-deposition pattern. Pigs are used elsewhere on the operation for targeted tillage and forest-floor disturbance. The design moves biological work: pest control, manure distribution, vegetation management, nutrient cycling, from purchased inputs and mechanical operations into the animals themselves. It is a serious piece of agroecological engineering, and it has earned the attention it has received. Any fair assessment of the operation starts by acknowledging that the integration works and works well within its own perimeter.

The rural economic picture is similar in structure. A typical industrial broiler contract puts the grower in capital debt to a vertically integrated processor, with most of the margin on the bird captured at processing and distribution.[^5] The grower absorbs the capital cost, the environmental externalities, and the labor; the processor absorbs most of the profit. A pasture-based operation at Polyface's scale, by contrast, captures significantly more of the value of the bird on the farm itself. Salatin has argued for decades that rural economic renewal runs through the direct-marketed farm rather than through the contract-grower arrangement, and he is right about this as a matter of basic accounting. Where pasture-based operations exist at scale in a region, they support local processing infrastructure, farm-to-consumer distribution networks, agritourism, and a retained agricultural workforce that the contract system has hollowed out. That hollowing has been measurable for decades. The rural sociology literature, some of the longest-running sociological work on American agriculture, has documented across fifty years of comparative studies beginning with Walter Goldschmidt's original comparisons of Arvin and Dinuba in the 1940s that the consolidation of US livestock and commodity production has coincided with measurable declines in rural population, retail employment, small-town median income, and civic participation across regions where the transition from diversified to contract production has been most complete.[^6] The pasture-based operation is working against that curve. The people Polyface employs live in the Shenandoah Valley. The money Polyface earns is spent in the Shenandoah Valley. The operation is not extracting value from its region toward a distant headquarters, which is what the contract model does structurally.

The carbon case for well-managed grazing, finally, is genuine. This is not the same thing as saying it is sufficient. Richard Teague and colleagues argued in a 2016 paper in the *Journal of Soil and Water Conservation* that adaptive multi-paddock grazing, if scaled across existing North American grasslands, could plausibly offset a meaningful fraction of North American agriculture's greenhouse gas footprint through soil carbon accumulation and reduced reliance on synthetic nitrogen fertilizer.[^7] The Teague paper is the most forceful peer-reviewed articulation of the climate case for regenerative grazing, and I take it seriously. I will come back to its limits later in this chapter, because the limits are real, but the positive case is not fabricated. Well-managed grazing genuinely pulls carbon into soil while producing food, and the effect is not a rounding error.

Put all of that together and you have a system that is, by any fair accounting, substantially better across several important axes than what it is trying to replace. It builds soil. It produces food. It restores something close to normal animal lives. It supports rural economies. It sequesters carbon. None of those achievements is imaginary, and none of them should be held at arm's length in what follows.

## Applying the Framework

Run the four-axis framework across this version of the operation, and the scoring changes noticeably from the industrial comparison in the previous chapter.

On the environmental axis, pasture-based operations score well. Soil carbon accumulates rather than erodes. Chemical inputs (synthetic fertilizer, herbicide, insecticide, fungicide, antibiotic) are absent or dramatically reduced, meaning the downstream externalities of runoff, resistance, and non-target toxicity are absent or dramatically reduced. Biodiversity on and around the operation is higher: perennial pastures host arthropod, avian, and small-mammal assemblages that row-crop fields cannot support.[^8] Water infiltration rates into rotationally grazed soil are higher than into tilled soil by substantial multiples, with consequent reductions in flood peaks and downstream sedimentation.[^9] The environmental axis, in a pasture-based system run competently, moves from the deep negative register of the industrial score into a positive register that is not marginal.

On the social axis, the picture is similarly improved. Worker conditions on a pasture-based operation are not the worker conditions of an industrial slaughterhouse. The operation sits inside the community it feeds rather than at the end of a commodity chain that terminates at a processor two states away. Rural community indicators (employment, retention, median income for agricultural families) track with the presence of diversified direct-marketed agriculture in ways that have been documented for a generation now.[^6] The social axis improves.

On the economic axis, the scoring is conditional and depends on scale. At the scale of a Polyface or a comparable operation, several hundred acres, owner-operated, direct-marketed, the economics work: the farm is profitable, the family is supported, the next generation has a ladder onto the operation if one of them wants it. The work is not easy and the margins are not wide, but the operation is economically viable in a way that much of conventional small-scale commodity agriculture is not. At the scale of the continental food supply, the economic axis becomes much harder to score because the system as currently practiced has never operated at that scale. Scaling a direct-marketed pastured-poultry model from one operation in the Shenandoah Valley to, say, the broiler demand of a metropolitan Atlantic seaboard would require land, processing infrastructure, labor supply, and concentrated feed in volumes that the existing direct-market architecture has not produced and the existing commodity architecture cannot produce without compromising the ecological distinctions that make the pasture-based operation worth doing in the first place. This is not a case against the operation at its own scale. It is a case for treating the operation's achievements as operation-scale achievements, which is how Salatin himself has generally framed them. The claim that what Polyface does could be extended to feed the country is a claim that has rarely been made by Polyface itself. It has more often been made by journalists and advocates around the operation, and the operation is not responsible for the claims made on its behalf.

On the survivability axis, pasture-based operations score substantially better than industrial ones, which is the axis the industrial system scored worst on. The operation does not depend on continuous diesel for its own field work in the same way a row-crop operation does, because most of the field work is being done by the animals themselves. It does not depend on continuous antibiotic administration, because the stocking densities and the rotation pattern handle the disease pressure that makes antibiotics necessary in confined systems. It does not depend on continuous federal subsidies for its own outputs, because those outputs are priced into a direct market rather than a commodity one. The system is more survivable than what it replaced.

But the survivability score is not the maximum possible score, and the reason the score is not maximum is the subject of the rest of this chapter. The operation, as currently practiced, still depends on something the industrial system supplies. That dependency is the structural ceiling.

## The Structural Ceiling

Regenerative agriculture, in its current operative form in the United States, is structurally tethered to the industrial commodity system it opposes. This is not a statement about any particular operator's motives or honesty; it is a statement about how the economics and the supply chain of the current US agricultural system constrain even the operations explicitly trying to operate outside it.

The ceiling shows up in different places at different operations. For operations that raise ruminants on pasture (grass-finished beef, pastured lamb, grass-fed dairy), the ceiling is lower and the tether is thinner, because ruminants can live most or all of their lives on forage produced on the farm itself and require relatively little from outside the operation. For operations that raise monogastric animals on pasture (broilers, layers, pigs), the ceiling is higher and the tether is thicker, because monogastrics cannot live on forage alone and must be supplemented with concentrated protein and energy that, under current conditions, comes from somewhere else.

Polyface is the worked example because it is the operation whose numbers are most completely in the public record. The numbers are worth walking through carefully.

## The Polyface Math

Polyface's broiler operation, at the scale Michael Pollan described in *The Omnivore's Dilemma*, processes approximately twelve thousand broiler chickens per year in mobile field shelters moved daily across pasture.[^10] Salatin's own *Pastured Poultry Profit$* gives the feed conversion ratio for the birds as roughly three pounds of feed for each pound of finished body weight, with an average finished weight in the neighborhood of four pounds per bird.[^11] The arithmetic from those numbers is direct. Twelve thousand birds, at four pounds of body weight each, at three pounds of feed per pound of body weight, comes to approximately one hundred and forty-four thousand pounds of feed per year for the broiler flock alone.

The composition of that feed is also in the public record. Polyface has published its layer and broiler ration specifications; independent writing on the farm, including longer profiles and follow-up interviews over the years, has confirmed the proportions. The ration is built around roughly forty-nine to fifty percent corn and roughly thirty to thirty-one percent soy, with the remainder made up of mineral premix, fish meal, kelp, and a few other supplements.[^12] Applied to the total feed figure, that means roughly seventy-one thousand five hundred pounds of corn and roughly forty-four thousand five hundred pounds of soybean meal per year, for the broiler flock alone. The layer flock, smaller but year-round, adds to these totals. So do the pigs that spend part of their finishing period on concentrate.

The corn and soy do not come from Polyface's own acres. The farm's perennial pasture system is not arranged for row cropping, and the Shenandoah Valley's climate and soils are not particularly suited to corn on the scale required. The feed comes from off-farm sources — grain sold by neighboring farmers and feed mills in the broader region of the valley and adjacent states. Salatin has written openly about this sourcing, and has emphasized that the farm works to buy from local non-GMO producers rather than from anonymous commodity channels.[^13] The sourcing preference is real. The underlying point is that even with the most scrupulous regional sourcing, the corn and soy feeding the Polyface birds are grown under the conditions that most corn and soy in the United States are grown under: annual row crop, tillage or herbicide-based residue management, synthetic nitrogen inputs, petroleum-powered field operations, and (directly or indirectly) the federal commodity support system that keeps the price of corn and soy low enough for a pastured-poultry operation to purchase them profitably. The feed rests on the industrial substrate.

I want to be careful about how this finding is registered, because the framing matters.

It is not a gotcha. Salatin publishes these numbers himself, in his own books, for farmers who want to replicate his system. The feed proportions are printed in *Pastured Poultry Profit$*; the scale is in *The Omnivore's Dilemma* and subsequent profiles. There is no concealment to expose. Anyone who has read the source material with any attention has encountered the math. The math was already public, in that sense, when any of us started having this conversation.

It is also not a personal failure. The operator is working inside the structural conditions of US agriculture as those conditions currently exist. Grass-finishing a broiler in any meaningful volume is not, under present conditions, economically possible at a price point that sustains a family farm. The price of corn and soy has been held down by half a century of agricultural policy; the cost of land, labor, and processing has not. An operator deciding, in 1995 or 2005 or 2025, whether to build a pastured broiler enterprise has faced a narrow set of real choices, and at every point in that window the economically survivable choice has involved purchasing grain from someone else. The alternative — growing the grain on the farm under perennial-compatible practice — is not absent from the regenerative literature, but it has not, to my knowledge, been implemented at any scale that makes broiler operations like Polyface's replaceable.

The point is worth sharpening, because the sharpening matters for everything that comes after. An operation like Polyface is doing the most that is currently doable inside the existing system. The ceiling on what it can do sits not at the gate of the farm but at the edge of the commodity market the farm buys from. Raising the ceiling would require changing the commodity market, which is a different kind of problem than changing how a farm is managed. An individual farmer can change how the farm is managed; an individual farmer cannot change the structure of US corn and soy production. The work Salatin and operators like him have done on the manageable side is the work they could do. The work on the substrate side is the work the rest of the food system has not done.

What the finding is, then, is a structural observation. Even the best version of regenerative agriculture as currently practiced, the operation with the most documented soil gains, the most direct-marketed income, the most visible animal welfare improvements, the most sustained rural economic impact, still has its monogastric-animal enterprises running on carbohydrate and protein inputs produced by the industrial commodity system it opposes. The system it is offering as the alternative is, in its concentrate-dependent component, riding on the substrate it wants to replace.

This is the structural ceiling. It is not the end of the argument; it is the thing the argument has to take seriously before it continues.

## The McWilliams-Salatin Exchange

The substrate question became public in a way most non-specialists would remember when James McWilliams published "The Myth of Sustainable Meat" in the *New York Times* in April of 2012.[^14] McWilliams, a historian at Texas State, had been arguing for several years that the environmental advantages of pasture-based animal production were overstated. The op-ed compressed the argument. It made several claims, some of which were wrong, one of which was right, and the combination produced a public exchange that illustrated the limits of the conversation as then framed more clearly than either side would likely have wanted.

McWilliams's central environmental claim, that pasture-raised beef produces substantially more methane per unit of beef than feedlot beef because grass-finished animals live longer and eat more lignocellulose, was overstated as applied. The methane-per-unit-beef numbers are genuinely higher on pasture, but the comparison is incomplete without the soil carbon side of the ledger, which can offset a meaningful fraction of the methane difference in well-managed systems. The Teague analysis and the subsequent literature have laid this out in more detail than the op-ed allowed for.[^7] The land-use claim, that pasture requires more acres per pound of finished animal than feedlot, was true on its own terms but also incomplete. The relevant question is not acres-per-pound but which kind of land is being used, for what, under what set of effects on downstream ecosystems. A great deal of pastureland is land that cannot productively support row crops.

The claim McWilliams made that landed, and landed in a way neither he nor his critics particularly wanted to admit, was the feed-sourcing claim. He pointed out that pasture-based operations raising monogastric animals still relied on purchased grain produced in the conventional industrial system, and that this reliance made the sustainability advantages of the pasture component narrower than their advocates claimed. On the feed-sourcing point, the math is the math, and it was already public in Salatin's own books.

Salatin's public response came through Polyface Farm's own channels and through subsequent essays and interviews.[^15] The response was vigorous and, in places, sincere in a way the op-ed had not been about Salatin's work. Several of his counterpoints were substantive: the methane comparison was being handled more simplistically than the science warranted; the land-use comparison was ignoring land-use distinctions; Polyface's local non-GMO sourcing was real and deserved to be distinguished from anonymous commodity sourcing. One argument in particular deserves engagement on its own terms, because it is the most serious thing said in the exchange and because it gets close to the structural question without quite reaching it.

Salatin argued, in effect, that the movement of grain from grain-growing regions into grazing regions via animal feed is not a pathology of the industrial system. It is an ancient pattern. Historically, lowland grain-growing regions and upland grazing regions have exchanged nutrients through exactly this kind of trade: grain flowed to the uplands as animal feed, and animal products flowed back to the lowlands as food. The pattern long predates petroleum agriculture, commodity subsidies, and the US Department of Agriculture. A pastured-poultry operation buying corn from a farmer fifty miles away is, under this framing, participating in a trade relationship as old as agriculture itself.

There is real history in that framing. The highland-lowland nutrient exchange is genuine, and it is defensible as a way of organizing agricultural landscapes. What the framing does not quite reach, in my reading of Salatin's reply, is the point underneath McWilliams's otherwise flawed argument. The historical highland-lowland exchange was regional and operated at a scale that did not require a continental commodity system. The modern version of the exchange operates at a scale that does. When Polyface sources non-GMO corn from within the Shenandoah region, it is still sourcing from farms whose own inputs, seed, fertilizer, herbicide, field equipment, transportation to market, run through the continental industrial substrate that corn and soy have been embedded in since roughly the Nixon administration's rewrite of the farm bill.[^16] The regional-sourcing move reduces the transportation footprint and the genetic-engineering exposure. It does not detach the feed from the underlying substrate.

Salatin's response, read carefully, is best understood as a defense of the farming model's integrity at the farm-gate scale, and at that scale it is largely correct. It is not a response to the structural observation that the farm, as a node in a larger agricultural system, cannot fully escape that system as currently organized. McWilliams had framed the feed-sourcing point as an accusation, which it did not need to be and which made the response defensive. The point was better made as a structural observation. This is how I am trying to make it now.

What the 2012 exchange illustrated, in retrospect, was the limit of the conversation as then conducted. McWilliams wanted to indict the pasture model. Salatin wanted to defend it. Between the two positions there was not much room for the observation that the pasture model was genuinely doing most of what it claimed to do and was still riding on the industrial substrate to do it. Both of those are true. Neither side's framing in the 2012 exchange made that doubled picture easy to see. The conversation has moved somewhat since then. Versions of the structural observation now appear in places where they did not appear in 2012: in *Civil Eats* coverage, in the peer-reviewed agroecological literature, and in the internal conversations of regenerative-agriculture advocates who have been working on perennial-grain and on-farm feed alternatives precisely because the ceiling is a ceiling they can see from inside the movement.

## Grazed and Confused

The climate side of the ledger deserves the same honest treatment. The case for well-managed grazing as a substantial climate mitigation lever is real but bounded. It is bounded in ways the most enthusiastic popular framing has not always absorbed.

Tara Garnett and colleagues at the Food Climate Research Network at the University of Oxford published *Grazed and Confused?* in 2017, which remains the most rigorous systematic assessment of grazing's climate claims.[^17] The report reviewed the accumulated peer-reviewed evidence for soil carbon sequestration under improved grazing management and concluded that the sequestration effect is genuine, measurable, and at a global scale able to offset somewhere on the order of twenty to sixty percent of annual ruminant greenhouse gas emissions under optimistic assumptions. The report also concluded that the effect saturates after several decades. A grassland accumulating carbon under improved management does not continue accumulating at the initial rate indefinitely. The upper end of the estimated sequestration potential requires management conditions that are difficult to achieve and maintain at scale.

Teague and colleagues have pushed back on parts of the *Grazed and Confused?* analysis, arguing that the report underestimates the sequestration potential of adaptive multi-paddock grazing specifically and that it aggregates the grazing-management literature in a way that flattens differences between management regimes that are in fact ecologically distinct.[^18] Their case is not implausible. The specific management regime matters: a continuous grazing system and an AMP system are not interchangeable from the soil's perspective, and the literature has not always treated them as distinct.

The saturation mechanism in the Garnett analysis deserves a moment of its own, because its implications are easy to miss in popular framings. Soil carbon accumulation under improved grazing is fastest in the early years after management change, when the soil is most below its equilibrium carbon level for the new management regime. As the soil approaches the new equilibrium, a process that takes in temperate grasslands somewhere on the order of twenty to forty years, the annual sequestration rate declines toward zero. The soil is not permanently scrubbing carbon out of the atmosphere the way an unsaturated chemical sink would. It is moving from one equilibrium to a higher equilibrium, and the movement is bounded. The total amount of carbon ultimately stored is meaningful; the rate of annual accumulation has an arc. Any long-run climate accounting for grazing has to incorporate this shape, and the popular framings often do not. This is not a criticism of Polyface or of any particular operator; it is a criticism of the rhetorical compression that has sometimes attended the climate argument for regenerative agriculture in the broader public conversation.

Read against each other, Garnett and Teague produce an honest calibration. Well-managed grazing is genuinely carbon-positive at the operation scale. It is not climate-neutral for ruminant production overall, even in the most optimistic scenarios. It is a meaningful contribution to the climate side of the agricultural ledger, substantially better than poorly managed grazing or feedlot, and not by itself a climate solution for ruminant agriculture. The claim that pastured livestock operations are climate-positive, stated carefully, is about the operation's own carbon balance rather than about the emissions the operation is also producing. Stated carefully, the claim is defensible. Stated as a blanket net-negative framing for ruminant agriculture as a sector, it is not.

None of this undoes the soil, welfare, or rural-economy case. All of those stand on their own. It does mean that the climate claim specifically needs to be stated with care. This care is compatible with the operation being ecologically serious, which it is.

## What the Ceiling Means

The chapter has, I hope, been doing two things at once. It has been taking regenerative agriculture seriously as the most developed alternative to the industrial system on offer, and it has been looking at what the most developed alternative can and cannot do as currently practiced. Both of those are honest descriptions. The honest account requires holding the operation's real achievements and its real limits at the same time.

What the structural ceiling means, stated as plainly as I can state it, is this. Regenerative agriculture as currently organized in the United States is significantly better than what it is trying to replace. It is also still riding on the substrate it is trying to replace. Its monogastric-animal enterprises import tens of thousands of pounds of corn and soy per year from the industrial commodity system. Its ruminant enterprises depend less directly on that system but still operate inside the broader infrastructure: the diesel for equipment, the steel for fencing, the mineral supplements, the veterinary care, that the industrial substrate supplies. An operation like Polyface is an achievement inside a system whose boundaries the operation cannot fully escape.

This is not a defeat. It is a limit. Limits can be mapped and worked against. A substantial fraction of the regenerative-agriculture literature over the last decade has been exactly this work: perennial-grain breeding programs trying to make cereal production compatible with perennial root systems,[^19] silvopastoral systems integrating timber, browse, and ruminant production on the same acreage, and multi-species rotation schemes that reduce the dependence on external concentrated feed. Every one of these is a real response to the structural ceiling. None of them has, as of this writing, produced the fully untethered alternative the framework I have been using would score highest.

The Land Institute's intermediate wheatgrass program, marketed under the trade name Kernza, is the most commercially advanced of these responses. After decades of breeding work in Salina, Kansas, Kernza now has commercial acreage in the low thousands of acres across the upper Midwest, a published grain market, and a small but real downstream supply chain in the craft-brewing and specialty-baking sectors. It is not yet a corn replacement at any scale that would matter to a Polyface feed order. It is not even a credible partial replacement at that scale. What it is is existence-proof that a deep-rooted perennial can be bred toward commercial grain production, and that a seed company and a farmer can, between them, make the numbers work at the margin. Silvopasture research in the southeastern United States and the Mediterranean dehesa literature offer similar existence proofs on the animal side: systems in which ruminants, browse, mast, and tree crops coexist on the same acreage under management regimes that resemble pre-industrial agroforestry more than they resemble modern pasture-based operations. The point is not that any of these is ready to replace the current substrate. The point is that the work of designing an alternative substrate is actively under way, even if the finished substrate is not yet here.

The question that closes this chapter is the question Part III of the book exists to take up. If the best version of the currently-available alternative is structurally tethered to what it opposes, even at the scale of a farm as studied and careful as Polyface, what would actually escape the substrate? What would an operation look like whose feed comes from the landscape rather than from the commodity system, whose animals are selected for the landscape rather than for the commodity market, whose relationship to the land the continent sits on runs through the land's deeper history rather than through the last two centuries of row-crop engineering?

I think the answer lives in the direction Paul Martin has been pointing us since the 1970s. The landscape this continent is, considered on the scale the continent has actually existed on, is not a landscape that evolved under row-crop corn and feedlot cattle. It is a landscape that evolved under a suite of large-bodied animals most of which have not been present for twelve thousand years. The animals we use to feed ourselves now are, in the main, animals whose ecological partnership is with somewhere else. The animals whose ecological partnership is with this continent are, in the main, not being used to feed us at all. That mismatch is the hinge of what follows.

Regenerative agriculture has taken the food system as far as it has been able to take it while working within the cast of species it inherited and the commodity substrate that cast depends on. The distance it has covered is real and should not be given back. What comes next is a different question: whether the cast of species can be reopened, and whether, reopened, it changes what sustainability looks like on this continent. The next chapter starts there.
