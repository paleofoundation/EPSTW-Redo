The structural shape of the chapter, per the outline:

1. Open by acknowledging that Pleistocene rewilding has serious critics. Donlan's 2005 *Nature* paper generated significant scholarly response, much of it skeptical. The chapter takes these critics seriously.

2. Walk through three major critiques, each presented in its strongest form before any response:
   - **Caro and proxy-equivalence:** Proxy species cause real ecological problems. The assumption that closely related species perform equivalent ecological functions is unsupported. Present this critique fully, then respond: the proxy-equivalence question is empirical and can be tested at small scales. It does not need to be assumed; it needs to be tested.

Present this critique fully, then respond: the author accepts this critique in significant part. The obstacles are real.    - **The Pleistocene-baseline-as-fantasy critique:** Conservation should focus on protecting what currently exists rather than restoring what was lost. Present this critique fully, then respond: focusing on what currently exists, when what currently exists is a depleted system, accepts the depletion as a permanent baseline. This is the argument from Chapter 2 returning in a different key.

3. Close with the ask: not certainty, but space for proof of concept. Rewilding hypotheses can be tested at small scales — camels on a single ranch in Arizona, burros in the Grand Canyon, wolves in the Northern Rockies (already done, with measurable results). The book asks for the experiments to be tried and assessed honestly.
