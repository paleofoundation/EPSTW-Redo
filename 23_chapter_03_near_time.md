# Chapter 3: Near Time

Paul Martin came to the extinction question in the 1950s, at roughly the moment the question first became answerable. He was a young paleoecologist at the Desert Laboratory in Tucson — Arizona's field station for the study of arid-land ecology, perched on Tumamoc Hill above the city — and he had been trained in the generation for which the fossil record was a stratigraphic problem. Animals disappeared from the record at some point in the strata. The strata told you roughly when. What the strata could not tell you, at the resolution then available, was whether one animal disappeared at the same moment as another, or centuries earlier, or centuries later, or whether any of them disappeared at the same moment as anything else: a climatic event, a vegetation shift, the arrival of a new predator. The granularity was not fine enough to see coincidence at the scale where coincidence becomes causation.

Willard Libby changed that. Libby's radiocarbon technique, published in the late 1940s and refined through the 1950s, allowed organic material up to about fifty thousand years old to be dated at a resolution of centuries rather than millennia.[^1] Martin later described what this did for the field in plain terms: it took a question that had been stratigraphic and made it chronological. Instead of asking which layer a bone came from, you could ask what year (within a century or two) the bone had lain down in its layer. For the first time, the timing of specific extinctions could be matched against the timing of specific other events. What the dating began to show, across the 1950s and 1960s, was that the timing of the great North American extinctions sat uncomfortably close to one specific other event, and the coincidence bothered Martin for the next half century.

The event was the arrival of humans on the continent.

## What Was Lost

The chapter has to begin with the inventory, because without the inventory the rest of the argument does not land. The prologue named a few of the animals as emblems. Chapter 2 gestured at the catalog without unfolding it. Here the catalog unfolds.

North America north of Mexico lost at least thirty-five genera of large mammals in the terminal Pleistocene pulse, concentrated in the few centuries around thirteen thousand calendar years before present.[^2] That is a genus-level figure, not a species count; counted at the species level the losses are higher. Martin's own tabulation in *Twilight of the Mammoths* ran to more than forty North American species above forty-five kilograms of body mass, or roughly a hundred pounds (the threshold biologists often use to separate large-bodied mammals from medium-sized ones).[^3] The loss on the proboscidean front alone involved both of the continent's mammoth species, the Columbian mammoth *Mammuthus columbi* and the woolly mammoth *Mammuthus primigenius*, together with the American mastodon *Mammut americanum*, a separate lineage with a range that extended through the eastern hardwoods. Three proboscideans, all gone in the same window. The closest parallel anywhere on Earth today would be to imagine Africa losing both species of elephant in the course of a human lifetime.

The ground sloths went with them. Martin counted at least four North American genera: *Megalonyx jeffersonii*, the Jefferson's ground sloth, widely distributed through the east; *Nothrotheriops shastensis*, the Shasta ground sloth, familiar from the dung-dense caves of the Colorado Plateau; *Paramylodon harlani*, Harlan's ground sloth, common enough at La Brea to be an exhibit standard; and *Eremotherium laurillardi*, a massive southern genus that ranged north into the Gulf Coast and occasionally farther. Two of these animals (*Megalonyx* and *Eremotherium*) reached sizes comparable to modern elephants. The others were bear-sized. None of them is present in any modern ecosystem. Their closest living relatives are the two- and three-toed tree sloths of Central and South American rainforests, which weigh a few kilograms and hang in canopies that the extinct North American sloths would have flattened.

The equids went with them. The genus *Equus* had diversified across the continent for the entirety of its evolutionary history, as the previous chapter described. By the terminal Pleistocene pulse several species of native American horse were still in place (*Equus scotti*, *Equus conversidens*, *Equus lambei*, and others whose taxonomy paleontologists continue to revise), and every one of them disappeared in the same window.[^4] The camelids went the same way. Martin described three North American genera going out together: *Camelops*, the Western camel of the prologue's concern; *Hemiauchenia*, the larger of the two native llamas; and *Palaeolama*, the smaller. The bovids and cervids took losses too, though not total ones; the woodland musk ox *Bootherium*, the stag moose *Cervalces*, and several pronghorn relatives *Capromeryx*, *Stockoceros*, and *Tetrameryx* all went out in the pulse.

The predator assemblage that had evolved around these herbivores collapsed with them. The American lion *Panthera atrox*, larger than any living cat, disappeared from a range that had extended from Alaska to central Mexico. The saber-toothed cat *Smilodon fatalis* and the scimitar-toothed cat *Homotherium* both went. The American cheetah *Miracinonyx*, whose pursuit pressure had shaped the pronghorn's still-extant running speed, was gone. The dire wolf *Aenocyon dirus* was gone. The short-faced bear *Arctodus simus*, the largest carnivorous land mammal of the North American Pleistocene and in some reconstructions of its ecology the most aggressive scavenger the continent has ever hosted, was gone.[^5] Beyond the predators, the bear-sized giant beaver *Castoroides*, the capybara-like *Neochoerus*, Jefferson's extinct peccary lineages *Platygonus* and *Mylohyus* — on and on, and then the list stops.

The list does not stop because the subtraction tapered gently. It stops because the continent had little more above forty-five kilograms of body mass to lose. Counting what remained in the Holocene — bison, elk, moose, bighorn, mountain goat, pronghorn, the surviving deer, and the surviving carnivores of wolf and grizzly and cougar — the North American large-mammal fauna at the beginning of written history was roughly half of what it had been, by genus, a few thousand years earlier.[^6] The same pattern, at varying magnitudes, appeared on every inhabited continent in near time. South America lost more than fifty genera, a greater proportional loss than North America; the glyptodonts, the car-sized armored herbivores *Doedicurus* and *Glyptodon*, the massive ground sloth *Megatherium*, the native ungulates *Macrauchenia* and *Toxodon*, and a range of sabertooths and short-faced bears all exited the continent's fauna in the same terminal Pleistocene window. Australia's losses were earlier, roughly forty-six to fifty thousand years ago by current chronology, and were nearly total for the continent's megafaunal assemblage: the rhinoceros-sized diprotodontids, the marsupial lion *Thylacoleo*, giant kangaroos, and the flightless bird *Genyornis* all disappeared. Eurasia lost the woolly mammoth, the woolly rhinoceros *Coelodonta antiquitatis*, the steppe bison *Bison priscus*, the giant deer *Megaloceros giganteus*, the cave bear *Ursus spelaeus*, and the cave hyena, each on its own regional chronology distributed across the late Pleistocene and into the early Holocene. Madagascar's losses came more recently still, within the last two thousand years: the giant lemurs, the elephant birds, and a dwarf hippopotamus disappeared after Malagasy colonization. Each continent's loss profile differed in timing and in which specific animals were involved. What each continent shared was the shape: disproportionate culling of the largest-bodied terrestrial fauna, with little parallel effect on small mammals, plants, marine species, or insects.[^7]

This is the subtraction the previous chapter framed as the reason the 1492 baseline is an inadequate reference point. The chapter has not yet said why it happened. The literature has been trying to say why it happened for the seventy years since Martin first began trying, and the literature has not, on the whole, settled.

## Three Hypotheses

Three explanations have done most of the work in the scholarly debate. They are not strictly mutually exclusive, and the current synthesis involves combinations of them, but it is easier to see the shape of the combination if the three are taken one at a time.

The first is overkill: the hypothesis that the extinctions were caused, primarily or substantially, by predation from newly arrived human hunters. Its most forceful articulation is the one Martin himself gave it, refined over four decades and summarized in *Twilight of the Mammoths* in 2005.

The second is climatic forcing: the hypothesis that the late-Quaternary climate oscillations, in particular the abrupt cooling known as the Younger Dryas around 12,800 calendar years before present, pushed species past thresholds their populations could not recover from. This was the dominant view among most vertebrate paleontologists before Martin's formulation entered the field, and it remains a serious position today, though the version that prevails now is more chronologically precise than the one Martin engaged with in the 1960s.

The third is cosmic impact: the hypothesis, developed in the peer-reviewed literature over the last two decades, that the Younger Dryas cooling itself was initiated or amplified by an airburst or impact event near the Laurentide ice sheet at approximately 12,900 calendar years before present. This is the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis, and it interacts with both of the other two: if the hypothesis is correct, the climatic mechanism has a proximate trigger it otherwise lacks, and the timing of the extinction pulse with respect to human arrival becomes a triple rather than a double coincidence.

Each of these is worth walking through in its strongest form, because each has been argued in its strongest form by serious researchers whose work deserves that level of presentation.

## Overkill, as Martin Made It

Martin did not invent the overkill hypothesis. Richard Foster Flint at Yale had floated human involvement earlier. Darwin's contemporary Richard Owen had proposed something similar for New Zealand's moas in the nineteenth century. What Martin did (working through the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s, often with collaborators in simulation work, comparative island ecology, and radiocarbon calibration) was make the case in a form rigorous enough that climate-centric alternatives had to argue against a specific alternative rather than merely assert their own.[^8]

The case, as Martin stated it in *Twilight of the Mammoths*, rests on three observations about near-time extinctions that climate-centric explanations struggle to accommodate.

The first is time-transgressiveness. Near-time megafaunal extinctions did not happen at the same moment around the world. Australia's pulse is roughly forty-six thousand years ago. Continental North America's is around twelve to thirteen thousand years ago. New Zealand's moa extinctions occurred less than a thousand years ago. Madagascar's losses date to within the last two thousand years. The Caribbean island sloths persisted into the middle Holocene and disappeared only after human colonization of the islands. What does fit each of these extinction events closely is the arrival of humans on the landmass in question. Climatic shifts of comparable magnitude, if they are invoked as the primary mechanism, have to be invoked at different times in different places, without a unifying global pattern of climate that matches the extinction pattern. "A climatic catastrophe forced extinctions in Australia around 46,000 years ago, and in North and South America 10,000 to 12,000 radiocarbon years ago," Martin wrote. If so, "neither event registered in New Zealand."[^9] Time-transgressiveness is the signature the overkill hypothesis predicts and the climate hypothesis does not.

The second is selectivity. Near-time losses fell almost exclusively on large-bodied terrestrial mammals. Trees and shrubs show essentially no contemporaneous extinction in the North American fossil record. Small mammals show essentially none. Marine mammals show essentially none. Beetles, birds outside flightless insular lineages, and other less-hunted groups show essentially none. The earlier Quaternary had repeatedly imposed severe climatic switches on the same flora and fauna without similar losses. If climate was the primary mechanism, its selectivity for mammals above forty-five kilograms (the threshold at which human hunting returns become energetically attractive) would be an extraordinary coincidence. Human predation, by contrast, predicts exactly that size cutoff. Large animals reproduce slowly, track easily, and yield high energetic returns per successful hunt. They are the animals a novel predator would exhaust first.

The third is the uniqueness problem. Climatic changes as dramatic as the Allerød–Younger Dryas switch had occurred repeatedly during the Quaternary. Greenland ice cores record dozens of them. None of the earlier switches, over hundreds of thousands of years, are associated with comparable megafaunal die-offs. For a climatic explanation of the near-time losses to be adequate, the climate shift at the end of the Pleistocene had to be uniquely severe. The ice-core evidence does not obviously support that uniqueness.[^10]

Martin paired the three criteria with a quantitative demonstration. In 1975, Martin and the statistician James Mosimann published a simulation showing that a founding human population of a few thousand, advancing at plausible rates through naive megafaunal prey, could in principle extinguish the continent's large-bodied mammal fauna in three to five hundred years. The simulation was sensitive to assumptions (population growth rate, hunting success, prey density, the rate of population spread), but its central finding was robust across a wide band of those assumptions. The claim was not that the simulation proved overkill had happened; it was that a critic could no longer reject overkill on the grounds that a small human population could not plausibly cause continental extinction. A small human population could. That was the point the simulation made, and its significance for the debate was that it shifted the burden of argument. Afterward, the climate camp had to explain why something that was computationally plausible had not, in fact, happened.[^8] Martin's later articulation of a "blitzkrieg" model — a rapid wave of human advance through an ecologically unprepared fauna — is the popular form of the same argument, and it remains the most vivid framing of the overkill case.

Martin did not claim the overkill hypothesis was complete. He did claim — openly, and with the pugnacity the climate-change camp sometimes found tiresome — that it was the hypothesis most consistent with the global pattern, and that the climate-change camp had a harder time explaining the pattern than they usually admitted. "I will lay aside impartiality," he wrote in the opening of the chapter of *Twilight* that takes the debate head on. "I think the overkill theorists have the more convincing argument for what happened in America 10,000 years ago."[^11]

It is important to hold the integrity of this argument in view. Martin was not a polemicist. He was a paleoecologist who had spent half a century on the evidence and had reached a considered conclusion. The hypothesis carried his name, fairly, because he had done more than any other researcher in his generation to put it on firm empirical footing. Anyone who wishes to retire the overkill hypothesis, as many working in the field now effectively have, owes Martin the courtesy of retiring it for reasons the evidence supplies, not by gesturing vaguely at discomfort with its implications.

## Climate, Reconsidered

The climate-change hypothesis, as Martin encountered it in the 1950s, was the inherited default position of vertebrate paleontology. Most vertebrate extinctions in the Cenozoic fossil record had been accounted for by climate change, often in the absence of any more specific mechanism. Applying the same default to the near-time losses was, from inside the discipline, the conservative move.

Martin's three-criterion critique — time-transgressiveness, selectivity, uniqueness — landed hard on the default. The default response, in the decades since, has been to refine the climate mechanism rather than abandon it. Current climate-centric accounts do not usually claim that a single global cooling event extinguished the world's megafauna in one stroke. They claim, more modestly, that rapid regional shifts in temperature, precipitation, vegetation structure, and seasonality interacted with the life-history traits of particular species to push specific populations below recovery thresholds in specific places. Under this account, the climate mechanism is local rather than global, continuous rather than catastrophic, and cumulative rather than discrete.[^12]

The Younger Dryas cooling (an abrupt return to near-glacial conditions in the northern hemisphere around 12,800 calendar years before present, lasting roughly 1,300 years) does receive particular weight in these accounts for continental North America. The timing of the Younger Dryas onset aligns closely with the terminal Pleistocene extinction pulse in North America. The severity of the climate shift was substantial, involving rapid drops in North Atlantic surface temperatures and significant reorganization of precipitation patterns across the continent. The leading proximate mechanism in the paleoclimate literature is a freshwater pulse from the catastrophic drainage of glacial Lake Agassiz (an enormous proglacial lake along the retreating margin of the Laurentide ice sheet) whose outflow into the North Atlantic is thought to have disrupted the thermohaline circulation and triggered the cooling. The details of that mechanism, including the precise routing of the meltwater, continue to be actively argued in the literature; what is not argued is that the cooling was abrupt and that its continental consequences were substantial.[^13] Whether the shift was sharp enough to drive extinction of animals that had weathered dozens of comparable earlier shifts remains the unresolved point. Ice-core records from Greenland do show the Younger Dryas clearly; they also show earlier oscillations of comparable magnitude. The selectivity problem Martin identified — why this oscillation rather than the last twelve — continues to cut.

What the climate camp has credibly retrieved from the Martin critique is that *regional* climate dynamics matter. The loss of mammoth populations in the Siberian arctic, where some refugial mammoths persisted on Wrangel Island until roughly four thousand years ago and on St. Paul Island until roughly five thousand six hundred years ago, is difficult to explain by human pressure alone; the chronology shows that these populations outlasted terminal Pleistocene conditions by thousands of years, then disappeared under what appear to be largely climatic or habitat-fragmentation stresses.[^14] What the climate camp has not credibly retrieved is the claim that a single, unique, catastrophic climatic event explains the synchronous disappearance of an entire large-bodied mammal fauna. That stronger claim runs into Martin's critique as soon as it is made globally.

## The Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis

A third candidate entered the peer-reviewed literature in 2007. Richard Firestone and colleagues, in a paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, proposed that an extraterrestrial impact event at approximately 12,900 calendar years before present (a comet or fragmenting bolide, most likely an airburst over or near the retreating Laurentide ice sheet) had initiated the Younger Dryas cooling and contributed to the megafaunal extinctions. The authors identified a dark sedimentary layer, which they called the "black mat," at archaeological and paleontological sites across North America, dating to the Younger Dryas boundary. The black mat contained elevated concentrations of magnetic microspherules, carbon spherules, nanodiamonds, iridium, and other markers they interpreted as consistent with an impact signature.[^15]

The paper was controversial on publication and has remained so. Critics have challenged the claimed peak concentrations, the interpretation of the spherule and nanodiamond markers, the statistical robustness of the chronology, and the plausibility of the proposed mechanism. Several rigorous critical reviews have argued that the evidence for the impact is insufficient to support the strong causal claim, and that alternative explanations for some of the proposed markers (cosmic dust background, wildfire signatures, biological spherules) have not been adequately ruled out.[^16]

Two subsequent papers extended the physical case. In 2013, James Wittke and colleagues reported evidence for deposition of approximately ten million tonnes of impact spherules across roughly fifty million square kilometers on four continents, centered on the Younger Dryas boundary layer.[^17] In 2015, James Kennett and colleagues applied Bayesian chronological analysis to the black-mat horizons at twenty-three sites across four continents and reported age ranges consistent with synchronous deposition at 12,835 to 12,735 calendar years before present.[^18] The synchrony result does not by itself prove an impact — a rapid globally distributed sedimentary horizon can be produced by processes other than impact — but it is a feature of the record the hypothesis predicts and alternatives have to account for.

The hypothesis remains contested. It is not the consensus position in the paleoclimate or paleontological literature. It is, however, a peer-reviewed hypothesis that has accumulated a non-trivial evidentiary body over the last two decades, and it is the correct place for a serious discussion of catastrophe at the Younger Dryas boundary to take place. Any reader interested in the question of what happened at 12,900 BP should read Firestone 2007, Wittke 2013, and Kennett 2015 in the original, along with the major critiques of each, and form their own judgment.

I want to state clearly what this book's engagement with the hypothesis is and is not. This book engages the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis through the peer-reviewed literature only. It does not engage catastrophist popularizations of the hypothesis circulating in trade publishing or documentary media, which have tended to detach specific claims from the evidentiary context in which the peer-reviewed work has made them. The distinction matters. The peer-reviewed work is a serious scientific hypothesis, contested but defensible; the popular accounts are a separate cultural artifact and do not carry over.

## The Strongest Current Case for Human Causation

While the impact hypothesis was accumulating evidence, a distinct body of work was re-examining the human-causation case at global scale with tools Martin did not have. In 2014, Christopher Sandom, Søren Faurby, Brody Sandel, and Jens-Christian Svenning published a paper in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* that applied spatially explicit global analysis to the late-Quaternary extinctions, testing the relative contributions of climate change and human arrival as predictors of extinction severity. The analysis used extinction data for roughly 178 mammal species across all ice-free continents, paired against reconstructed climate change metrics for the period 132,000 to 1,000 years before present and against hominin colonization chronology for each region.

The result was unambiguous. Extinction severity was strongly and consistently predicted by hominin arrival; it was not consistently predicted by climate change. The authors wrote that their findings "provide empirical support for prehistoric humans being the main driver of the Late Quaternary Megafauna Extinction."[^19]

The Sandom paper is, in my reading, the strongest current empirical case for human causation as the primary driver. It is not Martin's argument — Martin had made the comparable point at regional scale with the tools available to him in 2005 — but it is Martin's argument extended with global data and statistical methods that did not exist when Martin was writing. Anyone attached to the overkill hypothesis has a stronger set of references to cite in 2026 than they did in 2006. The hypothesis has aged, but it has also sharpened.

What Sandom and colleagues did not claim is that climate played no role. Their result was about the *relative* contributions. Humans appeared to be the dominant driver; climate appeared to modulate the outcome in specific regions and for specific species. That is a multifactorial finding dressed as a human-cause headline, and it is best read that way.

## Toward a Multifactorial Synthesis

Anthony Stuart, in a 2015 synthesis in *Geological Journal*, drew the continent-by-continent picture the last decade of work had assembled.[^20] Stuart's synthesis does not crown a winner. It argues, across Eurasia, North America, South America, and Australia, that the near-time extinction pattern is best explained by the interaction of climate change and human pressure, with the relative weight of each varying by region, by species, and by the phase of the late Quaternary under examination.

In some regions, the climate contribution appears to have been substantial. The mammoths that persisted on Siberian refugia well past the terminal Pleistocene pulse and then disappeared in the middle Holocene were not, as far as the archaeological record shows, subjected to sustained human hunting pressure in a way that would explain their final extinction; habitat fragmentation and climate-driven vegetation change are the more plausible proximate causes. In other regions, the human contribution appears to have been decisive. The New Zealand moa extinctions within a few centuries of Polynesian arrival cannot be accounted for by climate change; there was no substantial climate change to account for. Most of the world's near-time extinctions sit between these cases, with both factors present and the relative contributions unresolved.

Stuart's synthesis is not a retreat into vagueness. It is a recognition that the question "what caused the near-time megafaunal extinctions" is itself a shorthand for a family of more specific questions, and the specific answers differ. What caused the loss of *Mammuthus columbi* in Arizona is not necessarily what caused the loss of *Mammuthus primigenius* on Wrangel Island five thousand years later. The synthesis allows the specific answers to be specific.

Notice what this does to the larger interpretive framework. If the causation is multifactorial, with climate and human pressure interacting differently in different places and for different species, the clean story of either a single human culprit or a single climatic catastrophe is replaced by a more layered account. The layered account is less satisfying as a headline. It is probably closer to what actually happened.

## Where I Now Stand

I have held more than one position on this material. I read *Twilight of the Mammoths* in 2012, shortly after finishing the first version of this book, and was persuaded quickly. For a long stretch afterward, into the essays I published in the middle of the 2010s, I wrote as though the overkill hypothesis was effectively settled, and as though the rewilding case carried an ethical weight drawn from collective human responsibility for the extinctions. My 2015 and 2016 writing, which I have since retired, states the human-causation position flatly. I now think that framing was too confident.

What I missed at the time was exactly the material the present chapter has walked through: the peer-reviewed work on the Younger Dryas oscillation and its mechanisms, the Firestone and Wittke and Kennett papers on the impact hypothesis, the Stuart synthesis, and the global statistical work (including Sandom 2014) that has made the human-causation case more defensible but also more bounded than Martin's original formulation was. Read across the whole corpus, I am now not confident that the extinctions had a single dominant cause. I am confident they were heavily human-involved in most regions. I am also confident that climate oscillation, potentially triggered or amplified by the impact event the Firestone hypothesis describes, contributed materially in at least some regions and at least for some species.

This revision is not a repudiation of Martin. Martin's critique of mid-century climate-centric accounts was correct on the terms in which those accounts were being made. The subsequent refinement of the climate side of the debate has partly answered Martin's critique, and the subsequent accumulation of impact-hypothesis evidence has supplied a mechanism Martin did not have available to consider. The overkill hypothesis in its strong 2005 form is not quite where the field now stands. The overkill hypothesis in a weaker, multifactor-inflected form is, I think, the best reading of the current evidence.

I want to be explicit about what changes when one moves from a strong-overkill view to a multifactorial view, because the change is not merely scholarly. It alters the moral inflection of the rewilding case. The strong-overkill framing carries with it, often implicitly, an accounting of debt: humans caused the extinctions, humans ought to consider repair. The multifactorial framing does not carry that accounting. Humans may or may not have caused the extinctions — in any given region, in any given case, with any given confidence — and the case for restoration stands or falls on something other than collective human guilt. I think the case stands. I think it stands more cleanly when it is not entangled with an attribution question the evidence does not cleanly resolve.

It is worth naming, plainly, that I arrived at the strong-overkill position by reading Martin first and reading the challenges to Martin much later. That is a reasonably common trajectory for readers drawn to the rewilding argument, and I am not the only author to have walked it. The corrective, when it came, came from reading the Quaternary climate literature carefully, from taking the impact hypothesis seriously as a peer-reviewed proposition rather than dismissing it because of what had been made of it downstream, and from the simple act of sitting with Stuart's synthesis long enough to notice that it was not a split-the-difference move but a closer reading of the actual evidence than either pole had offered. A revised position of this kind is not a loss of confidence in the subject. It is a gain of confidence in the shape of what is known and what is not.

## What the Rewilding Case Actually Depends On

The argument the rest of the book is going to make rests on an ecological observation, not a historical one.

The observation is that an ecosystem missing its keystone large-bodied animals functions differently than one in which those animals are present, and functions worse by a number of measurable metrics: seed dispersal, nutrient cycling, vegetation structure, predator-prey regulation, fire regime, soil compaction and turnover. The observation is independent of why the animals are absent. The continent is without mammoths, ground sloths, and native equids for whatever reason. The reason does not change the ecological consequence. A grassland that evolved under proboscidean herbivory is poorer in structure and function without proboscidean herbivory, whether the proboscideans were hunted to extinction, cooled to extinction, or died in some combination of the two. A fruit evolved for a gomphothere is abandoned by its disperser regardless of the cause of the gomphothere's disappearance. A predator-prey calibration between cheetah and pronghorn is one-sided whether the cheetah was speared by a Clovis hunter or starved in a Younger Dryas winter.

This is the chapter's load-bearing point, and it is where the chapter lands. Near time is the window in which the present ecological assemblage was stripped of much of its large-bodied biomass. The stripping was probably multifactorial. Apportioning blame is a worthy scientific question that the literature will continue to refine. The decision of whether to attempt restoration does not depend on the blame question's resolution. It depends on whether we take the observation seriously that the stripped system is still, twelve thousand years later, functioning as a stripped system — incompletely, with unfilled niches, with species waiting on partners that are not coming back on their own.

The continents that lost their megafauna have not recovered the function those animals performed. No continent has. The fossil fruits still fall, the grasslands still miss their grazers, the pronghorn still runs from nothing. Whatever the cause of the missing animals was, the absences are still here, and they are still absences. The next part of this book asks what, knowing that, we should do.
