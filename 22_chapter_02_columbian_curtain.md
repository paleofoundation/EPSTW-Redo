# Chapter 2: The Columbian Curtain

The story most Americans carry about the horse is this: horses came from Spain. The Spanish brought them in the early 1500s, and before that there were no horses on the continent. This is, as far as it goes, true. It is also among the most misleading true things in the country's ecological imagination, because the sentence after it almost always gets dropped.

The sentence after it is that horses evolved here.

The genus *Equus* originated in North America roughly four million years ago and diversified on this continent for most of its evolutionary history.[^1] Horses walked out across the Bering land bridge, populated Eurasia and Africa, and continued to evolve there, eventually producing the animals that would be domesticated on the Eurasian steppe some six thousand years ago. Meanwhile, the horses that had stayed in their continent of origin went on being American horses until the end of the Pleistocene, when all of them disappeared in the same wave of extinctions that took the camels and the mammoths.[^2]

> **Sidebar — Definition: *Equus* and the deep equid record**
>
> The genus *Equus* — the lineage that includes every living horse, donkey, and zebra — is the crown of a much longer equid story. Before *Equus* the continent already had a succession of horse-family genera: *Dinohippus*, its likely direct ancestor, already essentially modern in body plan and single-toed; before that, the three-toed *Pliohippus*; before that, the grazing and browsing radiation of *Merychippus* and its contemporaries; and before those, the small, multi-toed browsers of the early Miocene. The equid family has been running on this continent for close to twenty million years. *Equus* is the last four million of that twenty. When paleontologists say horses evolved in North America, they are not saying one species showed up here, stayed a while, and left. They are saying the entire deep history of the family played out on this continent, and the Spanish ships of the sixteenth century were returning the crown of a tree whose roots had been American the whole time.

> **Sidebar — Case: The American horses by name**
>
> The late-Pleistocene equid fauna of North America went by several specific names, and the specifics matter if only to show that this was not one horse but many. *Equus scotti* was large, stout-bodied, and widely distributed across the southern and central portions of the continent; its body mass places it comfortably within the range of modern domestic horses. *Equus conversidens* was smaller and more gracile, centered on what is now Mexico and the American Southwest. *Equus lambei*, the Yukon horse, persisted in the Alaskan and Yukon refugia; the Thistle Creek genome that confirmed the deep relationship between late-Pleistocene American horses and modern *Equus caballus* came from this population. Other named forms — *Equus francisci*, *Equus complicatus*, *Equus occidentalis* — fill out the record with varying degrees of preservation. The point of the list is not taxonomic tidiness. American horses were a diversified fauna occupying a range of habitats and body sizes, and the wave of terminal-Pleistocene extinctions took the whole set.

Genetic work in the last two decades has tightened the story. In 2013 a team led by Ludovic Orlando reported a complete nuclear genome recovered from a horse bone frozen in permafrost at Thistle Creek in the Canadian Yukon. The bone is dated to the middle Pleistocene, around seven hundred thousand years old. The genome clustered within the diversity of modern *Equus*, confirming that the domestic horse and the Late Pleistocene American horse are close members of a single long-running lineage — the same animal, in a deep sense, with its genetic lights never quite going out.[^3] Earlier mitochondrial work by Jaco Weinstock and colleagues had reached a similar conclusion from Alaskan and Yukon Pleistocene material: North American late-Pleistocene horses fell within, not outside, the modern *Equus caballus* / *Equus ferus* group.[^4] The phylogeny does not particularly care which ships crossed which ocean. A horse on the plains in 1600 is the same kind of animal as a horse on the plains in 15,000 BP. The gap between the two dates is the question.

The gap is also not, by any reasonable ecological measure, a long one. Twelve and a half thousand years sounds like a great deal in the scale of a human life. It is not very much in the scale over which grassland plant communities, ungulate digestive physiology, or soil microbial assemblages evolve. The grasses of the central plains spent tens of millions of years evolving in the presence of large-bodied grazers of the horse's general architecture. Their defenses, their growth habits, their response to compaction and disturbance are calibrated to those animals and to their relatives. Thirteen thousand years of relative quiet on that front is not enough time for those calibrations to disappear. When the Spanish horse arrives on the American plains, it does not enter an ecosystem naive to its body plan. It enters one that has been waiting.

That gap is what the standard story hides. Told as "the Spanish brought horses," the sentence puts the entire evolutionary record of the genus outside the frame. Told as "horses returned to their continent of origin with the Spanish, after an absence of roughly thirteen thousand years," the sentence includes the record, and the ecological meaning of the animals' arrival changes. They are not an introduction from elsewhere. They are a homecoming that took the scenic route.

This is the conceptual move the chapter is about. Nothing in the fossil record changes whether we call the horse native or non-native. What changes is our choice of when to start counting.

## The Baseline Is a Choice

The dominant framework of American conservation treats the year 1492 as the effective ecological baseline for the continent. Animals present here when Columbus made landfall are categorized as native. Animals not present are categorized as non-native, invasive, exotic, or feral, depending on the disposition of the speaker and the legal category in which the animal has been slotted. The vocabulary varies; the baseline does not. Under this framework, the horse that returns with the Spanish is non-native. The burro that escapes into the Grand Canyon watershed in the nineteenth century is feral. The cattle that replace bison across the western grasslands are livestock rather than wildlife, even where they live in effectively free-ranging herds. Each classification depends not on the animal's relationship to the land but on whether its species was on the continent on a particular October morning in the late fifteenth century.

Paul S. Martin, who spent most of his career as a paleoecologist at the University of Arizona, named this framing the Columbian Curtain. The prologue has introduced it. I want us to take the name more seriously here than the prologue had room to. A curtain is the right image. It is not a hard wall. It is not a border or a boundary in any physical sense. It is a visual instrument — a piece of fabric hung at a particular point in time, with everything behind it obscured from view. The observer standing in front of the curtain treats whatever is in front of it as the whole of the room. Whatever is behind it is out of sight and, therefore, out of the conversation.

Martin's argument was not that the curtain is wrong in the sense that 1492 is a fake date. European contact is a real event, and it does mark one of the largest biotic exchanges in the planet's recent history. His argument was that the curtain is arbitrary. There is nothing about the ecological state of North America in 1492 that makes it the correct reference point against which to measure restoration. The continent Columbus encountered had been losing large-bodied fauna for twelve and a half thousand years by the time he arrived.[^5] More than forty species of large mammals were already absent from North America before a single Spanish sail appeared on the horizon. Calling that assemblage "the native condition" is a bit like calling a library "complete" on the strength of its remaining shelves, after most of its collection has been carted off.

Granted, every historical science has to choose a reference point somewhere. Paleontology works in sliding windows and does not pretend they have natural edges. The choice of 1492 as the baseline for "native" was not a deliberate scientific decision so much as a cultural inheritance. The framing came downstream from the history we were telling ourselves about the continent. The arrival of Europeans was the episode after which record-keeping, in the form that came to count, began. The animals present at that moment became the reference animals, and the ones that had been here earlier dropped out of the frame along with everything else the record did not carry forward. The baseline is a cultural artifact dressed as an ecological one.

Notice what the baseline does once it is fixed. A horse that shows up in the sixteenth century is, under this frame, an introduction. A horse that was here in the fourteenth millennium BP is not an ancestor to which the arriving animal is returning; it is an unrelated animal in an unrelated world. A burro roaming the Colorado Plateau is not occupying an ecological niche left behind by extinct American equids; it is a non-native species degrading a native landscape. The entire vocabulary of invasion biology, as applied to these animals, assumes the 1492 baseline. Change the baseline and you do not change whether the animal is doing what it is doing. You change what you call it.

The Bureau of Land Management currently manages roughly eighty-three thousand free-roaming horses and burros across ten western states, at levels its own appropriate-management-level estimates describe as well above the range at which the agency believes the rangeland can be sustainably grazed.[^6] The resulting program involves gathers, long-term holding facilities, fertility-control trials, and a political conflict that has continued for decades without much resolution. I am not here to resolve the management dispute. I want only to point out that the argument, as currently constructed, rests on a baseline choice. A horse on BLM land is treated as a non-native animal whose population must be reduced to protect a native range. Push the baseline back eleven thousand years and the same horse is a partially returned native whose relationship with the range is something the range has not experienced in a long time. The animals are the same. The analytical frame is different. The management implications are different. And the frame is almost never examined.

This is the first thing the Columbian Curtain hides: that it is a frame at all, rather than a fact.

## The Vocabulary of Invasion

The baseline does not stand alone. It is propped up by a vocabulary that carries the baseline's assumptions into every sentence in which the vocabulary appears. Once the vocabulary is in circulation, one can argue within it for decades without ever noticing that the argument has already been shaped by what the words assume.

"Invasive" is the clearest case. The term is doing real work in ecology and conservation biology — there are species introductions that cause measurable ecological harm, and a name for the category is useful. But the term is defined, operationally, against the 1492 baseline. A species is invasive, under the standard definitions deployed by state and federal agencies, if it has been introduced to an ecosystem in which it was not previously present and is causing or likely to cause harm.[^12] "Previously" here, in nearly every operative framework, means "at the time of European contact." The definition is internally coherent, and it reflects a genuine ecological concern about the rate at which human commerce has moved species across biogeographic barriers in the last five centuries. What it cannot see, by construction, is any species whose "introduction" is in fact a reintroduction at a deeper timescale.

"Non-native" carries the same assumption in a softer form. "Exotic" adds a faint aesthetic flavor, the suggestion that the animal is elsewhere's animal visiting. "Feral" contains an additional implication about the animal's relationship to human management, and typically marks an animal as out of place. All of these words are useful within their proper scope. All of them encode the 1492 baseline as a hidden premise.

The vocabulary produces one further effect worth flagging. A well-adjusted ecological assemblage (one that, under a 1492 baseline, is categorized as intact) can be assumed to be self-sustaining unless shown otherwise. A species classified as invasive can be assumed to be harmful unless shown otherwise. The burden of proof is asymmetric, and the asymmetry follows from the baseline. Reverse the baseline and the burden shifts. A species whose lineage was present on the continent for most of the last four million years does not become categorically benign by virtue of its lineage — there can be real ecological reasons to manage specific populations at specific densities — but the question of its presence stops being foreclosed by vocabulary. It becomes a question to argue.

This is, I think, the quieter cost of the Columbian Curtain. The baseline is not sitting in conservation textbooks as a debated proposition. It is sitting in the verbs and adjectives used to describe every animal on the continent, and most practitioners are using it without noticing.

## Near Time

A paleoecologist looking at the continent in 1492 does not see a native baseline. They see a system very recently stripped of most of its large-mammal biomass, in the middle of a long readjustment that has not yet stabilized. The frame in which this is visible is what Ross MacPhee, in the 1999 edited volume that gave the idea its current name, calls *near time* — the last fifty thousand years or so of Earth history, the window during which most of the world's large-mammal extinctions have occurred and the present biota has taken its current shape.[^7]

Near time is not a sentimental concept. It is the timescale on which the actual cast of the current ecological play was assembled, because most of the changes that produced the present continent happened within it. Plate tectonics and long-term climate shifts operate on vastly deeper scales. The specific animals and plants in any given ecosystem today, and the gaps in those ecosystems where animals used to be, settled into their current arrangement inside the near-time window. If we want to know what a continent was before humans arranged it into the present, near time is the window to look through.

MacPhee's framing also sets the stakes. The volume assembles case studies from every inhabited continent and major island group, and the synthesis across cases is unambiguous: the world has lost an extraordinary proportion of its large-bodied fauna within this window. Island losses are in some cases total. Continental losses are proportional but massive. Martin's own summary of the North American figure — more than forty large-mammal species extinct within near time, and thirty-five or so of them gone in the terminal Pleistocene pulse around thirteen thousand years ago — sits within this larger pattern rather than outside it. South America lost more than fifty genera. Australia lost nearly all of its megafauna tens of thousands of years earlier. The near-time extinctions are the single largest event shaping the modern ecological assemblages we inherit.[^8]

The comparison to what was there is sobering in a way statistics alone cannot convey. Using the late-Pleistocene fossil record, paleontologists estimate that the mean body mass of large mammals in North America during the period immediately before the terminal extinctions was roughly comparable to the modern East African savanna.[^9] The Great Plains supported proboscidean biomass. The southwestern basins supported camelid and equid biomass. The eastern woodlands supported an assemblage of ground sloths, peccaries, and tapirs whose nearest living analogues are neotropical. The continent Europeans encountered was a structurally different place from the continent that had existed twelve thousand years earlier, and the difference was not an improvement or a maturation or a natural development. It was a subtraction.

Near time is the window in which that subtraction happened. To take the 1492 baseline as "native" is to take the post-subtraction assemblage as the reference. It is to treat the end result of a mass extinction as the default state. The near-time frame does something different. It asks what the continent was like across the whole window in which the present system came together, and it lets the observer see the losses as losses rather than as the background against which all subsequent change is measured.

This is not a rewilding argument yet. It is a framing argument. The near-time window is the analytically honest timescale for thinking about what any given continent is now, because it is the window in which what it is now was assembled. Using any shorter window — five hundred years, two hundred years, the span of European land-use records — is not wrong, exactly, but it is structurally unable to see the shape of the assemblage's losses. A shorter window can tell us how a given forest has changed since the timber surveys of the 1870s. It cannot tell us what the forest is missing on a scale that matters to how the forest functions.

The near-time argument has a close cousin in fisheries science, and the parallel is worth drawing because it arrived at a similar insight from a different direction. In 1995 the fisheries biologist Daniel Pauly published a short essay in *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* naming what he called "shifting baseline syndrome."[^13] Pauly had noticed that each generation of fisheries scientists tended to take the species composition and abundance of the oceans at the start of their own careers as the reference condition against which subsequent change was measured. A scientist trained in the 1970s considered the 1970s ocean normal and was alarmed by the changes since then. A scientist trained in the 1990s considered the 1990s ocean normal and did not find the reduced abundance alarming, since it was what they had always known. Each generation ratcheted the reference condition down. The cumulative effect, across several generations, was that the "natural" state of the oceans slid progressively further from anything the oceans had been before industrial fishing began.

Pauly's essay was a disciplinary turning point within fisheries biology. It did what this chapter is trying to do for terrestrial ecology: it named the assumption hiding in the reference condition. The syndrome Pauly described is not quite identical to the one the Columbian Curtain produces — Pauly's diagnosis involves a rolling baseline that shifts with each observer, while the Columbian baseline is fixed at a particular date — but the underlying structure is the same. Both are cases where the reference condition is a recent artifact, shaped by the observer's position in time, mistaken for a deep property of the system. Both can be corrected only by stepping back to a longer frame.

In the fisheries case, the longer frame looks to historical records — ship logs, cannery data, colonial-era fish markets, archaeological fish-bone middens — to reconstruct what abundance was before the baseline slipped. In the terrestrial case, the longer frame is paleontological: the fossil record, the dung caves, the bone beds, the radiocarbon chronologies. The data are different. The corrective move is the same. Find the frame the observer has inherited, locate the assumption it is making about where to start counting, and then look further back.

I find the parallel reassuring in the sense that it was arrived at independently by people working on an unrelated problem. The 1492 baseline on land and the rolling baseline at sea are not identical pathologies, but the cognitive mechanism behind both is the same, and the correction in both cases is the same.

## What a Baseline Is For

A baseline does three kinds of work in conservation. It tells us what to count as native. It tells us what to count as damage. And it tells us what to count as restoration. Change the baseline and all three categories rearrange.

Consider the first. Under the 1492 baseline, the native vertebrates of Arizona are the ones present when Coronado's expedition passed through in the 1540s — a set that includes pronghorn, mule deer, bighorn sheep, elk, a handful of smaller ungulates, and the surviving predators. Under a near-time baseline, the native vertebrates of Arizona include those animals *and* the camelids, equids, and proboscideans whose fossils are distributed across the state's late-Pleistocene sediments, some of which are well enough preserved to appear in local natural-history museums within an hour's drive of many of the state's largest school districts. The animals are the same animals either way. Whether they count as native to the state is entirely a function of which window the question is being asked across.

Consider the second. Damage, in an ecological assessment, is usually measured against a baseline — the reference condition the system has diverged from. Under the 1492 baseline, damage to the American shortgrass prairie is the conversion of grassland to cropland, the loss of bison, the encroachment of mesquite, the overgrazing of exhausted cattle regimes. All of those are real and serious. Under a near-time baseline, damage to the same prairie includes all of those *and* the absence of the proboscidean biomass that likely contributed to the system's historical openness, the absence of the American cheetah that shaped the pronghorn's running speed, the absence of the ground sloths that may have played a role in seed dispersal for some of the legumes still standing there now. The losses the Columbian Curtain hides do not cease to be losses because the curtain is hiding them. They simply cease to be counted.

Consider the third. Restoration, under the 1492 baseline, means returning a degraded landscape to something resembling its state at the time of European contact. It is the frame against which Park Service management plans, Forest Service stewardship documents, and most published habitat-restoration studies are written. Under a near-time baseline, restoration can still include all of the 1492 targets, but the target list expands: it includes restoring, where possible, the large-bodied animals and ecological relationships that were present before the terminal Pleistocene losses. The two frames do not necessarily conflict in practice on short time horizons — returning bison to the plains scores well under both frames, and reforesting cleared eastern hardwoods scores well under both — but they begin to diverge on the margin. Reintroducing American burros to public land scores as damage under the 1492 frame and as partial restoration under the near-time frame. Considering camels as proxies for the extinct American camelids of the Southwest is, under the 1492 frame, not even a conversation worth having; under the near-time frame, it is a hypothesis worth testing.

The point is not that one frame is correct and the other is mistaken. The point is that both frames are choices, with different consequences, and the choice is not intellectually neutral. Conservation biology has, on the whole, chosen the 1492 baseline without examining the choice closely. The result is that a great many restoration proposals that would be coherent under a near-time baseline are ruled out before they can be evaluated. They are non-starters because of the frame, not because of the evidence.

I want to be precise about what I am claiming. I am not claiming that any restoration proposal that invokes the Pleistocene baseline is automatically a good one. I am not claiming that species introductions should be attempted casually. I am not claiming that conservation biologists who work under the 1492 baseline are foolish, or that the 1492 baseline produces worse outcomes than alternative frames in every case. Many of the people who have shaped the 1492 baseline into its current form are serious ecologists whose work I have learned from. The dominant paradigm is not dominant by accident.

What I am claiming is that the baseline is a choice, that the choice shapes the outcome, and that serious conservation thinking requires naming the baseline rather than treating it as a given. Once the baseline is named, it can be argued about. Until it is named, the argument is smuggled into every premise.

## What Is on the Other Side

The prologue made the point through camels and an osage orange orchard. I will not revisit either here, except to say that they are instances of a pattern, and the pattern is worth naming.

On the other side of the Columbian Curtain, the North American fauna is a different assemblage from the one Americans grew up knowing. The flatlands held proboscideans. The broken country of the Southwest held equids and camelids in numbers that, by modern standards, seem implausible. Eastern hardwoods held ground sloths — at least two genera of them, *Megalonyx* and *Eremotherium*, the smaller of which was roughly the size of an ox and the larger of which approached the size of a modern elephant. The canyons of the West held cats whose closest living relatives are African. The boreal north held mammoths until approximately 10,500 years before present in some refugial populations.[^10]

The list, stated all at once, has a faintly unreal quality for most readers. It reads like a natural-history exhibit rather than an inventory. But the animals were not mythical. They left bones in amounts that were once, and in some places still are, routinely exposed by arroyo cutting after heavy rains. They left dung in caves dry enough to preserve it for ten thousand years.[^11] They left tracks turned to stone. They left, in some cases, well-characterized genetic material. They were as concrete as any animal on the continent today. The only thing odd about them, from a modern standpoint, is that they are gone.

The recovery record is not speculative. It is grounded in physical evidence that can be held, measured, dated, and in many cases sequenced. Ground sloth coprolites from dry caves on the Colorado Plateau have yielded preserved plant material detailed enough to reconstruct the animals' browse preferences across thousands of years of occupation.[^14] Mammoth material recovered from permafrost has supported nuclear genome sequencing in the same rough ballpark as the Thistle Creek horse. Tar-seep deposits at Rancho La Brea in southern California have produced preserved animal bodies by the tens of thousands, from dire wolves to saber-toothed cats to the American lion *Panthera atrox*, with radiocarbon-dated material spanning roughly fifty thousand years.[^15] The late Pleistocene is not prehistory in the sense of being inaccessibly remote. It is prehistory in the sense that it predates writing. Much of what we know about it, we know at a resolution comparable to the resolution at which we know the historical Roman road network — from the ground, from what was left behind, from dating the physical evidence.

I am not going to make the rewilding case here. That case belongs to later chapters — to the chapter on what proxy species might plausibly be considered, to the chapter on the specific restoration work already under way, to the chapter that takes the critics of Pleistocene rewilding seriously and answers the criticisms I think can be answered. Here I only want the reader to hold, briefly, the shape of the other side.

The continent on the other side of the Curtain is not exotic. It is the continent the land itself still remembers. Grasses on the plains evolved under the hooves and digestive tracts of animals whose modern descendants are managed as livestock or warehoused on reservations or gone altogether. Soil communities in the Southwest evolved under trampling patterns that have not been produced in twelve thousand years. Predator-prey dynamics in the high canyons evolved around hunters whose bodies are in museum drawers. The landscape is not recovering from a bad decade. It is not recovering from European contact. It is, on the whole, still in the long thaw after a much larger subtraction.

The Columbian Curtain is the frame that makes this hard to see. Drop the frame and it becomes legible.

## Native to When

I opened this chapter with the horse because the horse is the cleanest case for the argument, and because it is a case the evidence has already essentially made on its own. The horse of the Plains is both a sixteenth-century immigrant and a thirteen-thousand-year returnee. Under a 1492 baseline, it is the former. Under a near-time baseline, it is the latter. The only question is which window we are using to look.

The question the chapter lands on is not whether horses are native, or whether camels would be native if reintroduced, or whether burros belong in the Grand Canyon. Each of those is a specific question that requires its own specific argument. The question the chapter lands on is the one upstream of all of them, the question the 1492 baseline has silently answered without asking: *native to when?*

Every conservation decision rests on an implicit answer to this question. The answer is rarely stated, and it is often buried under vocabulary that presents the baseline as natural rather than chosen. Once the question is stated in the open, every subsequent decision becomes a live argument rather than a settled one. We can argue about the right window. We can argue about what to do about particular species under particular windows. We can argue about the risks of different proposals. What we cannot do, once the question is in the open, is pretend that conservation has been doing anything other than drawing a time-line through the ecological past and calling one side of it native.

I want to note one thing about how the argument will develop from here, because it matters for the register in which the rest of the book is written. The point of naming the Columbian Curtain is not to discredit the practitioners who have worked within it. A great deal of serious conservation biology has been done under a 1492 frame, and a great deal of that work has produced real results. Wetland restoration in coastal North America, the recovery of the bald eagle and the American alligator, the return of the gray wolf to parts of its historic range, the ongoing expansion of bison onto grassland that has not seen significant bison presence in over a century — all of these operate within the 1492 baseline and all of them represent genuine conservation gains. None of this chapter's argument dismisses any of that. The argument is additive, not subtractive. The near-time frame does not replace the shorter-window frames used in existing conservation work; it makes visible a set of additional considerations that the shorter windows cannot see. The chapters that follow ask what those additional considerations are, what we should do with them, and where they lead.

The baseline is a choice. It has always been a choice. The work of the rest of the book, in large part, is to argue that the choice should be made deliberately rather than inherited without examination, and that when it is made deliberately, the near-time window is the one most consistent with what we actually know about the ecosystems we inhabit. A baseline chosen deliberately is still a baseline; it will have its own exclusions and its own assumptions, and it will need to be defended with the same honesty required to defend any other analytical frame. The difference is that a deliberate baseline can be argued against, refined, and corrected. The inherited one, by design, resists each of those moves.

But the argument begins here. With the horse returning to its continent of origin after a long absence, and a culture that mostly has not noticed the return. With a baseline dressed as a fact. With a continent whose memory is older than its paperwork. Native to when is the question. Near time is, I think, the defensible answer.

The next chapter asks what was lost in that window, and when, and what we know and do not know about why.
