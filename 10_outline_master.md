# Master Outline: *The Sustainability Diet*

**Working title:** *The Sustainability Diet: A Framework for Eating Systems That Survive*
**Target length:** 65,000 words (first draft) → 60,000 words (final)
**Structure:** Prologue + 13 chapters + epilogue
**Voice:** First-person, warm-then-direct-then-precise, slightly more formal than the author's spoken register

---

## Architectural Logic

The book has four parts and a frame. Each part does one job; together they walk the reader from recognition (something is wrong with how we evaluate food systems) through analysis (here is a better framework) to vision (here is what becomes possible when the framework is applied honestly) to practice (here is what to do about it).

The frame — prologue and epilogue — exists to give the book its emotional spine. Without the frame, the book is an analytical argument. With it, the book is also a longing.

**Part I: Seeing.** Chapters 1–3. Establishes the problem with the dominant ecological imagination. Introduces Paul Martin's reframing of "native." Walks the reader through what was lost in near time and what that loss means for how we think about restoration today.

**Part II: What We Eat Now.** Chapters 4–6. Introduces the survivability axis as the missing leg of sustainability assessment. Applies the resulting four-axis framework to the major food systems. Includes an honest assessment of regenerative agriculture's structural ceiling.

**Part III: What We Could Eat.** Chapters 7–9. The Savory food-truck scene as the bridge from regenerative agriculture into proxy selection. The vision of rewilding the plate. Honest engagement with the serious critics of Pleistocene rewilding.

**Part IV: Doing.** Chapters 10–13. Indigenous food systems and water-column rewilding as parallel restoration frameworks. Bioregional and urban applications. Practical guidance derived from the framework. Policy and culture.

## Frame

### Prologue: One Hundred Camels

**Length target:** 1,800–2,200 words
**Function:** Set the book's emotional spine. Plant the image of camels and an osage orange orchard as the longing the rest of the book exists to articulate.

**Argument arc:**
The prologue opens with Camelops hesternus — the Western camel, native to North America, hunted or extinguished by approximately 13,000 years ago. The opening is concrete and surprising: most readers do not know camels were once American. The reader's ecological imagination is dislodged in the first 200 words.

From Camelops the prologue widens to the larger argument about ecological memory: what was lost, what we have forgotten, what we now treat as "natural" that is in fact a depleted system. Paul Martin enters by name, briefly. The prologue does not yet do the conceptual work of the Columbian Curtain (that is Chapter 2's job); it gestures toward it.

The prologue closes with the author's own admission: somewhere, she would like to see one hundred camels and an osage orange orchard, just to see if it could work. The longing is named, plainly. The reader knows by the end of the prologue that this is a book written from a particular kind of want.

**Key moves:**
- Camelops as opening fact
- The mistake of using 1492 as the ecological baseline
- Brief introduction of Paul Martin
- The osage orange detail (its giant inedible-to-modern-fauna fruits as evidence of what's missing)
- Closing with the author's own longing, plainly stated

**Sources to cite:**
- Martin, P. S. (2005). *Twilight of the Mammoths: Ice Age Extinctions and the Rewilding of America.* University of California Press.
- Janzen, D. H., & Martin, P. S. (1982). Neotropical anachronisms: the fruits the gomphotheres ate. *Science*, 215(4528), 19–27. (For osage orange and the megafaunal-fruit-syndrome argument.)

**What this chapter does NOT do:**
- Does not introduce the survivability framework (that is Chapter 4)
- Does not engage Younger Dryas causation in detail (that is Chapter 3)
- Does not make any health claims about diet
- Does not name the book's enemies (the book has interlocutors, not enemies)

### Epilogue: Returning to the Orchard

**Length target:** 1,500–2,000 words
**Function:** Close the frame. Return to the camels. Convert the longing into a question the reader can sit with.

**Argument arc:**
The epilogue returns to the orchard image. By this point the reader has been through 60,000 words of framework, evidence, and vision. The orchard has accumulated meaning. The author asks, plainly, what it would take to actually try this — not just camels and osage oranges, but the broader proposal the book has made.

The epilogue is not triumphant. It is honest. The book has made an argument that is partial, contested, and dependent on conditions the author does not control. What the author can do is name the experiment that should be tried and ask the reader to consider whether it is worth the trying.

**Key moves:**
- Return to camels and osage orange
- Acknowledgment of what the book has not solved
- The honest position: the framework is a proposal; the rewilding case is a hypothesis worth testing; the work continues
- Final image: the orchard, still hypothetical, still possible

## Part I: Seeing

### Chapter 1: The Meat Counter

**Length target:** 4,500–5,000 words
**Function:** Ground the book's argument in a particular moment of recognition. Establish the author's credibility through specificity rather than credentials.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter opens at the Whole Foods meat counter in Overland Park, Kansas, in September 2009. The author is standing in front of grass-fed beef that turns out to be grain-finished. The moment of recognition is small and concrete: the label said one thing; the practice was another.

From that specific moment the chapter widens into the larger pattern. The food labeling system is structurally inadequate to convey what readers want to know. "Grass-fed," "organic," "free-range," "pasture-raised," "humane" — each term certifies something narrower than readers assume, and the gap between assumption and reality is where the food system hides its actual practices.

The chapter does not yet propose a framework. Its job is to make the reader feel the inadequacy of the existing tools for thinking about food systems, so that when the framework arrives in Chapter 4, it lands as an answer to a problem the reader has now felt.

**Key scenes:**
- The meat counter conversation (revised from the 2012 manuscript, voice transformed, names abstracted where appropriate)
- A second short scene illustrating the same pattern with another label term

**Key moves:**
- Personal narrative as evidence, not as memoir
- The structural observation that labels are designed to communicate less, not more
- The hand-off to the rest of the book: if labels can't tell us, what can?

**What this chapter does NOT do:**
- Does not introduce the four-axis framework (Chapter 4)
- Does not name Salatin or other figures critically (Chapter 6)
- Does not make the rewilding case (Part III)

### Chapter 2: The Columbian Curtain

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Make the central conceptual move of the book. Reframe "native" as a question of timescale rather than a fixed reference point.

**Argument arc:**
Adapted and substantially extended from the 2015 essay. The chapter opens with the horse — the standard story (Spanish ships brought horses to the Americas in the 1500s) and the deeper story (horses evolved on the North American continent for tens of millions of years, migrated out, went locally extinct, and returned with the Spanish to their continent of origin).

From the horse the chapter introduces Paul Martin's "Columbian Curtain": the conservation paradigm that treats 1492 as the ecological baseline. The chapter argues that this baseline is arbitrary and that the choice of baseline determines what we count as restoration versus introduction. With a 1492 baseline, returning camels to North America is "introducing a non-native species." With a Pleistocene baseline, it is restoring a long-absent native.

The chapter does not yet make the case for actually doing this (that is Chapters 7–9). Its job is to dislodge the assumption that "native" is a fixed category and replace it with the question: native to when?

**Key sources:**
- Martin (2005), *Twilight of the Mammoths*, especially the "Columbian Curtain" framing
- The 2015 essay's horse-as-returning-native argument, expanded with current paleontological literature
- MacPhee, R. D. E., ed. (1999). *Extinctions in Near Time: Causes, Contexts, and Consequences.* Plenum Press. (For the "near time" frame.)

**Key moves:**
- The horse story as the Trojan horse for the larger argument
- Definition and naming of the Columbian Curtain
- The argument that baseline selection is itself an ethical and ecological choice
- Brief preview of what other animals were here when the Curtain falls (camels, mammoths, ground sloths, American cheetahs, lions) without yet making the rewilding case

**What this chapter does NOT do:**
- Does not yet engage Younger Dryas causation (Chapter 3)
- Does not yet propose specific proxy species (Chapter 7)
- Does not engage rewilding's critics (Chapter 9)

### Chapter 3: Near Time

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Walk the reader through what was lost and when. Engage the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis literature honestly. Establish that the Late Pleistocene extinctions were likely multifactorial rather than purely human-caused.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter establishes the time frame. "Near time" — the last 50,000 years — is the period during which the planet lost half of its 200 genera of large animals. North America lost more than forty species of large animals in this window. The chapter walks the reader through the major losses: the mammoths, the mastodons, the ground sloths, the American camels, the American horses, the dire wolves, the saber-toothed cats, the American cheetahs, the giant beavers.

The chapter then engages the question of cause. Historically the dominant explanation has been overhunting by Clovis-era humans (the "overkill hypothesis," strongly associated with Paul Martin himself in his earlier work). The chapter presents this hypothesis fairly and notes that Martin championed it.

It then presents the alternative and complementary hypotheses: rapid climate oscillation during the Younger Dryas (~12,800 years ago); the cosmic impact hypothesis (Firestone et al. 2007 and the subsequent body of work); habitat change; disease vectors. The current scientific position is that the extinctions were likely multifactorial, with climate playing a larger role than the overkill hypothesis allowed.

The chapter does not pretend the question is settled. It engages the literature fairly, presents the strongest version of each hypothesis, and notes that the rewilding argument does not depend on which cause prevails. What was lost was lost. What restoration would mean is the same regardless of cause.

**Key sources:**
- Firestone, R. B., et al. (2007). Evidence for an extraterrestrial impact 12,900 years ago that contributed to the megafaunal extinctions and the Younger Dryas cooling. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 104(41), 16016–16021.
- Kennett, J. P., et al. (2015). Bayesian chronological analyses consistent with synchronous age of 12,835–12,735 Cal B.P. for Younger Dryas boundary on four continents. *PNAS*, 112(32), E4344–E4353.
- Wittke, J. H., et al. (2013). Evidence for deposition of 10 million tonnes of impact spherules across four continents 12,800 y ago. *PNAS*, 110(23), E2088–E2097.
- Martin, P. S. (2005). *Twilight of the Mammoths.* (For the overkill hypothesis as Martin himself articulated it.)
- Stuart, A. J. (2015). Late Quaternary megafaunal extinctions on the continents: a short review. *Geological Journal*, 50(3), 338–363. (For a synthesis of multiple-causation arguments.)
- Sandom, C., et al. (2014). Global late Quaternary megafauna extinctions linked to humans, not climate change. *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 281(1787), 20133254. (For the strongest current case for human causation, presented fairly.)

**Key moves:**
- Concrete inventory of what was lost
- Honest presentation of the overkill hypothesis as Martin's own position
- Honest presentation of the climate and impact alternatives
- Acknowledgment that the question is not settled
- The argument that rewilding does not depend on resolving the cause

**Compliance notes:**
- Hancock not cited (see exclusion list)
- The 2016 essay's "humans crossed the Bering Strait and caused this" framing is not reproduced; see authorial revisions log
- Martin is treated with full respect even where his overkill position is challenged; he was working with the evidence available to him and the field has moved on

## Part II: What We Eat Now

### Chapter 4: The Fourth Axis

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Introduce the survivability axis. Make the case that the dominant three-axis sustainability framework (economic, social, environmental) is incomplete.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter opens with a small concrete example: a food system that scores well on the three classic axes but collapses immediately when one input is removed. The reader feels the gap before the gap is named.

The chapter then walks through the history of the three-axis sustainability model and notes its assumption: stable conditions. The model was built during a period when "sustainable" meant "able to keep going under roughly current conditions." The 2020s have made that assumption unsupportable. Climate volatility, supply-chain shocks, energy-price political instability, and pandemic disruptions have made survivability legible to readers in a way it was not before.

The chapter introduces survivability as the missing axis: not "does this system function under current conditions" but "what does this system require to keep functioning, and what happens when those requirements are not met?" A food system that requires diesel, commodity feed, federal subsidies, or just-in-time supply chains to function is not sustainable in any meaningful sense; it is *piggybacking* on a substrate that may or may not persist.

The chapter does not yet apply the framework (that is Chapters 5 and 6). Its job is to make the case that the framework is needed.

**Key sources:**
- Taleb, N. N. (2012). *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder.* Random House. (Cited briefly as the antecedent thinking about systems under stress; not the central source.)
- Holling, C. S. (1973). Resilience and stability of ecological systems. *Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics*, 4, 1–23. (For the foundational ecological resilience literature.)
- Tendall, D. M., et al. (2015). Food system resilience: Defining the concept. *Global Food Security*, 6, 17–23.
- The 2016 essay (as the author's own development of this argument), with the framework presented descriptively rather than under a personal name.

**Key moves:**
- Concrete opening example
- History of the three-axis model and its assumptions
- The break in those assumptions during the 2020s
- Definition of the survivability axis
- Note that the framework is descriptive, not personally branded

**Compliance notes:**
- The framework is named descriptively. No "Pendergrass Score." See authorial revisions log.
- The framework is presented as a tool, not as a proprietary methodology.

### Chapter 5: Scoring the Industrial System

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Apply the four-axis framework to the two dominant industrial food systems: industrial feedlot animal production and industrial grain monoculture.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter applies the framework systematically. For each of the two industrial systems, the chapter scores against each of the four axes. The work is analytical, not polemical.

**Industrial feedlot animal production scores:**
- Economic: positive in the short term (cheap meat at the point of sale); negative when externalities are accounted (healthcare costs, pollution remediation, antibiotic resistance economic impact, rural community disinvestment).
- Social: net negative (worker conditions, community health impacts, displacement of family farmers).
- Environmental: net negative (waste lagoons, water pollution, antibiotic resistance, GHG emissions, no carbon sequestration).
- Survivability: very low. The system requires continuous commodity grain inputs, continuous federal subsidies for feed corn, continuous diesel for transportation, continuous antibiotics for animal health. Remove any one of these and the system fails.

**Industrial grain monoculture scores:**
- Economic: positive for centralized producers; negative for local economies and family farmers.
- Social: net negative for rural communities; positive for urban consumers in the form of cheap calories.
- Environmental: net negative (topsoil loss, biodiversity reduction, groundwater contamination, mycorrhizal damage from fungicides, run-off as the leading cause of non-point source water pollution in the US).
- Survivability: also very low. The system requires continuous chemical inputs, continuous fossil fuel for tractors and irrigation pumps, continuous federal subsidies, continuous favorable climate for predictable yields. Remove any one of these and the system fails.

The chapter notes that monocultures also have potential for improvement — better soil management practices, cover cropping, reduced tillage, integration with perennial systems can meaningfully improve scores on all four axes. The book does not present monocultures as categorically evil. It presents the current dominant industrial implementation of monoculture as scoring poorly on the framework.

**Key sources:**
- Pimentel, D., & Pimentel, M. (2008). *Food, Energy, and Society* (3rd ed.). CRC Press. (For the energy-input analysis.)
- Tilman, D., et al. (2002). Agricultural sustainability and intensive production practices. *Nature*, 418(6898), 671–677.
- Foley, J. A., et al. (2011). Solutions for a cultivated planet. *Nature*, 478(7369), 337–342.
- Pew Commission on Industrial Farm Animal Production. (2008). *Putting Meat on the Table: Industrial Farm Animal Production in America.* (For the externality accounting.)
- Updated externality figures from the 2012 manuscript, all re-verified against current sources.

**Key moves:**
- Systematic application of the framework
- Honest acknowledgment that monocultures have improvement potential
- Structural framing (these systems have these vulnerabilities) rather than categorical (these systems are evil)

**Compliance notes:**
- No personal attacks on industry figures. See exclusion list and anti-voice reference.
- The Vilsack/Monsanto material from the 2012 manuscript is not reproduced in its original form. If any of it appears, it is fact-checked, sourced to current peer-reviewed or major-press references, and presented analytically.

### Chapter 6: The Pasture, Honestly

**Length target:** 5,500–6,000 words
**Function:** Honest assessment of regenerative agriculture and holistic management. The chapter the Salatin material has been waiting for.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter opens with what regenerative agriculture and holistic management actually achieve. The chapter is generous and specific: the soil improvements at Polyface are real, the carbon sequestration potential of well-managed grasslands is real, the rural economic impact of family-scale pasture-based operations is real, the animal welfare improvements over feedlot systems are substantial.

The chapter scores holistic management against the four-axis framework. It scores well — significantly better than industrial systems on environmental and survivability axes, comparably on economic and social axes when viewed at the right scale.

The chapter then turns to the structural ceiling. Even the best version of regenerative agriculture, as currently practiced, is structurally tethered to the industrial system it opposes. The Polyface case is the worked example. The accounting is clean: at twelve thousand broilers per year, requiring three pounds of feed per pound of body weight, the operation requires approximately 144,000 pounds of feed annually. At the published feed-mix proportions, this means approximately 71,500 pounds of corn and 44,500 pounds of soy purchased from off-farm sources, almost certainly produced under industrial monoculture conditions.

This is presented as a structural finding, not as Salatin's personal failure. The chapter explicitly credits Salatin: he is not hiding this; the feed proportions are published in his own book; the practice is economically necessary given current commodity prices and the structural conditions of US agriculture. The point is not that Salatin is a fraud. The point is that even the best version of regenerative agriculture, embedded in the current industrial substrate, cannot fully escape that substrate.

The chapter walks through the McWilliams/Salatin exchange briefly. McWilliams's 2012 *New York Times* op-ed got most of its arguments wrong but got this one right. Salatin's response (the highland-lowland nutrient flow argument) was a sincere engagement that did not quite reach the question.

The chapter closes by setting up the question for Part III: if the best version of the currently-available alternative is structurally tethered to what it opposes, what would actually escape it?

**Key sources:**
- Salatin, J. (1999). *Pastured Poultry Profit$.* Polyface Inc. (Source for the feed proportions.)
- Pollan, M. (2006). *The Omnivore's Dilemma.* Penguin Press. (Source for the broiler counts and the broader Polyface description.)
- McWilliams, J. E. (2012, April 12). The myth of sustainable meat. *The New York Times.*
- Salatin's published response to McWilliams (Polyface Farm Facebook post and subsequent essays).
- Teague, W. R., et al. (2016). The role of ruminants in reducing agriculture's carbon footprint in North America. *Journal of Soil and Water Conservation*, 71(2), 156–164. (For the case that well-managed grazing systems can be carbon-positive.)
- Garnett, T., et al. (2017). *Grazed and Confused?* Food Climate Research Network, University of Oxford. (For the most rigorous critical assessment of grazing's climate claims.)

**Key moves:**
- Steelman first: what regenerative agriculture genuinely achieves
- The framework applied: it scores well, significantly better than industrial systems
- The structural ceiling: even the best version is tethered to industrial commodity inputs
- The Polyface accounting, presented analytically and without hostility
- The McWilliams/Salatin exchange as evidence of the limits of the conversation as currently framed
- Hand-off to Part III

**Compliance notes:**
- No hostility toward Salatin. See authorial revisions log and exclusion list.
- Steelman before critique. Salatin's strongest formulations and his real achievements are presented before the structural critique.
- The math is presented analytically. No "haha gotcha." No score-keeping flourishes.
- See anti-voice reference for the specific patterns to suppress in this chapter.

## Part III: What We Could Eat

### Chapter 7: The Wrong Proxy in the Right Place

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** The Savory food-truck scene. The argument that proxy selection matters and that the fossil record provides the guide for which proxies belong where. The bridge from regenerative agriculture into rewilding.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter opens with the Savory conference. The author is at Allan Savory's first major public conference. During the Q&A, she asks whether his holistic management framework has had trouble in any specific regions. He says yes, the Southwest. She waits. At lunch, she gets her plate from the food truck, takes a chair, and sits down beside him. She has Martin's *Twilight of the Mammoths* with her, marked up — she has read it three times by this point. She walks him through the relevant passages: the Southwest's Pleistocene fossil record shows equids and camelids, not bovids. The flora and the terrain co-evolved with those species. Cattle, as bovids, are the wrong proxy for the Southwest. The fossil record was telling Savory which proxy belonged in which place, and his framework's failures in the Southwest were the predictable result of using the wrong proxy.

The chapter is not about defeating Savory. The author respects him. The point is that the holistic management framework — which is a real intellectual achievement — gets significantly stronger when paired with proxy selection guided by paleontology. Savory's framework provides the management methodology. Martin's framework provides the species selection. Together they are stronger than either alone.

The chapter widens from the food-truck conversation into the broader principle. Across the continents, the question of which proxy belongs in which place is answerable from the fossil record. The Southwest wants equids and camelids. The shortgrass prairies want bison (which still exist) and possibly elephants as proxies for mammoths. The eastern hardwoods want different assemblages. The fossil record signals the answer; we have largely failed to consult it.

**Key sources:**
- Martin, P. S. (2005). *Twilight of the Mammoths.* (For the proxy-selection argument and the Southwest paleontology.)
- Savory, A., & Butterfield, J. (1999). *Holistic Management: A New Framework for Decision Making.* Island Press.
- Donlan, J., et al. (2005). Re-wilding North America. *Nature*, 436(7053), 913–914.
- Donlan, C. J., et al. (2006). Pleistocene rewilding: an optimistic agenda for twenty-first century conservation. *The American Naturalist*, 168(5), 660–681.

**Key scenes:**
- The Savory conference Q&A
- The food-truck conversation, told in detail
- The author's worn copy of Martin's book

**Key moves:**
- Personal narrative as evidence of intellectual seriousness
- Steelman of Savory's framework before the refinement
- The argument that the fossil record provides the proxy-selection guide
- The widening from the Southwest case to the broader principle
- Hand-off to the practical vision in Chapter 8

**Compliance notes:**
- Savory is presented with full respect throughout. The chapter is a refinement of his framework, not a critique.
- The food-truck scene is the author's actual experience, told as it happened.

### Chapter 8: Rewilding the Plate

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** The practical vision. What eating from rewilded landscapes actually looks like, today and at scale.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter answers the question Part III has built toward: what does it look like to eat from rewilded systems? The chapter is concrete and current, not aspirational.

It begins with what already exists. Feral hog harvesting in Texas, where invasive populations are causing ecological damage and providing a substantial protein source for those who hunt them. Bison restoration on tribal lands and at American Prairie. Wild-caught fish from intact fisheries (the Alaskan salmon fisheries managed under Hilborn-style stock assessment). Restored shellfish populations on the Atlantic and Pacific coasts. Managed elk and deer populations across much of the West.

The chapter then walks through what could exist with deliberate effort. Knepp Estate in West Sussex as a worked European example. The American Prairie Reserve in Montana as the most ambitious current US example. Smaller-scale rewilding efforts on private and public land.

The chapter is honest about scale. Rewilded food systems cannot, today, feed every person currently eating from industrial systems. The argument is not that we should abandon all current production tomorrow. The argument is that rewilded systems should be expanded substantially, that they score well across all four axes, that they represent a direction the food system should move toward, and that the question is one of trajectory, not of overnight replacement.

**Key sources:**
- Tree, I. (2018). *Wilding: The Return of Nature to a British Farm.* Picador. (Knepp.)
- Hilborn, R., & Walters, C. J. (2013). *Quantitative Fisheries Stock Assessment: Choice, Dynamics and Uncertainty.* Springer. (For the management framework.)
- Hilborn, R., et al. (2020). Effective fisheries management instrumental in improving fish stock status. *PNAS*, 117(4), 2218–2224.
- American Prairie Reserve project documentation.
- Texas Parks and Wildlife Department feral hog data.

**Key moves:**
- Concrete current examples, not just aspirations
- Honest scaling assessment
- The argument as trajectory rather than replacement
- The connection back to the four-axis framework

### Chapter 9: Proxies and Critics

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Honest engagement with the serious critics of Pleistocene rewilding. The chapter that earns the book the right to propose what it proposes.

**Argument arc:**
Pleistocene rewilding has serious critics. The chapter takes them seriously. Donlan's 2005 *Nature* paper proposing Pleistocene rewilding in North America generated significant scholarly response, much of it skeptical. The chapter walks through the major critiques fairly:

- Caro and others have argued that proxy species cause real ecological problems and that the assumption that closely-related species perform equivalent ecological functions is unsupported.
- Rubenstein and colleagues have argued that the cultural and political obstacles to introducing megafauna proxies are substantial enough to make the proposal practically unworkable.
- Other critics have argued that the Pleistocene baseline is itself a kind of conservation fantasy and that conservation should focus on protecting what currently exists rather than attempting to restore what was lost.

The chapter presents each critique in its strongest form and engages it honestly. Some critiques the author accepts (the cultural and political obstacles are real). Some critiques the author thinks are answerable (the proxy-equivalence question is empirical and can be tested at small scales). Some critiques the author thinks miss the larger point (focusing on what currently exists, when what currently exists is a depleted system, accepts the depletion as a permanent baseline).

The chapter closes with what the author asks: not certainty, but space for proof of concept. Rewilding hypotheses can be tested at small scales — not at the scale of restoring elephants to the Great Plains, but at the scale of restoring camels to a single ranch in Arizona, or burros to the Grand Canyon, or wolves to specific Northern Rocky Mountain ecosystems (where this has already been done with measurable results). The book asks for the experiments to be tried and assessed honestly.

**Key sources:**
- Caro, T. (2007). The Pleistocene re-wilding gambit. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*, 22(6), 281–283.
- Rubenstein, D. R., et al. (2006). Pleistocene Park: Does re-wilding North America represent sound conservation for the 21st century? *Biological Conservation*, 132(2), 232–238.
- Donlan, C. J., et al. (2006). Pleistocene rewilding: an optimistic agenda for twenty-first century conservation. *The American Naturalist*, 168(5), 660–681. (The fuller defense of Donlan's proposal.)
- Rubenstein, D. R., & Rubenstein, D. I. (2016). From Pleistocene to trophic rewilding: A wolf in sheep's clothing? *PNAS*, 113(1), E1.
- Svenning, J.-C., et al. (2016). Science for a wilder Anthropocene: Synthesis and future directions for trophic rewilding research. *PNAS*, 113(4), 898–906.
- Wolf reintroduction outcomes data from Yellowstone (multiple sources, verified).

**Key moves:**
- Each critique presented in its strongest form
- Honest acknowledgment of what the author accepts
- Argued response to what the author thinks is answerable
- The closing ask: room for proof of concept, not unconditional acceptance

**Compliance notes:**
- This chapter must not decry the critics. The author has explicitly stated this.
- The chapter asks for space to test ideas, not for capitulation to the proposal.
- All critics are named and their arguments fairly represented before any response is offered.

## Part IV: Doing

### Chapter 10: Indigenous Food Systems and the Water Column

**Length target:** 5,000–5,500 words
**Function:** Engage two parallel restoration frameworks the book has not yet addressed: Indigenous food management traditions, and rewilding in marine systems.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter opens with the recognition that Pleistocene rewilding is one frame but not the only one. Indigenous peoples in many regions managed their landscapes in ways that look like rewilding-with-people-as-keystone-species rather than rewilding-as-absence-of-people. The chapter engages the Traditional Ecological Knowledge literature seriously. Robin Wall Kimmerer, Lyla June Johnston, and others have articulated visions of human-inhabited restored landscapes that the Pleistocene framework should be in dialogue with rather than in competition with.

The chapter does not collapse the two frameworks together. They are different. The Pleistocene baseline asks "what existed before the late-Pleistocene losses?" The Indigenous management baseline asks "how did the people who lived here for the millennia between the Pleistocene and 1492 manage these landscapes?" Both questions are valid. Both produce different answers. The book's position is that both should inform restoration, with the appropriate framework chosen based on the specific landscape and the specific question being asked.

The second half of the chapter addresses water-column rewilding: kelp forest restoration, oyster reef restoration, intact pelagic fisheries, the difference between farmed salmon and wild Alaskan, the broader question of what marine ecosystems would look like restored to historical baselines. The marine domain has been almost entirely absent from the rewilding popular conversation. The chapter argues it should not be.

**Key sources:**
- Kimmerer, R. W. (2013). *Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants.* Milkweed Editions.
- Johnston, L. J. (multiple essays and talks on Indigenous ecological management; specific citations to be added).
- Anderson, M. K. (2005). *Tending the Wild: Native American Knowledge and the Management of California's Natural Resources.* University of California Press.

### Chapter 11: Bioregional and Urban

**Length target:** 4,500–5,000 words
**Function:** Apply the framework to where readers actually live. Address the urban reader directly.

**Argument arc:**
The framework adapts to bioregion. What sustainability looks like in arid Arizona is different from what it looks like in the Pacific Northwest is different from what it looks like in the New England hardwoods. The chapter walks through several worked bioregional examples, applying the framework to each.

The chapter then addresses urban readers directly. Most readers of this book will live in cities. The book's argument has implications for them even if they cannot raise their own food: they can support rewilded production systems through their purchasing, they can engage politically with conservation and food policy, they can advocate for the experiments the book has called for. The chapter takes urban readers seriously rather than implying that the only valid response is to move to the country.

**Key moves:**
- Concrete bioregional examples
- Direct address to urban readers
- The framework as adaptable rather than universal

### Chapter 12: Eating Toward the Vision

**Length target:** 4,500–5,000 words
**Function:** Practical guidance derived from the framework. What to do with the framework when shopping, when ordering, when cooking, when planning.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter does not provide a meal plan. It provides a framework for decisions. How to evaluate a label. What questions to ask a producer. How to think about cost honestly.

Cost is addressed directly. Sustainable, pasture-raised, wild, and rewilded foods cost more than industrial foods at the point of purchase. This is true. The chapter is honest about this and offers practical guidance for readers at different income levels: which choices have the highest sustainability return per dollar spent, which compromises are reasonable, what the price difference actually represents in terms of externalized costs.

The chapter does not lecture readers about priorities. The 2012 manuscript's "you could afford it if you had your priorities right" register is explicitly excluded.

**Key moves:**
- Framework for decisions, not prescriptions
- Honest cost discussion
- Practical guidance at different income levels
- Respect for readers' actual constraints

**Compliance notes:**
- See authorial revisions log on the cost-and-priorities tone
- See anti-voice reference on reader-hectoring patterns

### Chapter 13: What Must Change

**Length target:** 4,500–5,000 words
**Function:** Policy, culture, and producer economics. How the vision scales beyond individual readers.

**Argument arc:**
The chapter addresses the limits of consumer choice. Even widespread adoption of the book's framework by individual readers will not, by itself, produce the systemic change the framework calls for. The chapter walks through what would have to change at policy and structural levels: subsidy reform, conservation policy, land-use regulation, support for proxy-species experiments, fisheries management.

The chapter is honest that these changes are politically difficult. It does not pretend there is a clear path to enacting them. It identifies what would have to be true for them to become possible.

The chapter closes by handing off to the epilogue. The book has made its argument. What remains is whether the experiments will be tried.

**Key moves:**
- Acknowledgment of the limits of consumer choice
- Concrete policy directions
- Honest assessment of political difficulty
- Hand-off to the epilogue

## Drafting Order

Recommended order, which is not the order chapters appear in the book:

1. **Prologue** — Voice and emotional spine test. If this works, the book is alive.
2. **Chapter 2: The Columbian Curtain** — Central conceptual move. If this works, the argument carries.
3. **Chapter 6: The Pasture, Honestly** — Hardest craft challenge (writing critically about a living, named figure with intellectual fairness). If this works, the rest of the book is structural.
4. **Chapter 7: The Wrong Proxy in the Right Place** — The Savory food-truck scene. The book's most personal chapter.
5. **Chapter 1: The Meat Counter** — Personal narrative chapter, written after the framework chapters so the narrative can foreshadow the framework cleanly.
6. **Chapters 3, 4, 5** — Foundation material for Parts I and II.
7. **Chapters 8, 9** — Rewilding vision and critic engagement.
8. **Chapters 10, 11, 12, 13** — Application and closing.
9. **Epilogue** — Written last, when the full weight of the book is available to close on.

This order produces three concrete checkpoints (prologue, Chapter 2, Chapter 6) at which the project can be evaluated and corrected before deep investment in chapters that depend on them.
