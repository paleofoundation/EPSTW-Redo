## 1. The camel you did not know you had

For most of the last three million years, the American West was camel country.

*Camelops hesternus* — "yesterday's camel" — was the dominant North American camelid of the late Pleistocene. It stood roughly 2.1 metres at the shoulder, somewhat larger than a modern dromedary, and carried an estimated body mass of around 800 kilograms.[^1] Its fossil record stretches from Beringia in the Yukon[^2] through the Great Basin, the Columbia Plateau, the southern Rockies, the southwestern deserts, the southern Plains, and the California coast, with La Brea (Rancho La Brea) yielding one of the densest single-locality assemblages on the continent.[^3]

Most modern readers carry a picture of camels as Old World animals — dromedaries and Bactrians. That picture is backwards. Camelidae evolved on the North American continent across most of the Cenozoic.[^4] The ancestors of modern Old World camels crossed into Eurasia via Beringia; the ancestors of South American llamas and vicuñas crossed south into what is now Peru and the Altiplano. *Camelops* stayed. It was a North American animal for the entire history of its genus.

Genomic work published in 2015 revised the family tree further: *Camelops* is more closely related to living Old World camels (*Camelini*) than to any of the living New World llamas (*Lamini*), reversing a placement that had persisted in the morphological literature for decades.[^5] The camels that turn up on the Silk Road are descendants of North American emigrants. *Camelops* is their closer kin than the llama is.

Then, across the last several centuries of the Pleistocene, *Camelops* disappeared. The most robust synthesis of terminal-Pleistocene last-appearance dates places the final *Camelops* specimens at or before approximately 13,000 calibrated years before present, consistent with the broader North American megafaunal collapse.[^6]

## 2. What *Camelops* ate

Direct dietary evidence for *Camelops* is unusually good for an extinct megaherbivore. Dung deposits recovered from dry caves — Gypsum Cave in Nevada and several southwestern sites — preserved the plant material of *Camelops* last meals and were subjected to systematic macrobotanical analysis by Akersten, Foppe, and Jefferson in 1988. The dung contained a mix of grasses, sedges, and woody browse, including yucca, desert shrubs, and members of the pea family.[^7]

Subsequent dental microwear and mesowear studies support the same conclusion from a different angle: *Camelops* tooth wear patterns are consistent with a mixed feeder — part grazer, part browser — shifting toward more browsing in more arid and more shrub-dominated systems.[^8]

The practical implication for the landscape is the thing the page exists to make visible. *Camelops* was not a pure grazer competing with the bison and mammoths for grass. It was a mixed feeder that could crop mesquite, juniper, and shrub understory and move back and forth across grassland, shrubland, and the ecotones between. A vegetation community assembled around a mixed feeder of that size behaves differently, across decades, from one assembled without.

## 3. The assemblage

*Camelops* did not stand alone. The late Pleistocene American West was arranged around a set of large-bodied vertebrates that had been co-occurring, in overlapping and evolving assemblages, for hundreds of thousands of years.[^9]

The partial list of what shared the continent with *Camelops* at 15,000 BP:

Mammoths: *Mammuthus columbi* across the southern continent, *Mammuthus primigenius* in the north. Mastodons: *Mammut americanum*, principally in the forested east and the Pacific Northwest. Horses: *Equus occidentalis* and several other *Equus* species ranging the basin and plains. Another camelid: *Hemiauchenia macrocephala*, a larger-bodied llamine — a llama relative, not a close cousin of *Camelops*.[^10] Ground sloths: *Nothrotheriops shastensis* (Shasta ground sloth), *Paramylodon harlani* (Harlan's ground sloth), *Megalonyx jeffersonii* (Jefferson's ground sloth). Bison: *Bison latifrons* early in the interval, *Bison antiquus* later. And the predator guild: *Smilodon fatalis*, *Panthera atrox* (the American lion), *Miracinonyx trumani* (the American cheetah), *Arctodus simus* (the giant short-faced bear), *Canis dirus* (the dire wolf).

Almost all of these animals are gone. The bison survived, in a close-relative form. The horse lineage was extirpated on the continent and returned only with the Spanish in the 1490s. Everything else on the list vanished.

## 4. The fruits that are still waiting

If you grew up in the American Midwest or mid-South, you have met an osage orange tree.

*Maclura pomifera* — osage orange, bois d'arc, hedge apple — is common along old fence rows and in hedgerow plantings across the central United States. It was planted by the hundreds of thousands during the nineteenth century as a thorny living fence before barbed wire made it obsolete.[^11] Its fruit is grapefruit-sized, grainy, faintly citrus-scented, bleeding white latex when cut. To any extant animal on the continent, that fruit is essentially inedible. Horses will nose one and walk on. Cattle refuse them. The fruits fall, rot in place, and stain the ground beneath the tree.

In 1982, Daniel Janzen and Paul Martin published a paper in *Science* called "Neotropical Anachronisms: The Fruits the Gomphotheres Ate." They argued that a long list of New World plants produce fruits whose size, chemistry, and seed architecture are coherent only as signals to large-bodied mammalian dispersers that no longer exist — gomphotheres and other proboscideans, ground sloths, large camelids, giant tortoises.[^12] Janzen and Martin named the condition ecological anachronism: a plant whose dispersal partner is out of its time.

Within the American West and the transitional eastern edge of the *Camelops* range, the candidates are familiar. Osage orange (*Maclura pomifera*). Honey locust (*Gleditsia triacanthos*), whose long fibrous pods are sugary, indehiscent, and currently dispersed almost nowhere.[^13] Kentucky coffeetree (*Gymnocladus dioicus*), whose heavy toxic pods have been reduced to a near-relict distribution in modern forests.[^14] At the Pacific edge of the range, pawpaw (*Asimina triloba*), persimmon (*Diospyros virginiana*), and several *Prosopis* and *Acacia* species with comparable structural signatures.

No single one of these trees proves the anachronism hypothesis on its own. Some of their fruits are partially vectored by surviving dispersers — white-tailed deer, raccoons, extant livestock, humans. The phylogenetic distance from the originally-hypothesized dispersers to modern ones is not always large enough to make the system fully dysfunctional. The argument is statistical: across the suite of New World plants that carry this set of traits, the ecological fit is with dispersers that no longer exist.

The question the page asks is the one Janzen and Martin asked, restated for a visitor standing in front of a fallen osage orange on a Kansas fencerow: *what is this fruit for?*

## 5. When near time ended

"Near time" is the term the paleontologist Ross MacPhee and his colleagues introduced in 1999 to frame the last roughly 50,000 years of extinctions as a coherent scientific unit.[^15] Within that window, North America lost more than forty genera of large-bodied mammals.[^16] The *Camelops* lineage was one of them.

The cause of the near-time extinctions has been argued, hard, for more than half a century. Three major hypotheses remain in play.

**Human overkill.** Proposed by Paul Martin in the 1960s and developed across his career, the overkill hypothesis holds that the arrival of skilled big-game hunters in North America at or near the beginning of the Clovis period (ca. 13,000 BP) produced the continental collapse via direct and sustained predation pressure on animals that had not co-evolved with human hunters.[^17]

**Climate.** Rapid climate oscillation during the terminal Pleistocene — most sharply at the onset of the Younger Dryas, around 12,900 BP — imposed substantial ecological stress on assemblages already at the edge of viability.[^18]

**Cosmic impact.** Firestone and colleagues (2007) and a subsequent body of work under the banner of the Younger Dryas Impact Hypothesis propose that a fragmented impactor event at approximately 12,800 BP contributed to the Younger Dryas cooling and accompanying ecosystem collapse.[^19] The hypothesis remains actively debated in the peer-reviewed literature.[^20]

The current scientific posture, reflected in the synthesis reviews, is that the extinctions were most probably multifactorial — some mixture of climate, human pressure, and habitat change, with the weight of each factor varying by species and by region.[^21] David Meltzer's 2020 review in PNAS offers the most accessible recent synthesis of the critical case against a pure overkill account.[^22]

The rewilding argument — which the book you are reading a companion site to is substantially about — does not depend on which cause prevails. What was lost was lost. The question of whether it can be partly restored is separate from the question of what drove it out.

The land *Camelops* walked is still there. Most of it is under a use pattern that has no memory of *Camelops* in it.

In its former core range, the American West today is dominated by rangeland cattle operations, federal grazing leases, irrigated agriculture, and large blocks of protected federal land under the Bureau of Land Management, the Forest Service, and the National Park Service. Feral *Equus caballus* populations — the returned horse, the book's Chapter 2 case — persist in several western ranges, managed in practice by BLM round-ups.[^23] Pronghorn (*Antilocapra americana*) remain on the plains and basins, still the fastest land mammal in the hemisphere. Bison remain in fragments: Yellowstone, the National Bison Range, a few state and tribal herds, and restoration projects that have been growing steadily across the last three decades.[^24]

Osage orange, as noted, is everywhere and orphaned. Honey locust, cultivated and wild, is everywhere and orphaned. Kentucky coffeetree has contracted to a near-relict distribution, planted in cities for its ornamental qualities, barely dispersing in any wild system.[^25]

The continent is, in the terms this site uses, operating at half volume.

## 7. What could stand here again

Pleistocene rewilding as a formal scientific proposal dates to a pair of papers by C. Josh Donlan and colleagues published in *Nature* (2005) and *The American Naturalist* (2006).[^26] The proposal, simplified: where close living analogues of extinct megafauna exist — and where ecological, regulatory, and social conditions make it possible — introduce those analogues as proxies for the missing species, at first in small and controlled settings, to test whether they can restore ecological function.

Donlan and colleagues named candidate proxies explicitly. Bactrian and dromedary camels were among them, as proxies for *Camelops* and *Hemiauchenia*. Asian and African elephants were named as proxies for mammoths and mastodons. Bolson tortoises (*Gopherus flavomarginatus*) were named as a proxy for the extinct *Hesperotestudo* giant tortoises of the southwestern United States. The proposal was controversial when published, as its authors understood it would be, and it remains so. Chapter 9 of the book engages the critics in detail and fairly.[^27]

No current project in North America has introduced *Camelops* proxies at ecological scale. Several adjacent projects are doing work that would frame the possibility.

**American Prairie Reserve** (Montana) is assembling a large contiguous prairie landscape around a restoring bison population, with the stated long-term aim of recreating a functionally complete grassland ecosystem.[^28] The bison work is not proxy work — bison are themselves survivors — but the land base and governance model is the kind of scaffolding under which proxy experiments could be run, if political and regulatory conditions allowed.

**The Bolson tortoise restoration** at Ted Turner's Armendaris Ranch in New Mexico is the best existing North American example of a deliberate Pleistocene-proxy program at a working scale.[^29] The tortoises were first reintroduced in 2006 and the herd has reproduced and persisted.

**Pleistocene Park** (Chersky, Siberia), under the direction of Sergey and Nikita Zimov, is not a North American project, but it is the fullest operational test of the underlying hypothesis — that reassembling a functional megaherbivore community changes the vegetation community and the soil carbon balance in measurable ways.[^30] Their published data on soil temperature, vegetation change, and methane flux over two decades is the closest thing the field has to an experimental answer.

No one has tried, at scale, what the Prologue names as its wish. The permits would be difficult. The ecological risk assessments would need to be rigorous. The neighbors would have to be persuaded. Some of the most serious critics of Pleistocene rewilding — Tim Caro, Dustin Rubenstein, and others — have argued that the social and political frictions alone are enough to keep the proposal from ever being realized at meaningful scale.[^31] The critics are not dismissed here. Chapter 9 of the book engages them.

What the page asks, and what the book asks, is narrower. Not *camels restored to the continent.* Room for the experiment. An osage orange orchard, a hundred camels, a study site, a research protocol, a multi-year data series on what happens when the fruit has a mouth again.

## Methods and data

**Fossil occurrences.** *Camelops* locality data for the hero map and the address-lookup component are pulled at query time from the Paleobiology Database (PBDB) via its REST API, filtered to `taxon_name=Camelops, interval=Late Pleistocene`. Every occurrence record carries its PBDB occurrence ID, its collection reference, and, where present, a primary-source citation. Each visible dot on the map has a link-through to its PBDB record.

**Last-appearance dates.** The extinction timeline uses the date compilation published in Faith & Surovell (2009) for the continental synthesis, with individual-species updates as more recent dating work (e.g., specific locality redatings in *Quaternary Science Reviews* and *Quaternary Research*) becomes available. The date of last data refresh is shown at the foot of the component.

**Flora distributions.** Modern distributions of candidate orphan-syndrome plants (osage orange, honey locust, Kentucky coffeetree, pawpaw, persimmon) are pulled from USDA PLANTS (public domain) at the county level and supplemented with GBIF and iNaturalist research-grade records. Data is refreshed quarterly.

**Human-arrival chronology.** Human-arrival overlays use the peer-reviewed synthesis in the p3k14c radiocarbon compilation[^32] and the pre-Clovis literature as engaged in Chapter 3 of the book. The overlay is shown as a range, not a date, because the arrival date is genuinely uncertain and represents a probability distribution rather than a discrete event.

**Paleoclimate overlay.** Temperature reconstruction uses the GISP2 ice-core record. Sea-level reconstruction for paleogeography uses the synthesis in Lambeck et al. (2014).[^33]

**A note on bias.** Fossil localities reflect where paleontologists have dug, not where *Camelops* lived. Absence of dots on the hero map should not be read as absence of the species. The map is a map of our evidence, not of the Pleistocene.

**Licensing.** All data sources used on this page are licensed for public, attributed use. PBDB data is CC-BY. Neotoma data is CC-BY-4.0. GBIF data licensing is filtered per dataset at query time; only CC0 and CC-BY records are displayed. USDA PLANTS data is public domain. iNaturalist data honors the per-observation license set by the observer. A full license register is maintained at `/near-time/about/licenses`.

# Template notes (internal; not published)

Purpose: the other nine vignettes inherit the shape established here. Use this section as a specification.

## Structural shape

Every vignette consists of nine elements in this order:

1. **Hero map** (range + occurrence visualization) with page title and subtitle.
2. **Epigraph** — a single pulled sentence from the book, attributed to the chapter it came from, with a link to the excerpt. If the vignette has no direct book line, the epigraph slot takes a sentence of site-voice copy instead.
3. **Section 1: The species, introduced.** Who the animal was. Phylogeny. Morphology. Body size. Diet. Range. This is the grounding panel.
4. **Section 2: Diet and ecological role.** What the animal ate, how it moved, what its functional role in the ecosystem was. The panel that justifies everything that follows.
5. **Section 3: The assemblage.** Who else stood there. The other large-bodied vertebrates that co-occurred with the focal species.
6. **Section 4: The plants that are still waiting.** Janzen–Martin or the regional equivalent. Specific named plants with specific orphan-syndrome traits.
7. **Section 5: When it ended.** Terminal-extinction dates, causal hypotheses, honest uncertainty, the site's standing position on multifactorial causation.
8. **Section 6: What stands here now.** Current land use and vegetation. The interactive address-lookup component.
9. **Section 7: What could stand here again.** Proxy options, current rewilding projects in the region, honest statement of what has and has not been tried. Link to relevant *One Hundred Camels* project pages.
10. **Section 8: Read the chapter.** Link to the anchor book chapter's excerpt.
11. **Methods and data.** Source datasets, refresh cadence, bias statement, licensing.
12. **References.** Full scientific citations with DOIs, every inline reference numbered in the Prologue-style footnote format.

The address-lookup component in Section 6 and the project-cards component in Section 7 are the two interactive commitments that every vignette makes. Everything else is copy plus light visualization.

## Voice parameters

- **Register.** Adjacent to the book's higher register (framework-chapter voice, Chapters 4, 5, 9, 10, 12, 13). Not the book's lower register — the personal-scene voice belongs to Karen and stays with the book. The vignette page speaks with site authority, not with the author's first-person.
- **Pronouns.** Third-person default. Occasional second-person for direct reader address ("If you grew up in the American Midwest..."). No first-person singular. First-person plural ("we") only when naming the site's editorial position explicitly, and used sparingly.
- **Sentence cadence.** Short declarative openings to panels. Longer analytical sentences in the body. The pattern is Karen's — grounded image, then the expansion, then the analytical frame.
- **Citation density.** Every paleontological, ecological, archaeological, or climatological claim carries a footnote. Any claim without a defensible footnote gets cut or rewritten as framing. The target is: an academic reader should be able to follow any factual claim to a peer-reviewed source within one click.
- **Disputed claims.** Where the science is actively contested (Younger Dryas impact hypothesis, the tightness of human overkill causation, specific plant–disperser pairings), the contest is named plainly and the strongest cases on both sides are cited.
- **Exclusions.** The constraints in `feedback_sustainability_diet_hard_constraints.md` apply: no Graham Hancock, no personal-brand framings, no Paleo Approved promotion, no excluded-names list members. Steelman critics before engaging them.

## Figure/component inventory (reusable across vignettes)

- **Hero map** — PBDB occurrence query, Natural Earth basemap, taxon-level filter. Reused on every vignette.
- **Phylogeny inset** — small cladogram, used when the phylogenetic placement is itself a teaching moment.
- **Body-size comparison** — silhouettes at scale against a human reference. Reused across species pages too.
- **Assemblage chart** — horizontal species-over-time chart. One per vignette, with the focal species highlighted.
- **Plant–disperser pairing diagram** — two-column connected-pairs visualization. One per vignette where the regional anachronism hypothesis has at least two plant candidates with citation support.
- **Extinction timeline** — last-appearance dates plus human-arrival band plus climate proxy. One per vignette.
- **Address lookup** — zip-code or address input, returns nearest-fossil result. Universal component, the same instance, specialized per vignette by the taxon filter.
- **Project cards** — filterable rewilding-project list, the *One Hundred Camels* index component, specialized per vignette by geography.
- **Excerpt card** — link-out to the book excerpt, with first two paragraphs and a read-more CTA.

## What this vignette does not do

It does not advocate for a specific restoration program. It does not editorialize against the book's critics. It does not reproduce the Prologue's first-person voice. It does not make any dietary, nutritional, or Paleo-framework claim. It does not name living figures critically — rewilding's critics are engaged on their science, not their persons. It does not promote the Paleo Foundation or any certification program.
