## FIELD NOTE — "The Twilight Origin Story"

**Placement:** Note to Reader, after the paragraph that ends "I have tried to show my work."

I finished the first version of this book in 2012. It was a Paleo manuscript for Victory Belt, I turned it in, and a week or two later I picked up Paul Martin's *Twilight of the Mammoths* for the first time.

I read it in two days. I knew before I was halfway through that I had not written the book I wanted to write. The book I had turned in was answering a smaller question than the one Martin was asking. It was built on a baseline I had not realized was a choice: 1492, or something like it.

A year after I finished the manuscript, I had already begun to disagree with several of its premises. Two years later I had reversed position on others. The Paleo book did not publish. This one did, fourteen years later.

I am not embarrassed by the first version. The person who wrote it was doing her honest best with the evidence she had read. But one of the best things that ever happened to me was that the book did not go to press in 2012. I would have regretted it for the rest of my life. I have changed my mind too many times since then to have wanted that earlier version fixed in print. It took fourteen years, four framework revisions, and a considerable amount of argument with people I love before the right book was the one I could actually write.

This sidebar is here so you know, early on, that revision is not something that happened to this book. It is what the book is.

## TRY THIS — "The Osage Orange"

**Placement:** Prologue, adjacent to the osage orange passage.

Next time you see an osage orange fruit on the ground, pick it up. Feel how heavy it is. Look at it for tool marks. There will not be any. No mouse has chewed it. No deer has browsed it. No squirrel has cracked a single seed out. The fruit is, for the present fauna, inert.

Now picture the mouth the fruit was made for. A gomphothere stood roughly nine feet at the shoulder and weighed several tons. Its molars were the size of bricks. A fruit that looks large to you would have looked like a snack to it.

If you want a small exercise: count the osage oranges under one tree in late autumn. Note how many show any sign of being opened, by any animal, over the course of the winter. Most years, on most trees, the answer is zero. The tree produced a hundred-pound crop for a mouth that is not coming.

The data are not earth-shaking. But they are yours, and they are specific to a tree you can walk to.

## REVERSAL — "The Strongest Case for the Industrial System"

**Placement:** Chapter 5, early, before the scoring begins.

The strongest version of the case for the industrial system goes like this.

Nine billion people, by mid-century. A carrying capacity question that preindustrial agriculture has never met. Norman Borlaug's Green Revolution averted famine at a scale no other twentieth-century intervention can claim. Industrial grain yields per acre are several times what the same land produced a century ago. The system, whatever its externalities, has fed more people per unit of land and per unit of labor than any system in human history. To propose replacing it with anything less productive is to propose, by arithmetic, that some fraction of those nine billion does not eat.

This is not a straw man. It is the argument the FAO has made in its food-security publications. It is the argument made in peer-reviewed journals by economists at land-grant universities whose work I respect. It is the argument I had to take seriously before I could write a book that proposes the system has to change.

My answer to it is in the chapters that follow. But the argument deserves to be heard at full strength before the rebuttal, because the consequence of being wrong in the other direction is famine, and that is not a small matter.

## FIELD NOTE — "The Contract"

**Placement:** Chapter 7, adjacent to the paragraph ending "The vegetation is still shaped by the animals. The animals are no longer there."

For the record, when I eat fruits, I eat the seeds. I have ever since it occurred to me that there was a contract that we made with these fruits.

The contract is simple. The plant grows a sweet, fragrant, nutritious package around a small hard seed. Something eats the package, carries the seed some distance from the parent tree, and deposits it in its own fertilizer. The plant invests in the package because the seed gets dispersal and a fertilized start. The animal invests in finding the fruit because the package is calorie-dense, and its appearance is, literally, an advertisement.

Most of the animals the fruit trees of this continent made their contract with are gone. Mastodons, gomphotheres, ground sloths. The mouths that osage orange and honey locust and pawpaw grew their fruit for are not coming. The tree drops its crop every autumn and most of what is down there will rot where it fell.

I am not a mastodon. But I can keep one small piece of the contract. I eat the seeds when I eat the fruit. Dispersal in fertilizer is not guaranteed the way it once was. But there are few things in this world that actually want to be eaten. Fruits want to be eaten. Milk wants to be drunk. Grass wants to be grazed down. It seems to me that if a thing is asking, the polite response is to answer.

## CASE — "When the Test Stops Being a Thought Experiment"

**Placement:** Chapter 13 (What Must Change), at the section break after the cultural-shift / Columbian Curtain paragraph and before the "Minimum Viable Flip" Field Note. Drafted 2026-05-11.

This chapter has been arguing that the survivability axis matters because the conditions under which our food systems were optimized may not hold. The argument is not abstract.

The most-discussed grain-export chokepoint in the world as I am writing this is the Odesa region of the Black Sea, through which Ukraine has historically shipped roughly four-fifths of its grain and oilseed output. The export corridor has been under episodic military pressure since 2022, has operated at reduced capacity for stretches of every year since, and remains among the most concentrated routes by which carbohydrate calories reach the food-insecure populations of Africa and the Middle East. When that route falters, the World Food Programme reports an immediate caloric cascade thousands of miles away. The chokepoint is not theoretical.

The most-discussed nitrogen-fertilizer chokepoint is the Strait of Hormuz, through which a substantial fraction of the world's seaborne natural-gas trade transits, and which therefore sets the marginal global price of the natural gas that feeds the Haber-Bosch process. Roughly half of the nitrogen currently in living human bodies traces back to that industrial synthesis. Vaclav Smil has been making the point in print for thirty years: four billion people are alive who would not be alive without it. The dependency is not a hypothetical future risk. The dependency is the current architecture, and the pricing of its key input cycles through a strait that has been militarily contested repeatedly in this century.

These are not the only chokepoints. Phosphate runs through North Africa. Potash runs through Belarus and Russia. Seed stock concentrates in a handful of multinational firms. The cold chain runs on refrigerants whose supply is its own chokepoint. Each of these is a single point of failure for a different essential input to industrial food production — and each of them compounds with the climate-driven failure modes (drought, heatwave, flood) that act on the same production base from a different angle. The list is not new and it is not partisan. It is the structural reality of a global food system that has been optimized for cheap energy and stable trade lanes for the better part of a century.

The four-axis framework was built to make exactly this kind of dependency visible. An industrial food system that scores well on economic viability, on social fit, and even on present-day environmental footprint can score catastrophically on survivability if its essential inputs cycle through a few politically contested geographies. The reader does not have to take any particular position on the geopolitics to take the survivability score seriously. The dependency exists regardless of who holds office anywhere.

This is no longer a question only the Pentagon planners are asking. Singapore's *30 by 30* program is an explicit state-level commitment to producing thirty percent of the country's nutritional needs domestically by the decade's end — built around exactly the survivability axis this chapter has been describing. Israel's national food-security planning treats the dependency profile as an input on equal footing with conventional defense planning. China's strategic grain reserves, held at a scale that has been publicly estimated at roughly half of the world's wheat stockpiles, are the same calculation in a different vocabulary. The Pentagon, the war colleges, and the food-systems-resilience desks of the major think tanks are running the same math. The countries doing this work are not all on the same political team, and the math does not care which team they are on.

The United States enters this conversation carrying a structural disadvantage of its own, and a specific opportunity. The disadvantage is that the continent it operates on is, by the ecological measure that matters most for the fourth axis, already depleted — running its food systems on land that lost most of its large-bodied animals twelve and a half thousand years ago and has been operating below ecological capacity ever since. The Americas paid that survivability cost more acutely and on a more recent timescale than most other inhabited continents, and the cost is still on the books. The opportunity is that the disadvantage is one we can get ahead of. The framework this book has built points at what getting ahead of it would actually look like. The chapter that ends this book makes the case.

The Walking Dead question that opened this work was a thought experiment when I asked it fifteen years ago. It is no longer.

## CASE — "Two Future-Food Categories Through the Four-Axis Test"

**Placement:** Chapter 11 (Bioregional and Urban), at the section break after the New England paragraphs that close on "the formal economy has begun to measure" and before "The Urban Reader" Heading2.

The framework has to hold up against food systems we are still building, not just food systems we have inherited. Two of the most-discussed categories in 2026 are cultivated meat (sometimes called cellular agriculture or lab-grown meat) and closed-loop aquaponics. Below is how each scores when the framework is applied at the level of present-day implementation, with the caveat that both categories are evolving and each score moves with the technology.

*Cultivated meat.* Economic viability is the axis that has, as of this writing, been the hardest. Production costs have fallen by orders of magnitude since the 2013 Mosa Meat demonstration but remain well above grocery-shelf parity for unsubsidized retail. Social fit is mixed: regulatory approval has come in Singapore, the United States, and Israel, but consumer acceptance varies regionally and the supply chain (bioreactors, growth media, regulatory infrastructure) does not employ the agricultural workforce the displaced production did. Environmental fit depends entirely on where the energy comes from; with low-carbon electricity, life-cycle greenhouse-gas emissions can be substantially below conventional beef, but with grid-average electricity, the advantage shrinks. Survivability is where the framework gets least friendly: the system depends on continuous high-purity inputs (growth media, sterile bioreactor environments, reliable cold chain) that are themselves outputs of the industrial system. A cultivated-meat plant cannot operate during a sustained electricity or supply-chain disruption. The system is, on the survivability axis, more brittle than conventional pasture and roughly comparable to industrial broilers.

*Closed-loop aquaponics.* Economic viability depends heavily on scale and on energy cost; small operations carry high per-unit cost but high product premium. Social fit is high in urban and peri-urban contexts where the production is visible and integrates with restaurant or institutional buyers. There is a closed-loop aquaponics system running underneath a restaurant in downtown Kansas City as I write this; the produce reaches the kitchen of the restaurant above without leaving the building. Environmental fit is generally favorable: water reuse approaches ninety percent and inputs do not cycle through external soils. Survivability is the interesting axis. The system carries some of the same input-fragility as cultivated meat — pumps, filters, water heaters, climate control all depend on continuous power — but the system's smaller scale and shorter supply chain mean a more graceful failure mode. A neighborhood aquaponics system loses its fish before its bok choy. A cultivated-meat plant loses everything when the bioreactor's temperature drifts.

The four-axis test does not declare either system a failure or a success. What it does is force the conversation to happen on more than one axis. Cultivated meat may pass the environmental axis spectacularly while failing the survivability axis just as spectacularly. Aquaponics may pass survivability at small scale while losing economic viability at the scales it would need to feed a city. Both observations are true. Neither is a takedown. The framework's job is to surface the trade-offs, not to deliver a verdict.

## REVISION — "On Discovering Resilience Thinking"

**Placement:** Chapter 4, after the framework is fully laid out and before the closing line "The framework is a lens. The next chapters are what the lens is pointed at."

Sometime after I had written the four-axis framework as it appears in this chapter, a reader pointed me at C. S. Holling's 1973 paper on ecosystem resilience, then at Walker and Salt's *Resilience Thinking* (2006), then at the broader literature developed by Folke, Berkes, Ostrom, and others over fifty years. I had not arrived at the framework through that field. I arrived at it through certification work, and through asking, repeatedly, which of the food systems I had been writing standards for would still be food systems the day after their inputs got harder to count on.

It would have been a better book had I known about the resilience-thinking field when I started. I did not. The framework here was developed independently, and that fact does not shield me from the criticism that I am reinventing wheels with my own labels.

What I will say is this. When independent thinkers, working in different fields, with different prompts, arrive at the same structural observation — that systems described well at one moment in time can be described badly across changes in their conditions — the convergence itself is evidence. Evidence that the observation is not a quirk of one camp's framing. Evidence that something is missing in the framework most of us have been using. The reader should pause at the convergence.

I owe the resilience-thinking field a careful read. I will not pretend to have done that read in time for this book. Where my framework and theirs say the same thing, I want to be clear that they were there first. Where they say different things, I want to be clear about what the difference is, and I do not yet know enough to do that work in this volume. It is the next thing I owe, and I will write it.

## REVISION — "The Overkill Hypothesis"

**Placement:** Chapter 3, in the discussion of what caused the Late Pleistocene extinctions.

2011: I was confident the Late Pleistocene extinctions were caused by humans arriving across the Bering land bridge. The overkill hypothesis, as laid out in Martin's earlier work, was the version of the story I believed. Human arrival, large mammals gone within a few thousand years, clean narrative, rewilding justified as a kind of debt repayment.

2017: I began reading the paleoclimate work on the Younger Dryas and the challenges to the timing of human arrival. The fossil record did not line up as cleanly with the human signal as the overkill story required. At least some of the extinctions ran ahead of the first firm dates for human presence.

2024: I am no longer confident in overkill as the single cause. I think humans were involved. I think climate was involved. I think the mixture varies by species and by region, and I am not sure it is knowable from the evidence we currently have.

This matters for the book only in that the case for rewilding used to depend, in my thinking, on human responsibility. It no longer does. The case now rests on ecological function. The landscape is operating below capacity because animals that shaped it are missing. Why they are missing is a separate question, and the rewilding case holds either way.

I changed my mind. Because I changed my mind, I considered other ideas and arguments against its premise more seriously. It sharpened the thinking, and narrowed the arguments. The book is better for it.

## FIELD NOTE — "What Actually Happened at the Whole Foods"

**Placement:** Chapter 1, after the paragraph ending "the trust was unearned until I asked one specific question on one specific afternoon." (i.e., immediately before the "What 'Grass-Fed' Meant" section heading).

The version of the scene above is the cleaned one. The actual scene was louder. This is the dirty version.

I had been sick for over a year. By the end of 2009, at twenty-five, I had been diagnosed with insulin-resistant PCOS, PMDD, schizophrenia, grade-three anemia, cirrhosis, a kidney mass, GERD, peripheral neuropathy, optic neuritis, bacterial vaginosis, menopause, ascites with chronic edema, endometriosis, fibromyalgia, and finally, celiac disease. I had gained fifty pounds in three months. The anemia was severe enough that the physician treating me had already said I needed someone else to drive me around. Despite following the red-meat diet he suggested, I was still deteriorating.

That day at Whole Foods, I had already been crying. I had been grocery shopping for over half an hour, and the only thing I had added to the basket was apples. They were the only thing I could eat at the time without adverse effects, and I avoided grain-finishing because it had previously caused digestive issues. That is why I was so intent on getting grass-finished beef. I needed it. This was a dire situation. I was emotionally exhausted.

The man behind the counter had just told me that what I had been eating was grain-finished, which meant it had been making me sicker for months, which meant I had been paying premium prices to be poisoned more politely. There were expletives. There were a lot of them. Ten out of ten completely unhinged.

Security was called. My mother had to drag me out, and she did, and I have never loved her more. There were expletives all the way to the car. Then more crying.

I later found out that the incident traveled. It went up the chain to Whole Foods HQ in Austin, and there was an emergency meeting about what employees could and could not say about the grass-fed claim. For a while I was famous at that Overland Park store, in a way I did not want to be famous and have no reason to be quiet about now. That Whole Foods is gone now, too.

I am not in the slightest bit embarrassed of 2009 Karen either. She was sick, she had finally figured out that the system she was paying premium prices to trust was not telling her the truth, and she said so, loudly, in the kind of place where loudly is not how you are supposed to say it.

## FIELD NOTE — "What I Did Next"

**Placement:** Chapter 1, immediately after the "What Actually Happened at the Whole Foods" Field Note and before the "What 'Grass-Fed' Meant" section heading. Pairs with the prior Field Note.

I should say what I did next, because it is part of the same thing.

The version of me who got dragged out of that Whole Foods went home and started writing standards. Actual certification standards, the kind producers have to meet to put a label on a package. Paleo Approved came first, in 2010. It was an animal standard, and a strict one. It was also a flop. Ranchers did not yet understand the importance of it, and the Paleo community itself was still embryonic and not creating the demand. I was too early.

Paleo as a popular movement did not really take off until 2013 or 2014, and by then we had abandoned ship to move toward where the demand actually was, which was in consumer packaged goods.

What followed, under the Paleo Foundation, was Certified Paleo. Although I initially felt like a sellout for abandoning farm and ranch certifications, the most consequential ecological work I have done in my life is bound by non-disclosure agreements I signed willingly and intend to honor.

The thing I am proudest of in that entire arc happened under the Certified Paleo label, not Paleo Approved.

I am telling you this now because the Field Note above ends with me losing it at a meat counter. The version of me writing this book has a clearer view of the gap between what is on a label and what is in the package, in part because I have spent sixteen years writing the standards for the labels — and because there are things I wish I had done better.

## FIELD NOTE — "The Zombie Apocalypse Litmus"

**Placement:** Chapter 4, after the closing line of the opening thought experiment ("the question of what the system is riding on and whether the ride continues — is the question this chapter exists to introduce.") and before the "## The Three Axes and Their Assumptions" section heading.

The question this chapter is built around — what does a food system require to keep functioning, and what happens when those requirements stop being met — is older than any framework I have built around it. The first time I asked it was while watching an episode of *The Walking Dead*.

The show was set in a world where the supply chains had collapsed. The episode involved a farm. Hershel — the older farmer character with the beard — had a flock of chickens, and the show had taken the trouble to mention what he was feeding them. Corn and soy. Industrial poultry feed. It dawned on me: farmer Hershel is in trouble. There was no commodity grain in his world anymore. No gasoline-powered transport. His chickens were on a clock the show had not yet noticed.

I went home and started thinking about what a sustainable food system would have to look like if you removed the assumption that the inputs would keep arriving. Most of the systems I had been calling sustainable did not survive that test. They were *fragile*.

I gave the test a name. In the previous version of this manuscript I called it the zombie apocalypse litmus test of sustainability.

I have a different name for it now — survivability — and a more careful (and serious) framework around it. But the question is the same question. I just asked it for the first time on a couch while watching a fictional farmer feed his chickens industrial feed.

Imagine being this invested in a plot hole in a TV show. Yet, here we are.

## FIELD NOTE — "Why Not Goats?"

**Placement:** Chapter 9 (Proxies and Critics), after the proxy-equivalence body paragraph ending "And people with the nerve to actually do it." and immediately before the Pleistocene Park Case sidebar. Drafted 2026-05-06. Cites Pringle et al. 2023 (*Current Biology*, DOI 10.1016/j.cub.2023.04.024).

I have been asked, more than once, why I do not just propose goats.

The question is reasonable on its face. Goats are easy to permit. The regulatory pathway exists. The market for goat meat exists, modestly. They eat thorny browse that cattle will not touch. California already deploys them, by the thousands of acres, for fuel-load reduction in chaparral. Goats are right there. Why bring in something exotic?

My honest first answer to that question, given to a person whose work I respect over an exchange that escalated faster than I had planned, was a tit-for-tat: "Why not camels?" That is not a real answer. The ludicrous question deserved an equally ludicrous non-response, and that is what it got from me at the time. The better answer, the one this Field Note exists to make, is body size, and it has a literature behind it.

The 2023 Pringle review in *Current Biology* synthesizes a decade of large-herbivore impact studies and lands on a finding that has been clean across exclosure experiments and observational comparisons: small herbivores do not functionally compensate for large ones. Body size governs ecological impact in ways that scale across multiple variables, including biomass consumption, seed dispersal, depth of trampling, structure of browse pressure, and generation of ground disturbance. Above roughly one hundred kilograms, the regulation of the population shifts from top-down predation to bottom-up food limitation. Above roughly one thousand kilograms, the animal becomes essentially invulnerable to non-human predators and exerts ecological influence that no smaller animal can replicate. The review's framing is direct: livestock are poor surrogates for wild large herbivores precisely because the body-size profile of common livestock species is concentrated in the smaller end of the range that the wild species occupied.

The numbers locate the argument. Goats run thirty to fifty kilograms. Cattle run five to seven hundred. The dromedary is in the cattle range. The Bactrian goes higher, six hundred to a thousand. *Camelops*, the animal the proposal is actually about, sat above the cow and probably above the modern Bactrian. Goats and camels are not interchangeable proxies for the same lost role. They occupy different positions on the body-size scale, and the ecological impact of the larger animal is not something the smaller animal can produce by being more numerous.

It also deserves saying that cattle themselves are proxies for the wild aurochs the European landscape lost. That nobody calls them out as substituted does not make them less so. Cattle have monopolized the regenerative-agriculture conversation, but they are not the only large herbivore, and they are not the largest available. Camels, particularly Bactrians, are larger. Their ecological footprint, on a per-animal basis, is correspondingly greater. The argument for proposing them is not that goats are bad. It is that goats cannot do what *Camelops*-sized animals did, and the landscape was calibrated to *Camelops*-sized animals for most of its evolutionary history.

The eye-rolling I have received on this is real. It does not, by itself, change what the fossil record says or what the body-size literature documents.

## CASE — "Pleistocene Park"

**Placement:** Chapter 9 (Proxies and Critics), after the engagement with Caro 2007 ending "And people with the nerve to actually do it."

The proxy hypothesis the previous section discussed has, for thirty years, been undergoing a sustained empirical test. Sergey Zimov, a Russian geophysicist, started Pleistocene Park in 1996 in Yakutia, in the eastern Siberian Arctic. The premise is the proxy proposal carried to its conclusion: reassemble, at landscape scale, the large-herbivore community that occupied the mammoth steppe before the terminal Pleistocene losses, and watch what the land does.

The experimental enclosure now covers roughly one hundred fifty square kilometers and holds Yakutian horses, plains bison transferred from Alaska, musk-oxen, reindeer, yak, and a small number of Bactrian camels. The mammoth itself is, of course, absent; the project's framing has always been that the mammoth's *function* in the system — heavy-bodied browsing, snow disruption, dung-driven nutrient cycling — could be reconstructed from a community of available large herbivores even without the original keystone species.

What has been measured. Soil temperatures under grazed plots run five to ten degrees Celsius colder than ungrazed adjacent ground, because the herbivores compact and disturb the winter snow that otherwise insulates the soil and accelerates permafrost thaw. Vegetation has shifted in grazed plots from moss-and-shrub tundra toward a grass-dominated cover that more closely resembles pollen records of the Pleistocene mammoth steppe. Carbon dynamics — the project's larger claim, that grazer-driven cooling could buffer permafrost methane release at climatically meaningful scales — remain under measurement and dispute.

What it is not. Pleistocene Park is small relative to the landscape its hypothesis is about; it is not a controlled experiment in the strict statistical sense; and it is operating in a political environment that has constrained both the science and the international collaboration the project relies on. Critics including Caro 2007 would note that a single uncontrolled site does not settle the proxy-equivalence question even after thirty years. They are right that it does not settle it. They are also wrong, in the form the critique typically takes, that nothing has been learned: thirty years of measurements at a real site running a real version of the hypothesis is more empirical attention than the proxy debate had received from any other quarter when the project began.

The point of naming Pleistocene Park here is not to argue that it has succeeded or failed. It is to point out that, when the previous section said the experiment had not been run, that statement was true for the American case and not true for the Eurasian one. Somebody has been running it for thirty years. The question is whether anyone will run an analogous experiment on the continent the book is actually about.

## CASE — "Proxies Already in Use"

**Placement:** Chapter 7 (The Wrong Proxy in the Right Place), near the chapter's widening section, after "The principle extends far beyond the Southwest, and it extends far beyond the conversation I had with Savory in that classroom. Let me widen it."

The proxy concept this chapter has been developing — large herbivores selected to match the co-evolutionary record of a landscape — is not theoretical. Versions of it are running, on the ground, in several places. The catch is that in almost every case, the proxy chosen has been the proxy that was *available*, not the proxy the landscape co-evolved with.

California: goats. Cal Fire and various municipal fire-prevention contractors deploy domesticated goat herds across thousands of acres of chaparral each year, driven there to reduce the fuel load that drives California's increasingly catastrophic fire seasons. The goats are effective at brush reduction. They are also Old World animals introduced to a continent whose chaparral co-evolved under browsing pressure from large American camelids, ground sloths, and a now-extinct mountain goat (*Oreamnos harringtoni*), none of which were goats in the Eurasian sense.

Australia: cattle. Crown-land grazing experiments in the savanna woodlands of the Northern Territory have used cattle to reduce native grass fuel loads ahead of fire seasons, with measurable effects on fire intensity. The cattle are doing useful work. They are also doing it in a landscape whose original large grazers were marsupial — *Diprotodon* and the now-extinct megafauna of the Pleistocene Australian interior — and the proxy match between cattle and what was there is even looser than the cattle-for-bison match in North America.

The pattern repeats. The proxy in use is the proxy at hand: cattle and goats are domesticated, available, regulatorily uncomplicated, and economically embedded in the food system. The proxy the fossil record points at — for the American Southwest, large camelids; for southern Africa, the still-living elephant; for the central Asian steppe, the species Pleistocene Park has been working with — is, in most cases, harder to procure, harder to permit, and easier to skip.

Nobody has, to my knowledge, tried camels at any scale in the American Southwest specifically as a fire-and-desertification management proxy. The biology is in their favor: dromedaries do thrive on the thorny browse the chaparral and basin-and-range produce; the historical record places *Camelops* in those landscapes for most of their geological history; the regulatory framework Chapter Thirteen describes does not yet exist for proxies of any kind, but if it did, this would be a candidate. The experiment has, for the southwestern American case, never been run.

## DEFINITION — "Bactrian and Dromedary"

**Placement:** Chapter 11, Arid Southwest section, after the camel-milk-and-horse-meat paragraph and before the "A Million Camels Looking for a Continent" Case sidebar. Drafted 2026-05-06.

The proxy literature names "camels" as the candidate replacement for *Camelops*, and the book has followed that convention. The proxy decision is, in fact, two decisions. There are two living Old World camel species, they occupy distinct ecological niches, they tolerate different climates, and the difference matters for which American landscape gets which animal.

The dromedary, *Camelus dromedarius*, is the single-humped camel of the Sahara, the Arabian Peninsula, and the Horn of Africa. It is heat-adapted: a sparse coat, body mass typically four to seven hundred kilograms, and a metabolism optimized for high-temperature, low-humidity environments. Dromedaries thrive on hot-desert browse, including acacia, mesquite, palo verde, and the thorny succulents, and tolerate water deprivation that would kill cattle or sheep within days. They are the species the Saudi-Gulf trade is built around, and the species the US Camel Corps experimented with in the 1850s.

The Bactrian, *Camelus bactrianus*, is the two-humped camel of the Central Asian steppe, the Gobi, and the Mongolian and northern Chinese deserts. It is the wide-temperature-tolerance specialist of the camelid lineage. A dense winter coat, body mass commonly six hundred to a thousand kilograms, and a tolerance range that runs from forty degrees below zero in steppe winters to over a hundred degrees Fahrenheit in summer rangeland use. Bactrians eat plants other camels treat as marginal: saxaul, salt-stressed *Atriplex*, and the halophytic shrubs of the Amaranthaceae and Chenopodiaceae families that store sodium at concentrations toxic to most herbivores. They drink saline water that other livestock will not, with documented tolerance above two percent salinity. The Bactrian's foot pad, broader and heavier than the dromedary's, also performs a service the dromedary does less of: it fractures the biological soil crusts and physical caps that form on overgrazed and compacted desert ground, opening the surface to water infiltration and seedling establishment.

The halophyte-eating ability is the unsung edge. Halophytes — *Atriplex*, *Salicornia*, *Halocnemum*, *Salsola* — accumulate salt in their tissues at concentrations that exclude most herbivores from using them. The plants are dominant on salt-affected soils across the western United States: irrigated farmland where a century of input water has accumulated sodium and chloride beyond what conventional crops can tolerate, and natural saline playas where the geology has produced the same outcome. The Bactrian, alone among large livestock species, can convert halophyte biomass into meat, milk, and other commodities while removing salt from the system through the animal's tissues and excreted in urine. This is phytoremediation with a productive output, and the working land it could be applied to is not abstract: several million acres of the western US carry soil salinity sufficient to limit conventional agriculture, and most of that acreage is not currently producing food.

The utility extends well beyond restoration. Camel meat is lean, high in protein, and an established staple across much of the Old World. Camel milk is higher in protein and several minerals than cow's milk, lower in lactose, and increasingly used in the United States by the autism community for behavioral and gastrointestinal symptoms — the demand is anecdotal in clinical literature but real and rising in the marketplace. Lactoferrin, a milk protein with antimicrobial, antiviral, and immunomodulatory activity, occurs in camel milk at high concentration; recent work has identified that camel tears carry it as well, and a small extraction industry has begun to form around it. Bactrian wool, comparable in fineness to cashmere, supports the substantial commercial fiber industry of Mongolia and northern China. The animal is not a single-product animal. It is a multi-product animal in a regulatory environment that does not yet recognize multi-product animals as a category.

The bioregional decision follows. The hot southern deserts (Sonoran, Chihuahuan, lower Mojave) are dromedary country: the climate matches the species. The cold-winter Great Basin, the Colorado Plateau, the Wyoming Red Desert, and the saline-affected playas across the basin-and-range are Bactrian country: the cold tolerance, the halophyte browse, the soil-crust breaking, and the salt-water tolerance all line up. *Camelops*, the extinct American camel the proposal is actually about, was probably closer to the Bactrian in body mass and in temperature tolerance. The right answer to "which camel" is therefore both, allocated by landscape.

## CASE — "A Million Camels Looking for a Continent"

**Placement:** Chapter 11, Arid Southwest section, after the camel-milk-and-horse-meat paragraph ending "than the cattle-based system currently imposed on that landscape." Drafted 2026-05-06.

The supply question for the Southwest's camelid scenario has a counterintuitive answer: there are too many camels in the world already, and one continent is killing them rather than using them.

Australia carries the world's largest population of feral dromedaries — descended from camels imported in the nineteenth century to support exploration and freight across the interior, released into the bush after the rail and motor lines came in, and reproducing across a continent whose vegetation suits them well. By the late 2000s, federal and state surveys placed the population at approaching one million animals, expanding at roughly ten percent per year, and producing the kind of damage to fences, water infrastructure, and native vegetation that puts a species into the legal category of pest.

The federal response was the Australian Feral Camel Management Project, a four-year program (2009–2013) funded at roughly nineteen million Australian dollars, which culled more than one hundred forty thousand camels, primarily through aerial gunning from helicopters across the central deserts. The program reduced the population to an estimated three hundred thousand by program end. By 2020, with reproduction outpacing residual culling effort, the population had recovered enough that the Anangu Pitjantjatjara Yankunytjatjara Lands authorized an emergency cull during drought, killing approximately ten thousand additional camels over five days when the animals began damaging community water infrastructure searching for moisture. The current population is again estimated in the high hundreds of thousands and rising.

The Gulf states are running the inverse experiment. Saudi Arabia is the world's largest importer of camel meat and live camels, anchored in a regional camel-meat economy that runs through the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait, and Bahrain at multi-billion-dollar scale. The kingdom's interest is not nostalgic. Camel meat is a staple protein, camel milk a premium commodity, and camel racing a substantial industry. The trade has scaled to the point that the regional shipping infrastructure now includes dedicated multi-deck livestock-carrier vessels — purpose-built ships, not chartered aircraft — moving live animals across the Indian Ocean at volumes that air freight cannot economically support.

The contrast is the same contrast the feral-hog discussion will draw at smaller scale and inside one country. Australia treats the surplus animal as a damage vector and pays its taxpayers to kill it. Saudi Arabia treats the same animal as a food source and invests in infrastructure to import it. The United States, which has the landscape that wants the animal and a federal regulatory architecture that does not yet have a category for it, treats the question as not yet asked. Three continents, three outcomes, one species.

The animal-welfare reading writes itself. If the choice for a particular cohort of feral Australian camels is between aerial gunning and live transport across an ocean, the second outcome is the better outcome for the animal. If the choice is between aerial gunning and a transport pathway that ends with the camels released onto American Pleistocene-functional rangeland under a research protocol of the kind Chapter Thirteen describes, the gap between actual and possible widens further.

The supply problem the Southwest scenario faces is not, in the end, a supply problem. It is a regulatory and logistical problem: which animals are permitted to cross which oceans on which paperwork. The animals exist. The market for them exists. The transport infrastructure exists. What does not yet exist is the American regulatory category that would permit the third option.

## CASE — "Broken Arrow Ranch"

**Placement:** Chapter 11 (Bioregional and Urban), in the section that addresses bioregional food systems and feral-hog harvest as a regional model.

One operation worth naming in this chapter is Broken Arrow Ranch, a Texas game-meat processor that handles wild-harvested feral hog, axis deer, blackbuck, and other free-ranging species across the Texas Hill Country and surrounding regions. The model is straightforward: trained mobile-harvest crews work directly on the ranches where the animals are, field-dress the animals there, and bring them back to the Broken Arrow facility for processing.

The animals were never in a feedlot. They were never in a confined-feeding pipeline. They never required transportation to a centralized slaughterhouse, never received veterinary inputs, never ate purchased grain. They lived on the land and were harvested from it, and the operation that processes them is one of the few that has built a regulatory pathway through the existing USDA architecture for wild-harvested protein at scale.

Paleo Approved certified Broken Arrow under its animal standards, and the operation more than met the standards as written. The standards, in some sense, were written with an operation like this in mind — an operation working with, rather than against, the animals already on the land.

The contrast is worth holding alongside the survivability scoring of Chapter Five. The federal government, through the USDA and various state agencies, currently funds aerial-gunning programs in which contracted shooters in helicopters cull feral hogs across Texas rangeland, treating the animals as an invasive nuisance to be reduced as cheaply as possible. The same animals, harvested rather than gunned and routed through an operation like Broken Arrow's, become protein for human food, revenue for the landowner, and processing work for the local economy. Both are management responses to the same population. One costs the taxpayer money and produces nothing. The other costs the taxpayer nothing and produces protein. The difference is structural rather than ecological — a function of which regulatory pathway exists and which does not.

Broken Arrow does not solve the broader feral-hog problem, and it is not at the scale to feed a region. What it demonstrates is that a regulatory pathway for wild-harvested protein at any scale can exist. If it can exist for feral hog and axis deer in Texas, it can in principle exist for whatever the next animal is.

## DEFINITION — "*Equus* and the deep equid record"

**Placement:** Chapter 2, after "the same wave of extinctions that took the camels and the mammoths." Drafted 2026-04-19, inline in ch02_greene_preview.html.

The genus *Equus* — the lineage that includes every living horse, donkey, and zebra — is the crown of a much longer equid story. Before *Equus* the continent already had a succession of horse-family genera: *Dinohippus*, its likely direct ancestor, already essentially modern in body plan and single-toed; before that, the three-toed *Pliohippus*; before that, the grazing and browsing radiation of *Merychippus* and its contemporaries; and before those, the small, multi-toed browsers of the early Miocene.

The equid family has been running on this continent for close to twenty million years. *Equus* is the last four million of that twenty. When paleontologists say horses evolved in North America, they are not saying one species showed up here, stayed a while, and left. They are saying the entire deep history of the family played out on this continent, and the Spanish ships of the sixteenth century were returning the crown of a tree whose roots had been American the whole time.

## CASE — "The American horses by name"

**Placement:** Chapter 2, after the *Equus* definition sidebar. Drafted 2026-04-19, inline in ch02_greene_preview.html.

The late-Pleistocene equid fauna of North America went by several specific names, and the specifics matter if only to show that this was not one horse but many. *Equus scotti* was large, stout-bodied, and widely distributed across the southern and central portions of the continent; its body mass places it comfortably within the range of modern domestic horses. *Equus conversidens* was smaller and more gracile, centered on what is now Mexico and the American Southwest. *Equus lambei*, the Yukon horse, persisted in the Alaskan and Yukon refugia; the Thistle Creek genome that confirmed the deep relationship between late-Pleistocene American horses and modern *Equus caballus* came from this population.

Other named forms — *Equus francisci*, *Equus complicatus*, *Equus occidentalis* — fill out the record with varying degrees of preservation. The point of the list is not taxonomic tidiness. American horses were a diversified fauna occupying a range of habitats and body sizes, and the wave of terminal-Pleistocene extinctions took the whole set.

## DEFINITION — "Camelops and Its Kin"

**Placement:** Chapter 2, immediately after the "American horses by name" Case sidebar. Drafted 2026-05-06. Source: tarpits.org/stories/las-ancient-camel.

Camelidae is the family. Its living members include the dromedary and the Bactrian of Asia and Africa, *Camelus dromedarius* and *Camelus bactrianus*, and four South American species: the llama, the alpaca, the vicuña, and the guanaco. Llamas are camels.

The American Pleistocene fauna held two named camelid genera that are no longer with us. *Camelops hesternus*, the dominant North American camel of the late Pleistocene, ranged from the Yukon to central Mexico and is the species the rewilding proposal is most directly about. *Hemiauchenia macrocephala*, the large-headed llama, occupied the same continental range and is preserved at La Brea, alongside *Camelops*, in the asphalt fossil record of southern California. Both were extinct by the terminal Pleistocene pulse around twelve thousand years ago.

What is known about *Camelops* from the La Brea record and from skeletal material elsewhere on the continent: roughly seven feet at the shoulder, a foot taller than a modern dromedary, longer-necked, with leg proportions consistent with a sustained running speed in the neighborhood of forty miles per hour. Tooth wear and microwear analysis identifies it as a browser, feeding on shoots, leaves, and the woody growth its long neck would have placed within reach.

Whether *Camelops* carried the fatty hump or humps of its living Old World relatives is unresolved. Soft tissue does not preserve in the fossil record, and the vertebral column shows no anchoring features that would force the answer either way.

What is known, however, with certainty, is that *Camelops hesternus* is as American as apple pie.

## CASE — "Three Labels, Three Definitions, One Gap"

**Placement:** Chapter 1, adjacent to the paragraph ending "The gap is the same gap I encountered at the meat counter, wearing different clothes." Drafted 2026-04-19.

The three labels this chapter has walked through carry three different relationships to their definitions. "Grass-fed," under the 2007 USDA marketing-claim standard, had a precise definition that was voluntary and effectively unenforced; the standard was withdrawn entirely in 2016, after which any producer could use the term under any definition. "Free-range," under FSIS poultry labeling, requires only that birds have "access to the outside" — in practice, a door. "Organic," under the 1990 Organic Foods Production Act and its 2002 implementing regulations, carries the most rigorous definition of the three, verifiably prohibiting specific synthetic inputs through a third-party certification process. Three different labels, three different definitional rigors, three different gaps between what the consumer assumes and what the label certifies. The gap is not uniform across labels. It is structural across the labeling system.

## DEFINITION — "Time-transgressive Extinction"

**Placement:** Chapter 3, adjacent to the paragraph ending "Time-transgressiveness is the signature the overkill hypothesis predicts and the climate hypothesis does not." Drafted 2026-04-19.

A time-transgressive extinction is one that occurs at different times in different places — the same pattern recurring across landmasses, offset from each other by thousands or tens of thousands of years. The near-time megafaunal losses are the paradigmatic example. Australia's pulse dates to roughly 46,000 years before present. Continental North America's centers on 12,000 to 13,000 years. New Zealand's moa extinctions occur less than 1,000 years ago. Madagascar's sit within the last 2,000. The pattern that unifies these events is not a single global climate shift, which would affect all landmasses simultaneously. It is the arrival of human populations, which occurred at different times on different continents. Time-transgressiveness is the diagnostic signature by which paleoecology distinguishes human-driven from climate-driven extinction events, and it is the single observation the climate hypothesis has the hardest time accommodating.

## DEFINITION — "Fragile, Robust, Antifragile"

**Placement:** Chapter 4, adjacent to the paragraph ending "...performs superbly under the conditions it was designed for and breaks catastrophically under conditions it was not." Drafted 2026-04-19.

Nassim Taleb's 2012 taxonomy names three distinct responses a system can have to volatility. A *fragile* system performs well under stable conditions and breaks under stress: concentrated supply chains, high-debt financial instruments, monocultures. A *robust* system withstands stress without breaking but does not improve from it: a well-engineered bridge, a conservative portfolio. An *antifragile* system gains from disorder, strengthened rather than merely preserved by exposure to shock: evolutionary populations under selection pressure, decentralized networks that route around failures, certain biological immune systems. Most industrial food systems are fragile in Taleb's sense, optimized for efficiency under stable conditions and brittle under the conditions we can now see coming. The survivability axis measures this property directly, and it is the dimension most twentieth-century food-system assessment simply omitted.

## CASE — "The Knepp Experiment"

**Placement:** Chapter 8, adjacent to the paragraph ending "...the animals doing the ecological work are also the animals producing the food." Drafted 2026-04-19.

Knepp Estate in West Sussex, England — 3,500 acres of former Weald-clay farmland released from conventional management beginning in 2001 — is the most thoroughly documented ecological rewilding experiment in Europe. Charlie Burrell and Isabella Tree stocked the land with proxy herbivores chosen for ecological function rather than production characteristics: Old English longhorn cattle, Exmoor ponies, Tamworth pigs, fallow deer. No paddocks, no rotations, no purchased feed. Within two decades, turtle doves, nightingales, peregrine falcons, purple emperor butterflies, and all five British owl species were breeding on the estate. The meat operation (beef, venison, pork), combined with ecotourism and conservation stewardship payments, made the estate financially viable where the preceding conventional operation had failed. Tree's 2018 *Wilding* is the narrative record, and the ecological monitoring literature around Knepp continues to grow.

## DEFINITION — "Trophic Cascade"

**Placement:** Chapter 10, adjacent to the paragraph ending "...and the entire community reorganizes around the loss." Drafted 2026-04-19.

A trophic cascade is the amplification of ecological change through predator-prey relationships. When a top predator is removed, herbivore populations expand without control, consuming the foundation species (kelp, grass, coral) those herbivores depend on. The herbivores eventually starve, but not before the foundation species has been destroyed. The system reorganizes around the loss: kelp forests become urchin barrens, grasslands become shrublands, coral reefs become algal deserts. The cascade demonstrates that ecosystems are held in shape by relationships rather than by species individually. The predator does not merely eat the herbivore. It holds the entire structure in place by eating the herbivore. Remove the predator and the structure inverts. The pattern runs both directions, and the restoration evidence (sea otters, wolves, large sharks) shows the inversion can often be reversed if the predator returns in time.

## CASE — "The Clam Gardens of the Pacific Northwest"

**Placement:** Chapter 10, adjacent to the sentence introducing the clam gardens. Drafted 2026-04-19.

Between what is now British Columbia and Southeast Alaska, First Nations peoples engineered the intertidal zone for millennia through the construction of rock-wall terraces that modified substrate angle, sediment dynamics, and tidal exposure. These clam gardens increased clam biomass by factors of two to four compared to unmodified beaches, not through extraction but through deliberate ecological manipulation that enhanced the beach's productive capacity. Research by Dana Lepofsky, Nicole Smith, and colleagues has documented systematic construction, transmission across generations, and rapid recovery of productivity under restoration. Clam gardens represent the marine equivalent of California's Indigenous fire management: human-engineered ecosystems that produced food while maintaining, and often increasing, the ecological health they depended on. The archaeological record of the practice extends back thousands of years.

## DEFINITION — "Externalized Costs"

**Placement:** Chapter 12, adjacent to the paragraph ending "...borne by someone other than the buyer." Drafted 2026-04-19.

Externalized costs are expenses borne by parties who are not party to the transaction that produced them. When an industrial hog operation exports waste to groundwater, the cost of remediation falls to municipalities and affected residents rather than to the operator. When agricultural antibiotic use produces resistant bacteria, the cost appears as healthcare spending rather than as feed expense. When federal commodity subsidies hold corn below production cost, the cost is transferred from producer to taxpayer. When monoculture depletes topsoil, the cost is transferred to future farmers. The price at checkout reflects only the portion of total cost the producer has internalized. The 2019 Rockefeller Foundation analysis the chapter cites estimated the externalized cost of the U.S. food system at roughly $2.1 trillion annually, approximately three times the direct consumer expenditure on food.

## CASE — "The Feral Hog Paradox"

**Placement:** Chapter 12, adjacent to the paragraph ending "It also addresses an ecological damage vector." Drafted 2026-04-19.

Feral hogs descended from nineteenth-century escaped domestic stock and twentieth-century imported Eurasian wild boar now number roughly 2.6 million in Texas alone, with rapidly expanding populations across the Southeast and lower Midwest. USDA estimates the national herd at six to nine million animals and growing. They cause roughly $1.5 billion in annual agricultural damage in the United States, destroy ground-nesting bird habitat, and accelerate soil erosion on the landscapes they colonize. They are, by any standard ecological accounting, an invasive species problem. They are also a high-scoring protein source: wild-harvested feral hog requires zero feed, zero housing, zero veterinary care, and no supply chain beyond harvest. The harvest addresses the ecological damage while producing food. The contradiction is not a contradiction. It is what it looks like when a damage vector is also a food system.

## DEFINITION — "Subsidy Incidence"

**Placement:** Chapter 13, adjacent to the paragraph ending "...the feedlot system that depends on cheap corn is effectively subsidized as well." Drafted 2026-04-19.

Subsidy incidence is the economic principle that the party receiving a subsidy check is not necessarily the party capturing its economic benefit. A federal payment supporting corn production below cost is technically paid to the corn farmer, but the real beneficiary is whoever purchases the subsidized corn downstream. Between 1997 and 2005, the industrial livestock sector captured an estimated $35 billion in implicit savings through below-cost feed enabled by commodity subsidies — the Tufts analysis the chapter cites. The incidence of the subsidy determined what kind of livestock production became economically viable at scale. Restructuring agricultural support toward ecosystem-service payments rather than commodity price supports would shift that incidence, and with it the kinds of land use the subsidy system incentivizes. Where the money ends up determines what gets built on the land.

## FIELD NOTE — "The Minimum Viable Flip"

**Placement:** Chapter 13, after the "What Would Have to Be True" section, before the "## Closing" heading. (Drafted 2026-05-06.)

The structural changes this chapter has named are individually difficult and collectively formidable. None of them is a precondition for running the smallest version of the experiment. The MVP, to borrow a frame from outside ecology, is what Eric Reis, writing about startups rather than ecosystems, called a minimum viable product: not the cheapest version of the project, but the smallest version that can be falsified.

A hundred camels in an osage orange orchard is the long-tail outcome, not the MVP. The MVP is something more like: forty acres of distressed basin-and-range ground, six to ten camels (or feral burros, or whatever combination of available proxies for what the fossil record says belongs there can actually be permitted), a small planting of a co-evolved tree species, and a monitoring protocol that captures soil carbon, water infiltration, plant diversity, and animal body condition at fixed intervals over three to five years. Bounded. Instrumented. Designed to fail informatively if it fails. The point is not to prove the orchard hypothesis — the MVP cannot do that. The point is to generate the first real signal where signal currently does not exist, at a cost low enough that the permitting pathway and the funding architecture can be tested against a concrete proposal rather than a theoretical one.

The case for running the MVP rests on a separate argument this book has been making in different vocabulary throughout. The current food system is fragile in the sense Chapter Four named: optimized for stable conditions and brittle when those conditions change. The dominant agricultural modality is engineered toward an equilibrium of stable rainfall, stable energy prices, stable trade lanes, stable phosphate, stable nitrogen, stable insect ecology, stable microbial ecology. The engineering succeeds only as long as the equilibrium holds. The Dust Bowl was a fragility cascade. The Irish Potato Famine was a fragility cascade. Panama disease in bananas, late blight, foot-and-mouth, BSE, the broiler flock culls of the last decade — all fragility cascades. Each one was solved by reaching for more of the input the system already depended on, which deepened the specialization, which set up the next cascade.

A keystone-driven, ecologically diverse system would not be immune to disturbance. It would be the opposite of fragile to it. Fire, drought, herd movement, mass mortality — these are not anomalies in a Pleistocene-functional landscape. They are the conditions the landscape is calibrated to. Take the disturbance away and the system degrades. Put it back and the system reorganizes.

Intellectual honesty asks the policy framework to plan for both worlds. The one in which the dominant agricultural modality keeps refining itself, throws ever-better solutions at its dependencies, and reaches the better version of what it already is. That world is worth planning for. So is the other one. If the input chains tighten, if the climate signal shifts past what the cultivars can tolerate, if a microbial event takes out a single-genome crop the way late blight took out the lumper potato, the contingency is not "find a cleverer monoculture." The contingency is "have the alternative system already running, somewhere, at any scale, with enough data that it can be expanded."

That is the case for funding the MVP even if the dominant system is fine. Diversity of crops is what survivability looks like at the field level. Diversity of approaches is what survivability looks like at the system level. We did not learn nothing from the Dust Bowl. We learned, at minimum, that running every farm by the same playbook is what makes a Dust Bowl possible. The same logic applies one level up. The MVP is a way of buying optionality before the option needs to be exercised.

## FIELD NOTE — "The Standard Already Exists"

**Placement:** Chapter 13, in the section that addresses the regulatory architecture for proxy species and certification pathways, after the federal-category gap is named and before the closing. Drafted 2026-05-06.

I should say what is true about my own relationship to the regulatory architecture this chapter has been describing.

I wrote a camel standard. By 2013, or 2014 at the latest, under the Paleo Approved program, more than a decade before the book in your hands began to argue that the American Southwest could use the animal. The standard ran the categories a third-party certification standard runs: grass-fed and grass-finished, humanely treated, free range, antibiotic-free, hormone-free. It carried a banned-feedstuffs list — corn, soy, cereal grains except oats, milk replacers containing antibiotics or growth hormones, non-protein nitrogen, antibiotics, hormones. It carried a traceability requirement of one hundred percent from birth to harvest, with forty-eight-hour trace-back. It carried a welfare-indicator inventory the auditor uses to flag environmental issues: calluses, swelling, injuries, bruising, infections, laminitis, hematomas, abscesses, broken bones and tails, scars. The definitions section ran more than thirty terms, from Baleage to Vitamin, because a standard that cannot be audited is not a standard.

I used it. We certified Desert Farms, the US camel-milk operation, by visiting their producer outside Springfield, Missouri, walking the operation, and applying the standard against what we saw. We saw Walid Abdul-Wahab, the founder, again at the Ancestral Health Symposium in 2014. I could not have certified without writing the standard. The standard existed because I had written it.

I am telling you this in Chapter Thirteen because the chapter has been describing a regulatory architecture that does not yet have a federal category for keystone-proxy species. That description is correct as far as it goes. What it is not is complete. The federal pathway does not exist. The third-party certification pathway, for the specific animal this book is most directly about, does. It existed by 2014. The Paleo Approved program no longer runs, and the standard is no longer certifying anyone. But the document was written, the categories were defined, and the framework for what an audited American camel operation would have to demonstrate was laid out, by an author who is now asking the country to consider operations like the ones the standard was designed to certify.

If a federal pathway is built, it will not be built from scratch. There is already a draft. There is already an example of using it to certify a real producer in a real state. The harder work, what a federal architecture would have to add to a third-party standard to make it generally applicable, is the work the rest of this chapter has been naming. The pathway has been waiting on someone with federal-rulemaking authority to take the categories seriously. The categories have been there.

## FIELD NOTE — "The Popcorn"

**Placement:** Epilogue, adjacent to the paragraph about wanting to throw popcorn at a black hole. Drafted 2026-04-19; rewritten 2026-05-06 to land on the camels-vs-popcorn utility comparison.

The popcorn thing is real. I was twelve, maybe thirteen, and I had read Stephen Hawking the way you read Stephen Hawking when you are that age, grasping the shape of the thing without grasping the math.

It has stirred me deeply, ever since. And like camels, it has taken up residence in my mind. The point of the popcorn, I understood only much later, was not the popcorn. It was that some wants are worth carrying even when they exceed what the carrier can do about them. Throwing popcorn at the event horizon, what does that achieve, meaningfully? Seeing whether a hundred camels can reverse desertification, seeing whether we can learn something useful from running an experiment like that — that is a wish worth having.

I am not embarrassed to end a book of analytical argument talking about popcorn and refrigerators and event horizons and camels in an osage orange orchard. Some wishes are the reason for the analysis. At the very least, I have full faith that at least one of these wishes could plausibly be granted within my lifetime.

And if I had to choose one — and that would be a difficult choice — I would choose camels. There is no utility in finding out what happens to popcorn, or refrigerators, tossed into an event horizon. There is utility in finding out what camels can do for land-flipping and reversing desertification, while producing meat, milk that the autism community has been turning to in growing numbers, camel baby formula powder, hump fat, leather, and "camel cashmere," to name a few of the commodities I am aware of. And this does not even begin to include the money from potential safaris and tourism drawn by the attraction of large megafauna in a space where they seem foreign and exotic.
